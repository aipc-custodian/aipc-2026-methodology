# Drafting and Cross-Model Revision: Methodology Report, Stage Three


*Drafting, and review from outside the system*

**Purpose.** Stage three takes the outline that left stage two and produces the submitted paper. It has two parts: a drafting pass, and a revision loop in which other models review the draft and the drafter answers each review. Unlike the earlier stages, it was not built as software. It was run as a single conversation with one model instance working in a shared document, so what follows is a record of one run rather than the specification of a component. The run produced *Common Ground as Accidental Convergence*: 5,958 words with the abstract included and the bibliography excluded, ten sections, and twenty-eight references.

**Architecture: drafting.** One instance of Claude Opus 5.5 drafted the paper from the stage-two outline, writing directly into a shared document one section at a time. Two instructions came with the outline: write in the author's voice, using his published work as the model, and vary what paragraphs do, making some load-bearing and others transitional. The drafter retrieved the author's most recent article from his website and worked from an analysis of its features: first-person signposting, terms introduced with "call this," homely cases, a "to be sure" paragraph conceding limits, and a closing that nudges the reader rather than declaring victory. It made one retrieval while drafting, to confirm Yalcin's definition of common ground; every other claim about the literature came from recall, as in stage one. The first draft ran to about 5,800 words.

**Architecture: review and revision.** The author gave the complete draft to a different model, Claude Fable, with a short generic prompt asking whether the paper was good, novel and publishable, and where. Fable returned a referee-style assessment. The author passed each assessment to the drafter verbatim, with a generic instruction to address the concerns it judged legitimate. The drafter sorted the concerns into those it accepted and those it resisted, verified every bibliographic claim at issue by web search, and wrote the changes into a local copy. It then counted the result against the competition's limit, which it had first confirmed from the published rules (6,000 words, abstract counted, bibliography not), and only then wrote the changes into the shared document. Two rounds were run with Fable. Before the second, the author asked the drafter whether the review warranted changes at all; the drafter accepted most points, declined one, and proposed a plan, which the author approved without modification. The drafter's own report on the stage then listed the shared model family of drafter and reviewer as a limitation. On the drafter's advice, the author added a third review from a different family: ChatGPT (OpenAI), run at medium reasoning effort. The drafter had proposed a blind prompt asking for ranked objections and for which claims about the literature had been checked by search. The author used his original prompt instead, which kept the three reviews comparable. The third round then followed the second: assessment, plan, approval, revision.

**Method of development.** As in earlier stages, every substantive change followed from reading output, and several findings could not have been anticipated.

> *A reviewer from outside the conversation sees what the drafter built around.* The first draft's central case was a dean's confidential decision, known to a department chair and a colleague, which the chair refused to treat as settled: "Not officially." The first reviewer pointed out that "officially" appeals to an institution's status, not to the record between the two speakers, so the case did not isolate what it was built to isolate. The drafter had constructed the case and argued from it for two pages without registering the confound. The case was replaced by a minimal pair in which nothing institutional is at stake and the only variable is whether a fact has been said; the minimal-pair design was the reviewer's suggestion.
> 
> *The reviewer is a second recall, not a check.* The same reviewer misdated Stalnaker's goat example to 2002 (it is from 1978). It reported that it could not confirm a 2024 article that a bibliographic search confirms at once. And it counted the bibliography toward the length limit. Each error was caught only because the drafter verified before acting. Cross-model review widens the range of objections, but it does not change the status of claims about the literature, which remain hypotheses until retrieved. This is the stage-one limitation again, now on the reviewing side.
> 
> *Fixes relocate problems.* In the first draft, the argument against Camp's view rested on a commitment made in March and forgotten by May. The first review objected that, on a dispositional reading of representation, a forgotten agreement is still recallable. The revision made the forgetting total. The second review then observed that a commitment made in March is not on May's conversational record at all, which is what the argument against Camp required. The first repair had made the case more plainly cross-conversational, not less. Each repair answered the objection in front of it and opened the place where the next objection landed. This is the stage-one pattern of escapes migrating to new locations, appearing now in the argument rather than the architecture.
> 
> *The first reviewer's questions did more than its objections.* Each of its reviews closed with a question rather than a recommendation. The first asked whether the real distinction was between the settled and the merely in view. The second asked what, within a single conversation, ever separates commitment from representation. The drafter's answers produced the two largest structural changes in the paper. The first was its treatment of scoreboard models of context. The second was a deadline announced aloud at a meeting while one participant was not listening, which gives the paper a dissociation in both directions within one conversation. The reviewer's concrete proposals fared worse. Its recommended reframing, from "common ground is two things" to "Camp should un-nest," was declined, on the ground that the new case let the larger thesis earn its keep.
> 
> *Verification is a source of content.* Checking a citation to Geurts's encyclopedia entry turned up his description of mentalist and normative accounts as "complementary rather than contrary." That sharpened the worry that the paper's disagreement with him is verbal, and it became a paragraph answering the worry. Checking the source of Stalnaker's goat example showed that it is exploited with a demonstrative. That turned the reviewer's objection from the presupposition literature into evidence for the paper's second mechanism. Both changes came from reading a source to confirm a reference.
> 
> *Confirming a reference is not reading it.* The cross-family reviewer searched the literature where the Claude reviewer had worked from recall. It found two 2025 papers in the journal that published the paper's main target. Simons argues that the information available for interpretation is not the common ground. Bary argues that the common ground cannot "do double duty" and splits it into common beliefs and commitments, illustrating the split with the same goat example the paper uses. Neither Claude model had raised either paper. In fact, the drafter had retrieved Bary's article the day before to confirm the wording of the goat quotation, and used it for nothing else. The previous finding therefore needs a qualification: verification produces content only when the source is read for its bearing on the thesis, not merely for the sentence being checked. The discovery narrowed what the paper claims as new. Its double dissociation now sets commitment against mutual representation rather than against belief, a distinction Bary's examples do not draw, and the paper disputes Bary's assignment of presupposition to common belief. This reviewer's proposals also fared better than the first reviewer's. Its framing of common ground as a projection of correlated variables, and its proposed measure for the experiment the paper describes, were both adopted. Its proposal to retreat from "mechanisms" to "structures" was declined; the drafter supplied an interventionist criterion for the term instead.
> 
> *Critique is additive.* Every concern answered added words, and none removed any. The first round's changes took the paper from about 5,800 words to 6,520, the second round's from 5,961 to 6,294, and the third round's from 5,949 to roughly 6,180. All three were brought under the limit by cutting restatement: summaries of earlier sections, signposting, and second examples where one sufficed. Without the limit the paper would have grown with every review, and the cuts were the only point at which anything was taken out.
> 
> *Voice imitation is shallow.* For the author's teaching, the drafter was asked to set one of its paragraphs beside one of his and to rewrite its own to sound more like him. Closing the gap meant removing short sentences, epigrammatic turns ("publicity under another name") and direct address to the reader. Those features were the most visible tells of the drafter's default register, and removing them cost some vividness. The submitted text did not go through that rewrite and keeps the drafter's register. Style is not graded; the point is recorded because the voice instruction was part of the input.

**Human involvement.** The stage-two report found that the claim of a purely selective human role did not survive the review conversation, which recorded four contributions of content by the author. Stage three inherits those contributions through the outline and adds none. The author supplied the outline, named the source for the voice, and asked for variation in paragraph function. He chose the reviewers and gave each the same short prompt, asking whether the paper was good, novel and publishable. He relayed all three reviews to the drafter verbatim, with generic instructions and no comment on their content. He added the third review after the drafter recommended a reviewer from another model family, but kept his original prompt rather than the one the drafter proposed. Before the second and third revisions he asked whether the review warranted changes, and each time he approved the drafter's plan without modification. He also ruled out running the experiment the paper proposes, which two reviews recommended; that was a decision about resources, not about argument. He wrote no passage of the paper and supplied no argument, case or objection.

Origination by model was as follows. Every case in the final text originated with the drafter: the report card, the missed deadline, the faculty-meeting insinuation and the opening examples. The one exception is the minimal-pair design of the report card, which was the first reviewer's. Every reply to an objection, every cut, the declined reframings, the interventionist criterion for "mechanism," and the paper's specific disagreements with Bary are the drafter's. The objections are the reviewers', apart from those the drafter raised against itself, and so are the pointers to literature the first draft ignored. The first reviewer pointed to Sperber and Wilson, the scoreboard tradition, Abbott, Tonhauser and colleagues, and Stalnaker's goat. The cross-family reviewer pointed to Simons and Bary, and it contributed the framing of common ground as a projection of correlated variables and the dependent measure the conclusion proposes. The substantive decision the author made was to leave the judgment of which criticisms were legitimate to the drafter. That delegation is the main methodological choice of the stage, and its cost is recorded below.

**Known limitations.** The pipeline's governing principle, that variance is the product and should be generated in isolation before anything is selected, was not applied at this stage. One drafter wrote one draft and revised it serially, so the planned gate of selection among finished drafts had a single candidate. The drafter also judged the criticism of its own work. No independent adjudicator ruled on which concerns were legitimate, including the decisions to decline two reviewers' central reframings. The author's approval was the only check on that judgment, and the drafter's own assessment was the main evidence he had for it. Two of the three reviews came from the drafter's own model family. The third, from another family and working with search, found two directly relevant papers that neither Claude model had raised. That is evidence that the shared-family concern was real rather than hypothetical. A human referee would be needed to test whether the remaining agreement among all three reflects the paper's quality. Several soft spots were identified and left open. Simons's paper was read only in a 2022 draft, because the published version could not be retrieved, so the paper's claims about what Simons and Bary do not offer rest on partial reading. The reading of Camp as nesting the record within the common ground rests on her abstract and on secondary summaries, not on the chapter itself. The claim that what is manifest can satisfy a demonstrative's presupposition goes beyond the evidence of Tonhauser and colleagues, whose diagnostics use linguistic contexts only. And the crossed design the conclusion proposes is untested and will not be tested before submission. Finally, stages one and two are scripts, and stage three is not. It was run by hand in a conversation, with the reviews carried between models by the author. It is reproducible as a procedure, not as code, and the judgment calls recorded here are available only because the conversation was kept. Word counts were computed on plain text and may differ slightly under another counter.
