Thesis (fixed)
The asymmetry between self- and other-ascription is real but inverted: others have superior interpretive access to our standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns) because they observe behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning, while we have privileged non-interpretive access only to occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal and to the evidence (memory traces, private feelings) we use for self-interpretation

Outline — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Your Mind Better Than You Do

Establish that standing attitudes are behavioral-dispositional patterns requiring interpretive access, show that self-interpretation suffers systematic motivated reasoning distortions while other-interpretation does not, then argue that privileged first-person access applies only to occurrent states and self-interpretive evidence rather than to the standing attitudes themselves.

Surprise
The argument grants more to first-person privilege than usual skeptical positions (acknowledging genuine non-interpretive access to occurrent states and evidence) while still reaching a strongly revisionary conclusion. The structure places the scope-narrowing move before rather than after the motivated reasoning argument, making the case that we never had the kind of access that would matter.
Commitment
The paper commits to an empirical claim that motivated reasoning is sufficiently systematic and ineliminable to override evidential advantages, rather than treating it as one correctable bias among others. It also commits to functionalism about standing attitudes strongly enough that no amount of introspective phenomenology could constitute direct knowledge of them.
Gap
§4, inductive — The empirical evidence from social psychology about motivated reasoning in self-relevant contexts would have to generalize reliably to the philosophical domain of standing attitude interpretation, and the magnitude of distortion would have to be sufficient to outweigh any first-person evidential advantages systematically rather than occasionally.

Outline 1 — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

Distinguish occurrent from standing states, show traditional privileged access applies only to the former, then argue systematic biases make us worse interpreters of our own standing attitudes than external observers are.

Surprise
The argument inverts rather than dissolves the asymmetry, and does so by beginning with metaphysics of mental states rather than with epistemology. Most contemporary work either defends traditional first-person authority or argues for symmetrical unreliability; a systematic other-superiority claim for a major class of mental states has no clear precedent in recent literature.
Commitment
The paper commits to a genuinely bifurcated picture of self-knowledge where first-person authority is robust in one domain and systematically inferior in another, rather than hedging with 'sometimes' or 'to some degree'. It also commits to the empirical claim that motivated reasoning distorts self-interpretation of standing attitudes reliably enough to ground a philosophical thesis, not merely creates statistical tendencies.
Gap
§5, abductive — The absence of motivated reasoning and the presence of more complete behavioral data must be epistemically more valuable than privileged access to private evidence and reasoning processes when the interpretive task is identifying functional-dispositional patterns—this requires that pattern-detection advantages outweigh information advantages decisively enough to overcome the intuition that more evidence makes better interpretation.

Outline 2 — Against First-Personal Authority: The Interpretive Superiority of Others for Standing Attitudes

Establish that standing attitudes are functional-dispositional patterns requiring interpretation, demonstrate that self-interpretation suffers from systematic motivated distortions, and conclude that others' observational access yields superior interpretation despite subjects retaining non-interpretive access only to occurrent states.

Surprise
The outline treats occurrent access not as an objection to be answered but as a structural component of the thesis itself, using the delimitation of genuine first-personal authority to sharpen rather than threaten the inversion claim. Most arguments for other-knowledge superiority must defensively explain away first-person authority; this one incorporates it.
Commitment
The argument commits to the empirical claim that motivated reasoning distortions are not merely occasional or correctable but systematic and ineliminable in self-interpretation, making this a matter of structural epistemic position rather than contingent psychological failing. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes have equal or better access in specific cases.
Gap
§5, abductive — For this inference to succeed, it must be true that the magnitude of motivated reasoning distortion in self-interpretation exceeds the evidential disadvantage others face from lacking access to private phenomenology and memory, such that the net interpretive accuracy favors external observation despite its limitations.

Outline 3 — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

Distinguish types of mental states and access modes, then show that observational access to behavioral patterns systematically outperforms introspective access to functional-dispositional states while conceding introspective privilege only for occurrent judgments and private evidence.

Surprise
The paper defends other-superiority for standing attitudes while conceding traditional first-person authority for occurrent states, bifurcating the mental in a way the traditional debate does not. Most revisionist views either deny the asymmetry entirely (Gopnik, Carruthers) or preserve it for all mental states (Burge, Moran); this outline claims others know our minds better precisely where it matters most for character and identity.
Commitment
The outline commits to an empirical claim about the relative magnitude of two epistemic defects—motivated reasoning in self-interpretation versus evidential limitation in other-interpretation—that cautious versions could leave as 'depends on circumstances' or 'varies by person.' It also commits to functionalism about standing attitudes without hedging into a weaker 'if functionalism, then...' conditional, making the thesis hostage to that metaphysics.

Outline 4 — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

Establish that standing attitudes require interpretation, show that self-interpretation is systematically biased while other-interpretation is not, then demonstrate that only occurrent states and interpretive evidence remain privileged to the self.

Surprise
The paper treats other-superiority as the default requiring no special justification and makes first-person authority the phenomenon that needs explaining and limiting. Most self-knowledge literature assumes first-person privilege is the starting point and carves out exceptions; this outline reverses the burden entirely.
Commitment
The outline commits to the claim that motivated reasoning is not just one bias among others but a systematic asymmetry that cannot be corrected from the first-person perspective, and that this is sufficient to overturn first-person authority for dispositional states entirely. A cautious version would argue for 'sometimes superior' or 'equal in different ways' rather than systematic other-superiority.
Gap
§4, abductive — The systematic nature of motivated reasoning must be sufficient to reverse the standard presumption of first-person authority, which requires that no amount of additional first-person evidence can compensate for systematic interpretive bias, and that third-person behavioral observation provides adequate data despite lacking access to private states.

Outline 5 — Against First-Personal Authority: The Interpretive Superiority of Others for Standing Attitudes

Establish what privileged access actually covers (occurrent states and private evidence), then show why third-personal observation systematically outperforms self-interpretation for standing attitudes, then argue this produces genuine interpretive asymmetry favoring others.

Surprise
The structure front-loads the positive case for third-personal superiority before delimiting first-personal access, inverting the standard dialectic where residual self-authority is established first and then qualified. This makes the paper's burden defending an unusual existential claim (others are better) rather than a familiar negative one (selves aren't as good as thought).
Commitment
The outline commits to a clean metaphysical division where standing attitudes are purely functional-dispositional with no irreducible phenomenal or narrative component accessible only first-personally; a cautious version would allow that standing attitudes have both dispositional and phenomenal-narrative aspects, claiming other-superiority only for the former.
Gap
§5, abductive — The observational and motivational advantages documented must be sufficient to overcome any compensating epistemic advantages available to self-interpreters (such as greater sample size of observations, access to counterfactual reasoning about unrealized dispositions, or integration of private evidence) such that net interpretive position favors others.

Sharpest disagreement
