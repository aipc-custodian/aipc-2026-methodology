Target
Final value theorists like Kolodny, Scheffler, and Scanlon who argue friends' welfare has non-instrumental importance
Sketch

If my friend's good has final value, then setbacks to their good matter non-instrumentally. But my friend's good includes the satisfaction of their trivial preferences — getting the mug color they want contributes (however slightly) to their welfare. So on the final value view, I have non-instrumental reason to care about the mug color. Yet this seems absurd: good friends don't track such minutiae, and we don't criticize them for failing to. The key premise: there's no principled threshold at which components of a friend's good suddenly gain non-instrumental status. Either all aspects of their flourishing matter finally (absurd) or we need a derivative story that explains why only significant aspects engage our concern.

KILLER (PROVES TOO MUCH)

ATTEMPT. The argument assumes that if a friend's good has final value, then *every component* of their good, no matter how trivial, generates non-instrumental reasons of equal standing. But this premise, if sound, would equally undermine the final value of *anything*. Consider: your own well-being has final value (nearly everyone accepts this). Your well-being includes satisfaction of your own trivial preferences—whether you get the blue or red mug for yourself. So by this argument's logic, you must treat your own trivial preferences as non-instrumentally mattering in a way that seems absurd. Yet we don't conclude from this that your own good lacks final value—we simply recognize that final value admits of degrees, that trivial components contribute negligibly, and that rational attention is selective. The argument thus proves too much: it would force us to reject the final value of our own welfare, of happiness generally, of knowledge, of beauty—anything complex enough to admit trivial instantiations. Since the argument's structure applies equally to uncontroversial final values, it cannot specifically undermine friendship's final value without collapsing into absurdity.

DEFENDER

BEST DEFENCE.

The objection misses a crucial asymmetry between the first-personal and second-personal cases. When it comes to *my own* trivial preferences, I have direct, unmediated access to their significance (or lack thereof). I *feel* that the mug color doesn't matter much, and my practical reasoning can proportionally discount it without any theoretical commitment being violated. The final value of my welfare doesn't require that I consciously track every trivial contribution to it—I simply live my life, and trivial satisfactions contribute their negligible amounts automatically.

But friendship is essentially *other-directed*. The final value theorist claims I have non-instrumental reason to care about *your* good because of what it is, not because of what it does for me. This creates a distinctive pressure: I must somehow *track* and *respond to* components of your welfare from the outside. And here's the rub: if your getting the blue mug contributes (however slightly) to your welfare, and your welfare has final value for me, then I seem rationally required to have *some* pro-attitude toward your getting the blue mug—not for instrumental reasons, but because it's a constituent of something finally valuable.

The problem isn't that trivial components contribute trivially (everyone grants that). The problem is that the *source* of my reason to care—the final value of your welfare—doesn't come with a built-in threshold mechanism. Either the blue mug matters to me non-instrumentally (because it's part of your good) or my concern must be explained derivatively (I care because you care, or because caring about you requires responsiveness). The first horn is absurd; the second abandons final value.

With my own welfare, no such threshold problem arises. I don't need a theory of which components to care about—I just care about what I care about.

VERDICT.

But the proves-too-much objection already showed that final value *doesn't* require tracking every trivial component—not for my own welfare, not for happiness generally, not for anything.

KILLER (TRIVIAL)

ATTEMPT.

The thesis presents itself as revealing a problematic "commitment" of final value theories, but it actually just restates what final value theorists already explicitly acknowledge and build into their accounts. The claim that "treating X as finally valuable commits you to treating trivial components of X as mattering non-instrumentally" is not a discovery—it's a verbal repackaging of what "final value" means, combined with the uncontroversial observation that complex goods have trivial components.

Every final value theorist already accepts that:

No final value theorist denies this. The thesis is thus trivial: it observes that complex final values have trivial components (true but uncontested) and dresses this as a reductio, when it's actually just how final value works.

ATTEMPT

The thesis equivocates on "mattering non-instrumentally" between a metaphysical sense and a practical-deliberative sense, and the argument requires both readings to generate its apparent force.

Metaphysical reading: A component of a finally valuable whole "matters non-instrumentally" just means it contributes to something valuable for its own sake, rather than as a means to something else. On this reading, the claim that trivial preferences matter non-instrumentally is unproblematic—the blue mug contributes (negligibly) to welfare, welfare has final value, therefore the mug contributes to something finally valuable. This is a purely structural claim about value-composition.

Practical-deliberative reading: Something "matters non-instrumentally" means it properly figures in deliberation as a consideration, generates felt reasons, should be tracked and responded to, and its neglect constitutes a failing. On this reading, the claim that trivial preferences matter non-instrumentally is indeed absurd—good friends don't deliberate about mug colors, don't track such minutiae, and aren't criticized for ignorance of them.

The argument's force depends on running these together. The premise "my friend's good has final value, so setbacks to their good matter non-instrumentally" is plausible only on the metaphysical reading—it's just unpacking what final value means. But the conclusion "good friends don't track such minutiae, so this is absurd" works only on the practical-deliberative reading—it's pointing to norms of actual friendship. The argument appears to refute final value theories only by equivocating: establishing that trivial components matter metaphysically, then treating this as a practical-deliberative commitment, and finding that commitment absurd.

Final value theorists can accept the metaphysical claim while denying the practical-deliberative one, blocking the inference entirely.

DEFENDER

BEST DEFENCE

The objection trades on an ambiguity in "for his sake" that the thesis anticipates. When Alice cares about Bob's shortened life "for his sake," this phrase can mean two different things, and only one avoids the instability.

First possibility: "for his sake" means Alice's concern takes Bob's good as its intentional object rather than her own. But this is just reading (1) — the friend's good matters *to Alice* non-instrumentally. The thesis grants this happens; the point is that this structure is still relational and derivative. The concern exists because of how Bob stands to Alice (as friend), and its normative force flows through that relation. If we ask "why does Bob's reduced lifespan matter?" the answer is "because Alice cares about it, and she cares because he's her friend." This makes Bob's good matter *in virtue of* the friendship relation, which is exactly the derivative structure the thesis identifies. The objection says "the friendship is sufficient" as grounding, but sufficiency for generating Alice's concern doesn't establish that Bob's good has non-derivative final value — it establishes that the friendship derivatively generates value for Alice.

Second possibility: "for his sake" means Bob's shortened life is *objectively bad* — bad period, not just bad-relative-to-Alice's-concerns. But now we need an answer to: why is it objectively bad? The objection says "because he is her friend," but this cannot work. Whether Bob is Alice's friend is a relational fact about Bob and Alice. Relational facts about Bob cannot ground objective final value in Bob's outcomes unless the relation itself tracks something value-conferring. If I declare a rock my friend, the rock's "good" doesn't thereby acquire objective significance. So the friendship must be tracking something — Bob's personhood, his capacity for well-being, his status as a locus of value — that grounds why his good matters period. That's exactly the external grounding the thesis predicts is needed.

The objection claims the thesis conflates the *basis* of concern with *whose good* is the object, but this misses the point. The thesis is about what grounds non-derivative final value. Alice's concern being *directed at* Bob's good rather than hers doesn't settle whether Bob's good has non-derivative value or derivative value (derived from the friendship relation). The "stranger with identical properties" test actually supports the thesis: it shows Bob's good matters *because of the friendship relation*, which is precisely what makes it relationally derivative rather than finally valuable in itself.
