## Outline 0 — The Observational Advantage: Why Others Know Your Attitudes Better Than You Do

*Establish criteria for attitude knowledge through behavioral patterns, show self-observation fails these criteria due to motivated reasoning while external observation meets them, defend that occurrent access doesn't extend to attitudes, conclude others have superior access to our standing attitudes while our privilege is limited to occurrent states.*

- **Surprise.** Most arguments about self-knowledge asymmetry defend or deny first-person authority across the board. This outline grants traditional first-person privilege for occurrent states while inverting it for standing attitudes, using the same evidence (behavioral patterns) both parties must interpret but arguing the third-person position interprets it better. The structural surprise is that the privilege-granting section (5) strengthens rather than weakens the inversion by clarifying exactly what we do and don't have privileged access to.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the strong empirical claim that motivated reasoning in self-interpretation is systematic and ineliminable rather than a correctable defect, making third-person observation structurally superior rather than merely contingently better. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes have better access in certain domains, or that both perspectives have complementary advantages. This version commits to an across-the-board superiority claim for standing attitudes.
- **Gap.** §4, abductive: The motivational neutrality of external observers must sufficiently outweigh any epistemic advantages of the first-person perspective (access to reasons, context, disambiguating evidence) such that overall reliability in pattern identification favors the external position despite its informational limitations.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** Standing attitudes (beliefs, desires, values) are individuated by stable behavioral-cum-cognitive dispositions across contexts rather than by any momentarily accessible mental state.

## Outline 1 — The Inversion of Epistemic Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Standing Attitudes Better Than We Do

*Establish a distinction between two kinds of psychological states (standing attitudes vs occurrent judgments), show they require different epistemic methods (behavioral interpretation vs direct access), demonstrate that the distribution of epistemic advantages inverts across this distinction, then argue this inversion undermines the traditional privileged access thesis.*

- **Surprise.** The structure places motivated reasoning at the center of an epistemic advantage argument, making what looks like a cognitive defect into the key to understanding why others know us better in one domain. Specialists expect motivated reasoning to appear as an objection to be handled; here it is load-bearing for the positive case.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to denying that first-person authority extends to standing attitudes at all, not merely that it is defeasible or limited. Section 3 must argue that others are systematically better positioned, not equally positioned or better only sometimes. This rules out conciliatory positions where both perspectives contribute complementary insights to standing attitude ascription.
- **Gap.** §3, abductive: For the inference to go through, it must be true that motivated reasoning distorts first-person pattern detection more severely than third-person observational limitations (perspective effects, limited sampling, context-dependence) distort third-person pattern detection, and that this distortion is systematic rather than occasional.
- **Arrives at.** §5
- **Load-bearing.** Non-interpretive access (direct awareness without inference) is appropriate only for occurrent mental acts at the moment they occur, while interpretive access (inference from behavioral evidence) is required for standing dispositional patterns.

## Outline 2 — Against Interpretive Privilege: How Self-Ignorance Structures Self-Knowledge

*Establish that standing attitudes are essentially dispositional-functional patterns requiring behavioral evidence, then show that motivated reasoning systematically corrupts first-person access to this evidence while leaving occurrent states epistemically accessible, yielding the inverted asymmetry*

- **Surprise.** The paper spends more length defending the motivated reasoning premise (sections 5-6) than establishing the dispositional nature of attitudes (section 1) or the behavioral evidence asymmetry (section 3). This inverts the expected weight distribution because the dispositional claim is familiar from Ryle while motivated reasoning faces live empirical and philosophical resistance.
- **Commitment.** The paper commits to standing attitudes being nothing over and above behavioral-functional patterns—not patterns plus an inner state, not patterns grounded in neural representations. A cautious version would say first-person access to standing attitudes is comparatively worse; this version says others are literally better positioned for this specific cognitive task.
- **Gap.** §4, abductive: The motivated reasoning corruption must be systematic rather than sporadic, sufficiently robust to survive compensating first-person advantages, and domain-specific to standing attitudes rather than affecting all self-knowledge equally—which requires that standing attitudes specifically be identity-constitutive in ways occurrent states are not.
- **Arrives at.** §8
- **Load-bearing.** First-person interpretation of one's own behavioral patterns is systematically corrupted by motivated reasoning in ways that third-person interpretation is not, because self-interpretation threatens identity-constitutive self-conceptions while other-interpretation does not.

## Outline 3 — The Interpretive Asymmetry Inverted: Why Others Know Our Attitudes Better Than We Do

*Distinguish standing attitudes from occurrent states, show others have better epistemic access to the former while we retain privilege only for the latter, then argue motivated reasoning explains the asymmetry*

- **Surprise.** The argumentative weight falls entirely on motivated reasoning (§4) rather than on metaphysics of mind or observation, treating the epistemic asymmetry as a bias phenomenon rather than an access phenomenon. Most treatments of self-knowledge asymmetry locate the key issue in what kinds of states we have and how they're accessed; this makes it about what happens when privileged access feeds interpretation.
- **Commitment.** This outline commits to the empirical claim that motivated reasoning is ineliminable from self-interpretation even for careful, reflective subjects—not just that we sometimes deceive ourselves, but that the interpretive situation structurally prevents us from accessing our standing attitudes as reliably as observers can. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes have advantages in specific circumstances.
- **Gap.** §4, inductive: The motivated reasoning effects documented in cases where subjects have clear incentives to reach flattering conclusions must generalize to the ordinary case of self-interpretation where the distorting motivation operates below the threshold of conscious awareness and the interpreter has no recognition that self-flattering bias is at work
- **Arrives at.** §5
- **Load-bearing.** When subjects use their privileged access to occurrent states and private evidence to interpret their own standing attitudes, motivated reasoning systematically distorts this interpretation in ways that third-person observation avoids.

## Outline 4 — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

*Distinguish standing attitudes from occurrent states, show behavioral evidence grounds the former while first-person access grounds only the latter, then argue others' systematic observation beats our motivated self-interpretation for standing attitudes*

- **Surprise.** The paper grants traditional first-person authority where philosophers have been most skeptical (occurrent judgments as non-interpretive) while denying it where it has seemed most secure (knowledge of one's own enduring beliefs and desires). The structural surprise is that the dialectical work happens by showing each opponent correctly identifies one side of the distinction but fails to see it is only one side.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to a functionalist metaphysics of standing attitudes strong enough that no internal state could constitute evidence for them independent of behavior—ruling out any phenomenological or introspective route to self-knowledge of beliefs and desires. A cautious version would leave room for introspection to provide some evidence about standing attitudes even if behavioral evidence were evidentially superior.
- **Gap.** §4, inductive: The motivated reasoning documented in laboratory settings and particular domains (political belief, self-enhancement) must generalize to self-interpretation of standing attitudes across the board, rather than being limited to high-stakes identity-relevant cases where subjects might otherwise have reasonable access
- **Arrives at.** §5
- **Load-bearing.** Determining what standing attitudes a person has requires systematic observation of behavioral patterns across contexts, because standing attitudes are constituted by such patterns rather than by any introspectible internal state.

## Outline 5 — The Asymmetry Inverted: Why Others Know Our Attitudes Better Than We Do

*Establish a distinction between occurrent judgments and standing attitudes, show we have privileged access only to the former, demonstrate that standing attitudes require behavioral evidence that others observe more reliably, then argue the asymmetry inverts for standing attitudes specifically*

- **Surprise.** The outline locates self-knowledge's distinctive success in the least philosophically interesting place—momentary avowals and raw feels—while giving away authority over what philosophers have cared most about (belief, desire, intention) to external observers. Most restructurings of self-knowledge debates preserve some domain of first-person authority over contentful attitudes; this one deliberately does not.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to denying that privileged access to evidence (memories, feelings, imaginative exercises) provides a net epistemic advantage over comprehensive behavioral observation, which requires holding that motivated reasoning and selective attention create systematic distortions large enough to outweigh informational advantages. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes know our attitudes better in specific domains or conditions; this version claims structural superiority for others regarding standing attitudes as such.
- **Gap.** §4, abductive: It must be true that the epistemic disadvantages others face (limited observational access, inability to observe private feelings, less comprehensive knowledge of the subject's history) are outweighed by their epistemic advantages (comprehensive pattern detection, freedom from motivated reasoning) sufficiently often that they achieve superior reliability overall, and that standing attitudes are sufficiently constituted by behavioral patterns that comprehensive observation of those patterns grounds better interpretation than privileged access to selected internal evidence.
- **Arrives at.** §5
- **Load-bearing.** Self-ascription of standing attitudes is an interpretive process using the same kinds of behavioral and contextual evidence available to others, but with systematic distortions from motivated reasoning, narrative self-conception, and privileged access to selected evidence rather than comprehensive patterns.

## Divergence

**Forced** — every outline does this, so the thesis requires it:

- Distinguish standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns) from occurrent judgments (token mental events at moments of avowal)
- Establish that standing attitudes are constituted by behavioral patterns across contexts rather than introspectible internal states
- Argue that knowledge of standing attitudes requires interpretation of behavioral evidence
- Show that first-person privileged access extends to occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal
- Show that first-person privileged access extends to evidence used in self-interpretation (memory traces, phenomenal states)
- Argue that motivated reasoning systematically distorts first-person interpretation of behavioral patterns
- Argue that third-person observers lack the motivated reasoning that distorts first-person interpretation
- Conclude that others have superior access to standing attitudes while first-person privilege is limited to occurrent states and evidence

**Free** — the outlines split, so this is yours to choose:

- **whether to establish criteria for attitude knowledge before showing self-observation fails them** — establish general criteria for knowing dispositional patterns, then show self-observation fails these criteria (outline 0); proceed directly to showing the epistemic advantages distribute differently across the standing/occurrent distinction (outlines 1, 3, 4, 5) *(outline 0 alone establishes criteria first; outlines 1, 3, 4, 5 move directly to distributing advantages)*
- **whether to argue that third-person observers have MORE behavioral evidence than first-person subjects** — argue third-person observers access more numerous and varied instances of behavioral patterns (outline 2); argue only that third-person observers interpret the same behavioral evidence without motivated reasoning (outlines 0, 1, 3, 4, 5) *(outline 2 claims observational advantage in quantity/variety; others claim only interpretive advantage)*
- **whether to explain WHY motivated reasoning affects self-interpretation specifically** — explain that standing attitudes are identity-constitutive and their accurate assessment threatens self-conception (outlines 2, 4); assert motivated reasoning distorts self-interpretation without explaining the mechanism (outlines 0, 1, 3, 5) *(outlines 2 and 4 explain identity-threat mechanism; outlines 0, 1, 3, 5 treat motivated reasoning as given)*
- **whether to address and reject compensating mechanisms for first-person advantage** — explicitly argue that privileged access to reasons, formation memories, and phenomenal feels doesn't overcome motivated reasoning because these themselves get reinterpreted (outline 2); omit discussion of why privileged evidence doesn't compensate for motivated reasoning (outlines 0, 1, 3, 4, 5) *(outline 2 alone addresses and rejects compensating mechanisms)*
- **whether to argue the inversion undermines traditional self-knowledge debates** — conclude by showing the inversion reveals false presuppositions in traditional debates about uniform epistemic privilege (outline 1); conclude with the inversion thesis itself without meta-philosophical implications (outlines 0, 2, 3, 4, 5) *(outline 1 alone draws meta-philosophical conclusions about the debate)*
- **whether to establish that non-interpretive access is appropriate ONLY for occurrent states** — argue non-interpretive access is categorically appropriate only for occurrent mental acts, while interpretive access is required for dispositional patterns (outline 1); show first-person access extends to occurrent states without arguing this is the only appropriate domain for non-interpretive access (outlines 0, 2, 3, 4, 5) *(outline 1 makes a categorical claim about appropriate epistemic methods; others describe what access extends to)*
- **how to characterize what first-person privilege extends to regarding evidence** — privileged access to 'currently accessible evidence' or 'evidence at the moment of introspection' (outlines 0, 3); privileged access to 'phenomenal evidence' or 'memorial and phenomenal evidence' (outlines 1, 4); privileged access to 'memory traces, felt dispositions, proprioceptive states' with detailed enumeration (outline 5); privileged access to 'memory traces, current phenomenal states' as evidence that itself becomes object of motivated reinterpretation (outline 2) *(outline 0 and 3 use temporal qualifiers; outline 1 and 4 use 'phenomenal'; outline 5 enumerates types; outline 2 notes evidence gets reinterpreted)*

**Sharpest disagreement.** Whether third-person observers have MORE behavioral evidence than first-person subjects (outline 2) versus whether they merely interpret the SAME evidence without motivated reasoning (all others). This determines whether the argument is about observational position or interpretive corruption—whether others win by seeing more or by seeing clearly. If outline 2 is right, the paper must defend that we somehow observe our own behavior less comprehensively than others do, which seems empirically dubious. If the others are right, the paper can grant we have all the behavioral evidence but argue we systematically misinterpret it, which is psychologically more plausible but makes motivated reasoning do all the work.

**Absent from all of them.** None of the outlines address potential objections that first-person subjects could overcome motivated reasoning through deliberate self-distancing, therapeutic techniques, or adopting third-person perspective on themselves—they treat motivated reasoning as if it were insurmountable rather than explaining why these corrective strategies fail or are unavailable
