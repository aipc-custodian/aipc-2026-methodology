Thesis (fixed)
The asymmetry between self- and other-ascription is real but inverted: others have superior interpretive access to our standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns) because they observe behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning, while we have privileged non-interpretive access only to occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal and to the evidence (memory traces, private feelings) we use for self-interpretation

Built on the post-kill restatement. The headline thesis was: The asymmetry between self- and other-ascription is real but inverted from what everyone assumes: others have MORE interpretive access to our standing attitudes than we do, while we have privileged access only to our occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal.

Outline — The Observational Advantage: Why Others Know Your Attitudes Better Than You Do

Establish criteria for attitude knowledge through behavioral patterns, show self-observation fails these criteria due to motivated reasoning while external observation meets them, defend that occurrent access doesn't extend to attitudes, conclude others have superior access to our standing attitudes while our privilege is limited to occurrent states.

Surprise
Most arguments about self-knowledge asymmetry defend or deny first-person authority across the board. This outline grants traditional first-person privilege for occurrent states while inverting it for standing attitudes, using the same evidence (behavioral patterns) both parties must interpret but arguing the third-person position interprets it better. The structural surprise is that the privilege-granting section (5) strengthens rather than weakens the inversion by clarifying exactly what we do and don't have privileged access to.
Commitment
The outline commits to the strong empirical claim that motivated reasoning in self-interpretation is systematic and ineliminable rather than a correctable defect, making third-person observation structurally superior rather than merely contingently better. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes have better access in certain domains, or that both perspectives have complementary advantages. This version commits to an across-the-board superiority claim for standing attitudes.
Gap
§4, abductive — The motivational neutrality of external observers must sufficiently outweigh any epistemic advantages of the first-person perspective (access to reasons, context, disambiguating evidence) such that overall reliability in pattern identification favors the external position despite its informational limitations.

Outline 1 — The Inversion of Epistemic Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Standing Attitudes Better Than We Do

Establish a distinction between two kinds of psychological states (standing attitudes vs occurrent judgments), show they require different epistemic methods (behavioral interpretation vs direct access), demonstrate that the distribution of epistemic advantages inverts across this distinction, then argue this inversion undermines the traditional privileged access thesis.

Surprise
The structure places motivated reasoning at the center of an epistemic advantage argument, making what looks like a cognitive defect into the key to understanding why others know us better in one domain. Specialists expect motivated reasoning to appear as an objection to be handled; here it is load-bearing for the positive case.
Commitment
The outline commits to denying that first-person authority extends to standing attitudes at all, not merely that it is defeasible or limited. Section 3 must argue that others are systematically better positioned, not equally positioned or better only sometimes. This rules out conciliatory positions where both perspectives contribute complementary insights to standing attitude ascription.
Gap
§3, abductive — For the inference to go through, it must be true that motivated reasoning distorts first-person pattern detection more severely than third-person observational limitations (perspective effects, limited sampling, context-dependence) distort third-person pattern detection, and that this distortion is systematic rather than occasional.

Outline 2 — Against Interpretive Privilege: How Self-Ignorance Structures Self-Knowledge

Establish that standing attitudes are essentially dispositional-functional patterns requiring behavioral evidence, then show that motivated reasoning systematically corrupts first-person access to this evidence while leaving occurrent states epistemically accessible, yielding the inverted asymmetry

Surprise
The paper spends more length defending the motivated reasoning premise (sections 5-6) than establishing the dispositional nature of attitudes (section 1) or the behavioral evidence asymmetry (section 3). This inverts the expected weight distribution because the dispositional claim is familiar from Ryle while motivated reasoning faces live empirical and philosophical resistance.
Commitment
The paper commits to standing attitudes being nothing over and above behavioral-functional patterns—not patterns plus an inner state, not patterns grounded in neural representations. A cautious version would say first-person access to standing attitudes is comparatively worse; this version says others are literally better positioned for this specific cognitive task.
Gap
§4, abductive — The motivated reasoning corruption must be systematic rather than sporadic, sufficiently robust to survive compensating first-person advantages, and domain-specific to standing attitudes rather than affecting all self-knowledge equally—which requires that standing attitudes specifically be identity-constitutive in ways occurrent states are not.

Outline 3 — The Interpretive Asymmetry Inverted: Why Others Know Our Attitudes Better Than We Do

Distinguish standing attitudes from occurrent states, show others have better epistemic access to the former while we retain privilege only for the latter, then argue motivated reasoning explains the asymmetry

Surprise
The argumentative weight falls entirely on motivated reasoning (§4) rather than on metaphysics of mind or observation, treating the epistemic asymmetry as a bias phenomenon rather than an access phenomenon. Most treatments of self-knowledge asymmetry locate the key issue in what kinds of states we have and how they're accessed; this makes it about what happens when privileged access feeds interpretation.
Commitment
This outline commits to the empirical claim that motivated reasoning is ineliminable from self-interpretation even for careful, reflective subjects—not just that we sometimes deceive ourselves, but that the interpretive situation structurally prevents us from accessing our standing attitudes as reliably as observers can. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes have advantages in specific circumstances.
Gap
§4, inductive — The motivated reasoning effects documented in cases where subjects have clear incentives to reach flattering conclusions must generalize to the ordinary case of self-interpretation where the distorting motivation operates below the threshold of conscious awareness and the interpreter has no recognition that self-flattering bias is at work

Outline 4 — The Inverted Asymmetry: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

Distinguish standing attitudes from occurrent states, show behavioral evidence grounds the former while first-person access grounds only the latter, then argue others' systematic observation beats our motivated self-interpretation for standing attitudes

Surprise
The paper grants traditional first-person authority where philosophers have been most skeptical (occurrent judgments as non-interpretive) while denying it where it has seemed most secure (knowledge of one's own enduring beliefs and desires). The structural surprise is that the dialectical work happens by showing each opponent correctly identifies one side of the distinction but fails to see it is only one side.
Commitment
The outline commits to a functionalist metaphysics of standing attitudes strong enough that no internal state could constitute evidence for them independent of behavior—ruling out any phenomenological or introspective route to self-knowledge of beliefs and desires. A cautious version would leave room for introspection to provide some evidence about standing attitudes even if behavioral evidence were evidentially superior.
Gap
§4, inductive — The motivated reasoning documented in laboratory settings and particular domains (political belief, self-enhancement) must generalize to self-interpretation of standing attitudes across the board, rather than being limited to high-stakes identity-relevant cases where subjects might otherwise have reasonable access

Outline 5 — The Asymmetry Inverted: Why Others Know Our Attitudes Better Than We Do

Establish a distinction between occurrent judgments and standing attitudes, show we have privileged access only to the former, demonstrate that standing attitudes require behavioral evidence that others observe more reliably, then argue the asymmetry inverts for standing attitudes specifically

Surprise
The outline locates self-knowledge's distinctive success in the least philosophically interesting place—momentary avowals and raw feels—while giving away authority over what philosophers have cared most about (belief, desire, intention) to external observers. Most restructurings of self-knowledge debates preserve some domain of first-person authority over contentful attitudes; this one deliberately does not.
Commitment
The outline commits to denying that privileged access to evidence (memories, feelings, imaginative exercises) provides a net epistemic advantage over comprehensive behavioral observation, which requires holding that motivated reasoning and selective attention create systematic distortions large enough to outweigh informational advantages. A cautious version would claim only that others sometimes know our attitudes better in specific domains or conditions; this version claims structural superiority for others regarding standing attitudes as such.
Gap
§4, abductive — It must be true that the epistemic disadvantages others face (limited observational access, inability to observe private feelings, less comprehensive knowledge of the subject's history) are outweighed by their epistemic advantages (comprehensive pattern detection, freedom from motivated reasoning) sufficiently often that they achieve superior reliability overall, and that standing attitudes are sufficiently constituted by behavioral patterns that comprehensive observation of those patterns grounds better interpretation than privileged access to selected internal evidence.

Sharpest disagreement
Whether third-person observers have MORE behavioral evidence than first-person subjects (outline 2) versus whether they merely interpret the SAME evidence without motivated reasoning (all others). This determines whether the argument is about observational position or interpretive corruption—whether others win by seeing more or by seeing clearly. If outline 2 is right, the paper must defend that we somehow observe our own behavior less comprehensively than others do, which seems empirically dubious. If the others are right, the paper can grant we have all the behavioral evidence but argue we systematically misinterpret it, which is psychologically more plausible but makes motivated reasoning do all the work.
Absent from all of them
None of the outlines address potential objections that first-person subjects could overcome motivated reasoning through deliberate self-distancing, therapeutic techniques, or adopting third-person perspective on themselves—they treat motivated reasoning as if it were insurmountable rather than explaining why these corrective strategies fail or are unavailable
