Thesis (fixed)
The asymmetry between self- and other-ascription is real but inverted: others have superior interpretive access to our standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns) because they observe behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning, while we have privileged non-interpretive access only to occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal and to the evidence (memory traces, private feelings) we use for self-interpretation

Outline — The Occurrent/Standing Divide: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

Establish that self-knowledge divides into two kinds with opposite asymmetries—privileged occurrent access and inferior standing-attitude access—then show this division explains both phenomenology and confabulation data better than uniform first-person authority accounts.

Surprise
The architecture makes others epistemically superior for the central case (standing attitudes like beliefs and desires) while preserving first-person authority only for a narrow domain (occurrent acts). Most revisionist views nibble at the edges of first-person authority; this inverts the core and vindicates the intuition only at the margins.
Commitment
The outline commits to treating motivated reasoning as not just a correctable bias but as a structural feature of self-interpretation that makes third-person access superior in principle, not merely in certain circumstances—this rules out therapeutic or methodological fixes to first-person interpretation and requires accepting that the phenomenology of self-knowledge is systematically misleading about where our epistemic situation is strongest.
Gap
§5, abductive — Motivated reasoning distortions must be sufficiently systematic and severe that they outweigh the first-person's advantages in quantity of evidence and access to private data—the inference requires that the quality penalty (bias) exceeds the quantity advantage (more data points) for standing attitude interpretation.

Outline 1 — The Occurrent Turn: Why Self-Knowledge Is Not About Standing Attitudes

Establish that the traditional debate mislocates the asymmetry by focusing on standing attitudes, then show that occurrent judgment is non-interpretive while standing attitude self-ascription is interpretive, then demonstrate that observers have superior access to the interpretive domain while we retain authority only in the occurrent domain.

Surprise
The paper concedes the skeptical case about standing attitudes rather than defending against it, then relocates authority to a domain (occurrent judgments) that the traditional debate has treated as uncontroversial. The load-bearing claim is not about what we can know but about what others know better.
Commitment
The paper commits to the strong empirical claim that others are systematically better interpreters of our standing attitudes—not merely that we are unreliable, but that the third-person perspective is epistemically superior for this domain. This goes beyond the confabulation literature's claim of first-person unreliability to assert second-person superiority.
Gap
§2, inductive — The confabulation literature would have to generalize from experimental contexts to standing attitude self-ascription generally, and the advantage others gain from avoiding motivated reasoning would have to systematically outweigh the evidential disadvantages they face from lacking access to private mental states—requiring that behavioral patterns be more probative than the combination of behavioral patterns plus private evidence when motivated reasoning corrupts the interpretation of the latter.

Outline 2 — The Occurrent/Standing Divide: Why Others Know Your Beliefs Better Than You Do

Establish a sharp distinction between occurrent judgments and standing attitudes, show that first-person authority applies only to the former while third-person observation succeeds better with the latter, then argue this explains both phenomenology and empirical data better than traditional accounts.

Surprise
The structure treats the conceptual distinction (section 1) and critical engagement (section 2) as prior to presenting empirical evidence (section 3), inverting the usual order. This is necessary because the confabulation literature is ambiguous between threatening all first-person authority and revealing a selective pattern—only with the occurrent/standing distinction in hand can we see which interpretation the data support.
Commitment
The paper commits to a sharp metaphysical distinction between occurrent judgments as constitutive acts and standing attitudes as dispositional patterns, ruling out any middle ground where standing attitudes might be hybrid states with both dispositional and occurrent-accessible components. A hedged version would leave room for degrees of occurrentness or allow that some standing attitudes might be introspectively accessible.
Gap
§6, abductive — The best explanation of why self-reports about standing attitudes are systematically unreliable while other-reports are more accurate must be that others and ourselves are working from the same evidence base (behavioral patterns) but only self-interpretation is distorted by motivated reasoning—this requires that there be no additional privileged internal evidence that would give self-interpretation an advantage despite the motivational distortion.

Surprise

Sharpest disagreement
Whether to lead with the occurrent/standing distinction itself or with the standing-attitude half of the inversion. Leading with the distinction (outlines 0, 2, 4) frames the paper as discovering that a familiar category divides into two with opposite epistemic profiles. Leading with third-person superiority on standing attitudes (outlines 1, 3, 5) frames the paper as inverting received wisdom about self-knowledge, with the occurrent domain emerging as the residual space of first-person authority. This determines whether readers experience the thesis as 'the traditional debate was asking about the wrong thing' versus 'the traditional answer was backwards.'
Absent from all of them
None of the outlines includes a section defending the functional-dispositional conception of standing attitudes against alternatives (like standing attitudes as stored representations or as higher-order dispositions to form occurrent judgments). This conception is load-bearing—if standing attitudes were introspectable mental states rather than behavioral patterns, the third-person superiority claim would fail—but every outline treats it as established in passing rather than argued for.
