# Outlines — selfo2

**Thesis (fixed):** The asymmetry between self- and other-ascription is real but inverted: others have superior interpretive access to our standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns) because they observe behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning, while we have privileged non-interpretive access only to occurrent judgments in the moment of avowal and to the evidence (memory traces, private feelings) we use for self-interpretation

## Outline 0 — The Occurrent/Standing Divide: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

*Establish that self-knowledge divides into two kinds with opposite asymmetries—privileged occurrent access and inferior standing-attitude access—then show this division explains both phenomenology and confabulation data better than uniform first-person authority accounts.*

- **Surprise.** The architecture makes others epistemically superior for the central case (standing attitudes like beliefs and desires) while preserving first-person authority only for a narrow domain (occurrent acts). Most revisionist views nibble at the edges of first-person authority; this inverts the core and vindicates the intuition only at the margins.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to treating motivated reasoning as not just a correctable bias but as a structural feature of self-interpretation that makes third-person access superior in principle, not merely in certain circumstances—this rules out therapeutic or methodological fixes to first-person interpretation and requires accepting that the phenomenology of self-knowledge is systematically misleading about where our epistemic situation is strongest.
- **Gap.** §5, abductive: Motivated reasoning distortions must be sufficiently systematic and severe that they outweigh the first-person's advantages in quantity of evidence and access to private data—the inference requires that the quality penalty (bias) exceeds the quantity advantage (more data points) for standing attitude interpretation.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** When we report our own standing attitudes, we rely on the same behavioral-pattern interpretation that external observers use, drawing on memory traces and self-observation rather than direct access.

## Outline 1 — The Occurrent Turn: Why Self-Knowledge Is Not About Standing Attitudes

*Establish that the traditional debate mislocates the asymmetry by focusing on standing attitudes, then show that occurrent judgment is non-interpretive while standing attitude self-ascription is interpretive, then demonstrate that observers have superior access to the interpretive domain while we retain authority only in the occurrent domain.*

- **Surprise.** The paper concedes the skeptical case about standing attitudes rather than defending against it, then relocates authority to a domain (occurrent judgments) that the traditional debate has treated as uncontroversial. The load-bearing claim is not about what we can know but about what others know better.
- **Commitment.** The paper commits to the strong empirical claim that others are systematically better interpreters of our standing attitudes—not merely that we are unreliable, but that the third-person perspective is epistemically superior for this domain. This goes beyond the confabulation literature's claim of first-person unreliability to assert second-person superiority.
- **Gap.** §2, inductive: The confabulation literature would have to generalize from experimental contexts to standing attitude self-ascription generally, and the advantage others gain from avoiding motivated reasoning would have to systematically outweigh the evidential disadvantages they face from lacking access to private mental states—requiring that behavioral patterns be more probative than the combination of behavioral patterns plus private evidence when motivated reasoning corrupts the interpretation of the latter.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** When we ascribe standing attitudes to ourselves, we use the same interpretive processes that others use to ascribe attitudes to us, except that others systematically outperform us because they observe our behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning distorting their interpretation.

## Outline 2 — The Occurrent/Standing Divide: Why Others Know Your Beliefs Better Than You Do

*Establish a sharp distinction between occurrent judgments and standing attitudes, show that first-person authority applies only to the former while third-person observation succeeds better with the latter, then argue this explains both phenomenology and empirical data better than traditional accounts.*

- **Surprise.** The structure treats the conceptual distinction (section 1) and critical engagement (section 2) as prior to presenting empirical evidence (section 3), inverting the usual order. This is necessary because the confabulation literature is ambiguous between threatening all first-person authority and revealing a selective pattern—only with the occurrent/standing distinction in hand can we see which interpretation the data support.
- **Commitment.** The paper commits to a sharp metaphysical distinction between occurrent judgments as constitutive acts and standing attitudes as dispositional patterns, ruling out any middle ground where standing attitudes might be hybrid states with both dispositional and occurrent-accessible components. A hedged version would leave room for degrees of occurrentness or allow that some standing attitudes might be introspectively accessible.
- **Gap.** §6, abductive: The best explanation of why self-reports about standing attitudes are systematically unreliable while other-reports are more accurate must be that others and ourselves are working from the same evidence base (behavioral patterns) but only self-interpretation is distorted by motivated reasoning—this requires that there be no additional privileged internal evidence that would give self-interpretation an advantage despite the motivational distortion.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** First-person authority obtains precisely for occurrent judgments and fails for standing attitudes, as shown by the match between the occurrent/standing distinction and the empirical pattern of when self-ascription succeeds versus fails.

## Outline 3 — The Occurrent Inversion: Why Others Know Our Beliefs Better Than We Do

*Establish that standing attitudes require interpretation for anyone, show that others interpret our patterns better than we interpret our own, then argue that genuine first-person authority exists only for occurrent acts of judgment that constitute momentary attitudes rather than reporting standing ones.*

- **Surprise.** The structure gives no section to defending the occurrent/standing distinction itself—it treats that distinction as standard, then inverts which party has access to which side. The surprise is that what looks like a revisionary metaphysics of mind (occurrent attitudes constituted by avowals) is actually conservative, requiring only that we stop assuming first-person authority tracks standing attitudes.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to treating confabulation as the normal case for standing attitude self-ascription rather than as noise around a generally reliable process. A cautious version would say we sometimes confabulate; this says we always interpret standing attitudes and that observers often interpret better, making third-person ascriptions more reliable than first-person ones for the attitudes we care about most in agency (beliefs, intentions).
- **Gap.** §3, inductive: The systematic distortions introduced by motivated reasoning in self-interpretation must outweigh both the evidential advantages subjects have from memory and phenomenology and any distortions observers face from limited behavioral samples and projection of their own attitudes onto others.
- **Arrives at.** §6
- **Load-bearing.** Both self-ascription and other-ascription of standing attitudes require interpretation of behavioral and affective evidence rather than direct detection of psychological facts.

## Outline 4 — The Occurrent Turn: Why Others Know Our Minds Better Than We Do

*Establish a fundamental distinction between standing and occurrent attitudes, show that privileged access applies only to the latter, demonstrate that others have superior access to the former through behavioral observation, then explain why this inversion resolves apparent contradictions in the self-knowledge literature.*

- **Surprise.** The structure frontloads the standing/occurrent distinction in section 1 and makes it do sustained work through sections 3, 4, and 5, rather than introducing it late as a solution. This commits the paper to defending the distinction's psychological reality before showing why it matters, which is riskier but makes the payoff sections (4-6) much shorter because the conceptual apparatus is already in place.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the claim that observers are systematically better at identifying standing attitudes (section 3), not just that they are sometimes better or better under certain conditions. A cautious version would say first-person and third-person access have different strengths, trading reliability for immediacy. This version says others are superior, full stop, for standing attitudes—which means if subjects ever have better predictive success about their own standing attitudes, the argument fails.
- **Gap.** §4, abductive: The phenomenology of first-person authority and the constitutive relation between judging and avowing must be explained by non-interpretive access rather than by either interpretive access that happens to be fast/reliable or by some non-epistemic feature of avowals like their expressivist function, which requires that there be a genuine epistemic asymmetry at the occurrent level that cannot be reduced to pragmatic or speed differences in the same interpretive process.
- **Arrives at.** §6
- **Load-bearing.** Subjects have non-interpretive privileged access to their occurrent judgments at the moment of avowal because the act of judging-that-p in the deliberative context constitutes rather than reports a psychological state, and this access extends to the phenomenal and memory evidence the subject is currently entertaining, but not to the standing attitudes those judgments may or may not express.

## Outline 5 — The Occurrent Turn: Why We Know Our Judgments But Not Our Beliefs

*Establish that standing attitudes require interpretation for everyone, then show occurrent judgments are accessible only to the subject, then argue this explains both phenomenology and confabulation data better than traditional self-knowledge views*

- **Surprise.** The standard debate assumes first-person authority must concern persisting attitudes to be philosophically interesting, treating occurrent states as too thin to matter. This outline makes the occurrent-standing distinction the central explanatory posit, treating what seemed peripheral as fundamental and inverting what seemed obvious about standing attitudes.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the claim that observers genuinely know our standing attitudes better than we do—not just 'sometimes' or 'in some respects' but systematically and structurally. A cautious version would say we and others have 'different' or 'complementary' access; this says others have superior access where it matters.
- **Gap.** §3, abductive: The phenomenology of judging must reliably track a genuine metaphysical distinction between interpretive and non-interpretive access, rather than being an artifact of processing fluency or another factor that could occur even if all self-knowledge were interpretive.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** When a subject avows a judgment in the moment of forming it, they have non-interpretive access to that occurrent act that observers lack.

## Divergence

**Forced** — every outline does this, so the thesis requires it:

- Distinguish standing attitudes (functional-dispositional patterns across time/contexts) from occurrent judgments (momentary acts of judging)
- Establish that self-ascription of standing attitudes requires interpretation from evidence, not direct access
- Argue that others have superior interpretive access to our standing attitudes because they observe behavioral patterns without motivated reasoning
- Argue that subjects have privileged non-interpretive access to occurrent judgments at the moment of avowal
- Argue that subjects have privileged access to certain evidence used in self-interpretation (memory traces, private feelings)
- State the thesis: the asymmetry is real but inverted, with others superior on standing attitudes and subjects privileged only on occurrent judgments and interpretive evidence

**Free** — the outlines split, so this is yours to choose:

- **how to position the account relative to existing theories** — engage critically with Moran's deliberative stance as mislocating authority in standing attitudes rather than occurrent judgments (outlines 1, 2); treat existing theories as background without extended critical engagement (outlines 0, 3, 4, 5) *(Outlines 1 and 2 dedicate sections to Moran; others mention existing theories only in passing or in resolution sections)*
- **whether to foreground empirical confabulation data** — use confabulation studies as independent evidence that the occurrent/standing divide tracks when self-ascription succeeds versus fails (outlines 2, 5); treat confabulation as something the account explains rather than as independent motivation (outlines 0, 1, 3, 4) *(Outlines 2 and 5 establish confabulation patterns before arguing for the inversion; others use it only in explanatory/resolution sections)*
- **how to sequence the two halves of the inversion** — establish the occurrent/standing distinction first, then show opposite asymmetries for each (outlines 0, 2, 4); establish third-person superiority on standing attitudes first, then establish first-person privilege on occurrent judgments (outlines 1, 3, 5) *(Outlines 0, 2, 4 lead with the distinction itself; outlines 1, 3, 5 lead with the standing-attitude half of the inversion)*
- **whether to include a dedicated resolution/explanation section** — conclude with a section explaining how the inverted asymmetry resolves puzzles (phenomenology plus confabulation data) that other theories cannot (outlines 0, 4, 5); integrate explanatory payoffs throughout without a dedicated resolution section (outlines 1, 2, 3) *(Outlines 0, 4, 5 have final sections showing the account resolves apparent contradictions; outlines 1, 2, 3 end with the thesis statement)*
- **how to characterize what makes occurrent access non-interpretive** — the avowal constitutes the mental act rather than reporting a pre-existing state (outlines 1, 3, 4); the subject undergoes/experiences the occurrent judgment as it happens (outlines 0, 2, 5); both constitution and experiential access (outline 4 most explicitly combines these) *(Outlines 1, 3, 4 emphasize constitution; outlines 0, 2, 5 emphasize experiential presence; outline 4 integrates both)*
- **whether to establish that both self- and other-ascription of standing attitudes are interpretive before arguing for third-person superiority** — first establish interpretive symmetry, then show observers do interpretation better (outlines 3, 5); establish third-person superiority directly without first establishing symmetry (outlines 0, 1, 2, 4) *(Outlines 3 and 5 have separate sections for 'both require interpretation' and 'others interpret better'; others combine these)*

**Sharpest disagreement.** Whether to lead with the occurrent/standing distinction itself or with the standing-attitude half of the inversion. Leading with the distinction (outlines 0, 2, 4) frames the paper as discovering that a familiar category divides into two with opposite epistemic profiles. Leading with third-person superiority on standing attitudes (outlines 1, 3, 5) frames the paper as inverting received wisdom about self-knowledge, with the occurrent domain emerging as the residual space of first-person authority. This determines whether readers experience the thesis as 'the traditional debate was asking about the wrong thing' versus 'the traditional answer was backwards.'

**Absent from all of them.** None of the outlines includes a section defending the functional-dispositional conception of standing attitudes against alternatives (like standing attitudes as stored representations or as higher-order dispositions to form occurrent judgments). This conception is load-bearing—if standing attitudes were introspectable mental states rather than behavioral patterns, the third-person superiority claim would fail—but every outline treats it as established in passing rather than argued for.
