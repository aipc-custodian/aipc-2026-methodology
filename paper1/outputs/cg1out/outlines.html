Outline 0 — Common Ground as Convergence: Two Mechanisms, One Illusion

Show that two families of data previously unified under 'common ground' track different phenomena, then argue that these phenomena arise from independently operating mechanisms whose outputs merely happen to align.

Surprise
The outline places the heaviest burden on section 5's independence claim rather than on the initial data separation in section 2, betting that opponents will grant the empirical distinction but resist the independence of mechanisms. Most common ground theorists can accept that presupposition and coordination are different phenomena while maintaining they draw on a unified resource.
Commitment
This structure commits to a strong cognitive architecture claim about implementation and subsystem independence rather than settling for a merely conceptual or functional distinction; a cautious version would argue only that presupposition and coordination are theoretically distinct kinds of phenomena without claiming they arise from cognitively independent mechanisms.
Gap
§5, abductive — The best explanation for the observed dissociations and functional autonomy between presupposition licensing and collaborative coordination is that they are implemented by genuinely separate mechanisms rather than by a single mechanism with multiple outputs or by mechanisms where one is defined in terms of the other; alternative architectures that preserve unity while accommodating the dissociation data would need to be demonstrably less explanatorily adequate.

Outline 1 — Two Mechanisms, One Name: The Disunity of Common Ground

Show that the two core uses of common ground in linguistic practice satisfy different individuation conditions and respond to different theoretical pressures, then argue this pattern indicates distinct underlying mechanisms rather than aspects of one phenomenon.

Surprise
The outline commits to architectural disunity being demonstrable from functional conflicts alone, without detailed empirical work on underlying mechanisms. This is surprising because philosophy of mind typically treats questions about mechanism individuation as requiring significant empirical input, but here the argument is that functional analysis already decides the case.
Commitment
The outline commits to treating the unity assumption as more than a harmless simplification—as a substantive error that generates explanatory failures. A cautious version would leave open that 'common ground' could still usefully name a family resemblance or a role multiply realized, but this argument denies even that: it insists on calling the convergence 'accidental,' ruling out principled connections.
Gap
§4, abductive — For this inference to go through, functional differences must be the kind that necessitate rather than merely suggest architectural differences, and there must be no unexplored architectural design that could serve both functional profiles.

Surprise
The paper places nearly all its weight on empirical differentials rather than conceptual analysis of 'common ground,' making the case through behavioral patterns rather than the usual route of showing conceptual incoherence in the unified view.
Commitment
The outline commits to the claim that absence of systematic interaction is sufficient for mechanism-separation, rather than hedging by saying the patterns 'may be' distinct or 'appear to require' different treatment—it argues for actual ontological plurality in what has been treated as a unified phenomenon.
Gap
§6, abductive — The differentials established in sections 3 and 4 must be not merely consistent with but best explained by separate mechanisms, and the absence of systematic interaction between the patterns must be sufficient to defeat the default preference for unified theories of linguistic phenomena.

Outline 3 — The Accidental Unity of Common Ground

Establish two independent functional profiles, show they are satisfied by different mechanisms, then demonstrate that standard common ground discourse systematically equivocates between them.

Surprise
The outline makes the critical move at §4 rather than §3: instead of resting on dissociation cases alone, it argues that unification theories face a structural dilemma regardless of how they respond to cases. This shifts the debate from trading intuitions about examples to comparing theoretical costs.
Commitment
This outline commits to defending actual, robust empirical dissociations where presupposition and coordination come apart, not merely possible or hypothetical cases. If the phenomena turn out to be less stable than claimed, the entire argument collapses at §3. Most cautious versions would rely on conceivability arguments or weaker modal claims.
Gap
§5, abductive — The empirical dissociations established in §3 must be best explained by genuine independence of mechanisms rather than by a single mechanism with two modes of operation that happen to dissociate in performance, and the burden of unification established in §4 must make the parsimony cost of positing two mechanisms lower than the explanatory cost of maintaining one.

Outline 5 — Common Ground as Convergence: Two Mechanisms, One Phenomenon

Show that two phenomena systematically diverge where a unitary account predicts they would not, then argue the best explanation is that they are independent mechanisms whose outputs merely converge in standard cases.

Surprise
The outline's architecture commits to showing systematic divergence before explaining it, making the paper vulnerable at section 3: if those cases are eliminable or reanalyzable, everything downstream fails. Most papers arguing for dual mechanisms hedge by presenting the mechanisms first, making the empirical claims defeasible.
Commitment
This outline commits to the claim that convergence between two mechanisms counts as accidental even when it is ecologically ubiquitous and evolutionarily stable—a much stronger claim than merely saying the mechanisms are distinct, and one that invites the objection that ubiquitous, stable convergence is precisely what we should call a unified phenomenon.
Gap
§4, abductive — The inference from systematic divergence to independent mechanisms requires that no plausible unified architecture could generate the divergence patterns—specifically, it requires that context-sensitivity, processing limitations, and selective access cannot explain why the same underlying state would license assertoric presupposition in exactly those cases where it fails to enable planning coordination.

Divergence

Forced — the thesis requires these
  • Distinguish two phenomena currently grouped under 'common ground': one governing presupposition in assertion, another governing collaborative planning coordination
  • Establish that these two phenomena can dissociate or come apart in systematic cases
  • Argue that the dissociation indicates two independent mechanisms rather than one unified phenomenon
  • Conclude that common ground is the accidental convergence of a public commitment tracking mechanism and a shared mental state tracking mechanism

Sharpest disagreement
Whether the argument proceeds primarily through showing that each phenomenon CAN occur without the other (outlines 4, 5) versus showing that they REQUIRE incompatible features (outline 1) versus showing they exhibit DIFFERENT patterns of sensitivity and repair (outlines 2, 3) versus showing they are IMPLEMENTED by distinct cognitive subsystems (outline 0). This determines whether the paper is fundamentally about possibility (dissociation cases), necessity (architectural requirements), behavioral patterns (differential responses), or cognitive architecture (implementation). The choice shapes what counts as decisive evidence and what objections must be addressed.
Absent from all of them
None of the outlines addresses how the two-mechanism view handles intermediate or borderline cases where it's unclear whether we're dealing with assertion or planning, or what happens in contexts where the mechanisms might conflict rather than merely dissociate. Also absent: consideration of developmental or evolutionary questions about why these two mechanisms exist or how they arose.
