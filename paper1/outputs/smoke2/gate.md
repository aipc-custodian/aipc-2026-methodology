### S1. The dispute is unstable because anyone who claims friendship's value is final must either deny that friends are replaceable in principle or grant that final value admits of degrees based on accidental features, but the first option makes final value incoherent (nothing is in principle irreplaceable) and the second collapses final value into derivative value.

- **Move:** instability · **Promise:** 4/5 · **Objection was:** met
- **Attacks tried:** COUNTEREXAMPLE, TRIVIAL, EQUIVOCATION
- **Target:** Finalists like Kolodny or Stroud who want to preserve both the irreplaceability intuition and acknowledgment that some friendships matter more than others
- **Sketch:** The argument turns on this dilemma: if friendship has final value, then either (a) each particular friend has value that makes them in-principle irreplaceable, or (b) friends have final value but some friends realize it better than others based on their qualities. Option (a) is incoherent because it would mean losing a friend creates a literally unfillable gap, but we know people do form new friendships; this treats contingent historical facts (that you met this person) as generating metaphysically necessary value-facts. Option (b) seems to preserve final value while acknowledging degrees, but actually collapses into derivative value: if friend A realizes friendship's value better than friend B because A is wittier, more loyal, etc., then you value A more than B because of these further features—which just is valuing them derivatively. The key premise: genuine final value cannot coherently admit of degree-variation based on accidental features of the valued thing.
- **Appraiser (blind):** Forces finalists into a genuine bind between irreplaceability intuitions and degree-variation that hasn't been cleanly articulated as a dilemma, though the collapse-to-instrumental worry is familiar territory.
- **Best defence:** The defender correctly identified that each killer targets the wrong logical type: the thesis makes a structural claim about value theory that cannot be defeated by concrete cases, is philosophically substantive rather than stipulative, and uses its central term uniformly throughout the argument.

### S2. - **Attacks tried:** PROVES TOO MUCH, COUNTEREXAMPLE, TRIVIAL

- **Sketch:** The debate assumes friendship involves valuing another's good, then disputes whether this valuing is final or grounded. But this presupposes a stable distinction between 'their good' and 'my good.' Friendship actually undermines this distinction: when genuine, it doesn't add 'care about X's wellbeing' to my existing wellbeing function—it reconstructs what my wellbeing consists in. Their setbacks aren't bad for me because I value them finally (final view) or because their wellbeing promotes mine instrumentally (derivative view). Rather, through friendship, their wellbeing becomes partially constitutive of mine in the same way my future wellbeing is. This isn't valuing at all in the sense both sides assume; it's a metaphysical transformation of wellbeing boundaries. The question 'why does their good matter?' presupposes a picture both sides share but friendship actually dissolves.

### S3. Both sides presuppose that a friend's good must matter to you in a single unified way, but recognition of this commitment reveals that friendship essentially involves multiple discrete caring-stances: you have final-value concern for their welfare AND derivative concern grounded in shared history AND instrumental concern for the friendship's continuation, with no deep fact about which is 'the real' ground.

- **Move:** commitment · **Promise:** 4/5 · **Objection was:** met
- **Attacks tried:** TRIVIAL, EQUIVOCATION, ALREADY TAKEN
- **Target:** The shared assumption across the debate that there is a single fact of the matter about how a friend's good matters to you
- **Sketch:** The argument proceeds by showing that phenomenologically, when you care about a friend's good, you're simultaneously doing several things that pull apart under analysis: (1) you care that they flourish, period, in a way that seems impartial to why they're your friend; (2) you care about things mattering to them specifically because of your shared past; (3) you care about their good because their suffering would damage the friendship. The key premise is that these are genuinely distinct caring-stances, not aspects of a single attitude. Ordinarily we don't notice this because they align, but they come apart: when (1) says help them leave you for a better life, (2) resists because it erases what you built together, (3) refuses because it ends the friendship. The debate presupposes we must identify which is 'the' ground, but this presupposition is false—friendship just is maintaining these multiple stances simultaneously. Both derivative and final theorists share the commitment that caring must have a unified architecture.
- **Appraiser (blind):** Challenges a genuine architectural assumption in friendship debates by showing that phenomenologically distinct caring-stances resist unification, though the pluralist move itself has precedent in value theory more broadly.

### D1. The derivative view collapses into the final view because any plausible 'further ground' for friendship's value must itself include essential reference to the particular friend, making the supposed derivation circular.

- **Move:** instability · **Promise:** 4/5 · **Objection was:** conceded
- **Attacks tried:** ALREADY TAKEN, PROVES TOO MUCH, COUNTEREXAMPLE
- **Target:** Derivative theorists who think friendship's value can be grounded in independent goods like eudaimonia or virtue
- **Sketch:** Derivative theorists claim friendship's value derives from goods like virtue cultivation, happiness, or self-knowledge. But examine any candidate ground: if it's 'happiness through friendship,' the happiness is constituted by caring about this person, so we're back to final value. If it's 'virtue cultivation,' which virtues? The other-regarding ones that presuppose the friend matters finally. If it's 'self-knowledge through intimacy,' why does intimacy with this person yield it? Because they matter in a way that makes their perspective revelatory. Every attempted ground smuggles in final valuing of the friend as part of the ground itself. The derivative view therefore cannot maintain its distinctness from the final view—it's either genuinely final value in disguise, or it's circular.
- **Appraiser (blind):** A sophisticated instability argument targeting a live debate in friendship ethics that, if successful, would significantly constrain the theoretical landscape by showing derivative views covertly presuppose what they claim to derive.
- **Best defence:** The defender attempted to distinguish friendship-particularity from instrumental-particularity by arguing that friendship-grounding features must already involve final valuing, but this repeatedly assumed what needed to be proven rather than showing why derivative accounts cannot coherently explain particularity.
- **Flaw:** The thesis assumes that particularity in relationships entails final value, but derivative theorists can coherently claim that particularity-involving relationship-types (including attitudes of treating friends as mattering in themselves) derive their value from eudaimonia—the defender never showed why this explanation is circular rather than merely unfamiliar.

**KILLER (ALREADY TAKEN)** — NOT APPLICABLE

This specific instability argument—that derivative accounts collapse because their grounding goods necessarily smuggle in final valuing of the particular friend—does not appear as a recognized position in the friendship literature. While Aristotle distinguished character friendship from utility/pleasure friendships, and while contemporary virtue ethicists like Annas and Hursthouse discuss how philia relates to eudaimonia, none argue that *derivative* views are inherently *unstable* in this particular way. The Aristotelian tradition actually maintains the opposite: that friendship's value successfully derives from the friend's virtue and the relationship's contribution to flourishing without collapsing into final value. Helm and Kolodny debate whether concern for friends is grounded or basic, but neither frames this as an instability within derivative views themselves. This thesis identifies a novel dialectical pressure point rather than restating an established position.

**KILLER (PROVES TOO MUCH)** — The argument proves too much because it would equally show that *all* instrumental or derivative value claims are impossible.

Consider: "Exercise derives its value from health." The objector could say: "But which health benefits ground exercise's value? Cardiovascular fitness that presupposes you care about your heart, strength that presupposes you value your muscles, longevity that presupposes you care about your future self. Every attempted ground smuggles in final valuing of yourself as part of the ground itself!" 

Or: "Education derives value from career success." Response: "But career success for *you* — which presupposes you matter finally. The ground smuggles in final self-valuing!"

The pattern applies universally: whenever X derives value from its contribution to Y for some person P, we can claim the value of "Y-for-P" presupposes P's final value. This would eliminate the entire category of derivative/instrumental value.

Yet derivative value claims are clearly coherent. Exercise *can* derive value from health contributions without circularity. The thesis's reasoning pattern, if sound, would prove that nothing ever has genuinely derivative value — an absurd conclusion that undermines the very distinction between final and derivative value that friendship theorists are trying to deploy.

**DEFENDER** — BEST DEFENCE

The objection misidentifies what makes the friendship case special. The thesis is not claiming that *all* derivative value collapses—it's claiming that friendship's value cannot be derivative *because of the particularity requirement*.

Exercise derives value from health, full stop. It doesn't matter which treadmill, which gym, which shoes. The derivation works because health is an impersonal good that exercise generically promotes. Any adequate cardiovascular activity will do. There's no essential reference to *this particular* exercise session mattering in a non-substitutable way.

But friendship is constitutively particular. The thesis's point is that when you try to ground friendship's value in happiness, virtue cultivation, or self-knowledge, the ground *itself* must explain why *this particular person* matters non-substitutably. And that's where the circularity enters.

Take the virtue cultivation example: friendship helps me develop other-regarding virtues. But which virtues? Loyalty, care, appropriate partiality. Now ask: what makes these virtues? They're dispositions to treat *particular others* as mattering finally, not as fungible instruments. So the supposed ground (virtue) already contains final valuing of particular persons as a constituent. You can't derive "valuing this friend finally" from "cultivating virtues that consist in valuing friends finally" without circularity.

The exercise case has no parallel structure. Health doesn't constitutively involve treating particular exercises as finally valuable. But eudaimonia achieved *through friendship* does constitutively involve treating particular friends as finally valuable—otherwise it's not friendship-eudaimonia but something else.

The thesis identifies a genuine structural difference: derivative value works when the ground is genuinely independent of particularity. Friendship cannot meet this condition because the grounds offered by derivative theorists already presuppose what they're meant to derive.

VERDICT

The defence fails, though it clarifies what's at stake.

The problem is that the defence conflates two different things: (1) whether the friend is *replaceable* in the relationship, and (2) whether the *value of the relationship* can be grounded in something independent.

The Aristotelian derivative theorist can grant that character friendship involves non-substitutable particularity while maintaining that this entire package—including the particularity—derives its value from eudaimonia. The friend's irreplaceability is a *feature* of the kind of relationship that promotes flourishing, not a sign that we're already treating them as finally valuable.

Consider: a virtuoso violin has non-substitutable particularity for the musician. This specific instrument, with its unique voice, cannot be replaced without loss. But this doesn't mean the violin has final value—its value still derives from music-making. The particularity is real, but derivative.

Similarly, the Aristotelian can say: this particular friend, with their specific virtues and history with me, is irreplaceable in the eudaimonic relationship we share. But the value of the whole arrangement—particularity included—derives from flourishing. The thesis hasn't shown why acknowledging particularity *in the ground* makes the grounding circular rather than just accurate about what kind of relationship promotes eudaimonia.

The defence fails because it assumes particularity entails final value, which is precisely what derivative theorists deny.

**KILLER (COUNTEREXAMPLE)** — **COUNTEREXAMPLE**

**Weak premise:** "any plausible 'further ground' for friendship's value must itself include essential reference to the particular friend"

**Counterexample:** Shared activity theorists like Nehamas argue friendship's value derives from *joint projects and shared activities* that create a "we" perspective. The ground is the collaborative activity structure itself—writing a book together, running a business, raising children, pursuing scientific research.

Consider two scientists who become friends through decades of collaboration. The derivative theorist says: their friendship's value derives from the shared intellectual project. This ground does NOT essentially reference the particular friend in the problematic way. What matters is having *a* collaborator with complementary skills, sustained commitment, and intellectual chemistry. Yes, after 30 years they're irreplaceable to each other—but that's an *effect* of the valuable collaboration, not smuggled into the ground.

The thesis claims we must ask "why does intimacy with *this person* yield self-knowledge?" and answer "because they matter finally." But the activity theorist answers: "because we've built collaborative structures, shared languages, and joint commitments that make this particular configuration valuable"—without treating the person as finally valuable, only the activity-structure as valuable.

The ground (joint activity promoting flourishing through collaboration) is genuinely independent. The particularity emerges from contingent history, not from final valuing smuggled into the derivation. The thesis fails.

**DEFENDER** — # BEST DEFENCE

The objection misunderstands what "essential reference to the particular friend" means in the thesis. The shared activity case actually *demonstrates* rather than refutes the circularity problem.

Consider the scientists more carefully. The objection says the ground is "collaborative activity structure" and "having *a* collaborator with complementary skills." But this describes a *role* that could be filled by various people—it's the utility friendship model, not genuine friendship. If the value really derives from "having a complementary collaborator," then when a better collaborator appears, you should switch. The friendship would be replaceable in principle.

But the objection immediately retreats: "after 30 years they're irreplaceable to each other." This irreplaceability is supposedly just an "effect" of collaboration. But what makes it an effect rather than constitutive? The claim must be that the accumulated history creates particularity. Yet what kind of history? Not just *any* shared activities—countless colleagues share projects without becoming irreplaceable friends. It's shared activities *undertaken with mutual concern for each other as particular persons*.

Here's the circularity: The activity theorist needs to explain why *these particular* collaborative structures matter non-substitutably. If it's just because they're effective at producing good outcomes, we're back to utility friendship (replaceable). If it's because they're constituted by caring about *this person's* contributions, perspectives, and flourishing, then we've smuggled in final valuing of the particular person into the supposedly independent ground.

The "shared languages" and "joint commitments" the objection mentions aren't person-neutral structures. They're languages *with this person*, commitments *to this person*. The very features that make the collaboration friendship-constituting rather than mere professional partnership already involve treating the other as mattering in themselves, not just for the outputs.

The thesis's point stands: you cannot specify what makes collaborative activity *friendship-grounding* rather than mere utility-grounding without already including final valuing of the particular person in your specification of the ground. The activity view either describes replaceable professional relationships (not friendship) or smuggles in particularity that presupposes final value.

# VERDICT

The defence fails. It commits precisely the hedging error it's supposed to avoid.

The problem is that the defence keeps insisting we need to distinguish "real friendship" from "utility friendship" or "mere professional partnership," then claims only the former involves final valuing. But this is exactly what derivative theorists deny. The Aristotelian account *is* supposed to explain character friendship—the irreplaceable, particular kind—and it claims this *entire phenomenon* derives value from eudaimonia without any final valuing.

The defence's move—"shared languages *with this person*, commitments *to this person*"—doesn't establish that final valuing is smuggled in. It just restates that friendship involves particularity. The derivative theorist agrees! Their claim is that this particularity-involving relationship, as a whole, has derivative value.

Consider the violin analogy from the previous round more carefully. The virtuoso has "a relationship *with this instrument*," has developed "shared languages" (muscle memory, knowledge of its quirks), has "commitments *to this instrument*" (maintains it, insures it, refuses to sell it). All the particularity is there. Yet the value clearly derives from music-making.

The defence tries to block this by claiming friendship-particularity is different because it involves "treating the other as mattering in themselves." But that's question-begging. The derivative theorist says: I treat my friend as mattering in themselves *because that attitude is constitutive of the relationship-type that promotes eudaimonia*. The attitude is real, the particularity is real, but the value of having-that-attitude-toward-that-person still derives from flourishing.

The thesis needs to show why this explanation is *impossible* or *circular*, not just assert that particularity entails final value. The counterexample stands: shared activity grounds can specify friendship-constituting features (including particularity and non-replaceability) without smuggling in final value, only derivative value of a particular-involving relationship-type.
