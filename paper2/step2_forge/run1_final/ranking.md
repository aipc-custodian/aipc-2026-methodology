# The leaders, in rank order

Every thesis scored 4 or 5 for promise, put in a strict order by ranking it against others in small shuffled batches. The index is its position in raw.json, which is what --index takes downstream.

| 1 | 0 | 4 | dead | 4 | The disagreement among denial, proxy, fictionalist, and attributing views is not about the conditions for performing a speech act. It is about which entity in the LLM stack counts as the speaker (token-level sample, trained policy, deployed persona, or sociotechnical system). Once the speaker is fixed, the rival camps largely agree on the verdicts. |
| 2 | 14 | 4 | dead | 4 | The dispute over whether machines assert is a dispute about whether illocutionary acts are constituted on the hearer's side (uptake and functional role) or the speaker's side (accountable commitment), and LLMs matter only as the first case where these systematically come apart. |
| 3 | 47 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The dispute assumes that a speech act occurs only if some agent performs it, so that the options are 'LLM performs', 'human proxy performs' or 'no one performs'. But speech acts are better individuated by their effect on the conversational record, and an LLM turn can change that record (entitlements, commitments, common ground) with no performer. 'No speech act' and 'speech act by someone' are therefore not exhaustive. |
| 4 | 3 | 4 | wounded | 3 | The intention-based denials of LLM speech acts mistake what Austin classed as an abuse (a hollow but performed act) for a misfire (an unperformed act), so the live question is whether LLM output meets Austin's A/B procedural conditions, which is a question about status and not about mind. |
| 5 | 17 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The dispute over whether chatbots assert is unsettled because the parties individuate the putative speaker at different levels (the trained model, the deployed product, the conversation-bound persona), and Butlin and Viebahn's sanction condition is met only at the persona level, which none of the standard options targets. |
| 6 | 6 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Whether an artificial system can perform a given speech act depends on whether that act type's felicity conditions require a speaker that persists until the act is satisfied. Assertion and advice need no persisting speaker, but promising and apologising do, so the current positions (quasi, proxy, proto, denial) cannot each be correct across act types. |
| 7 | 16 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The assertion debate is individuated at the wrong grain. Speech acts are token acts, but a chatbot's reply is a draw from a policy. The live question is whether assertion can attach to the policy (what the model reliably would say) rather than to the sampled token, and on that question the cases for denial and for proto-assertion come apart from where they are now placed. |
| 8 | 31 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The assumption that a speech-act intention exists prior to or alongside output, which underlies both Gubelmann's malfunction-versus-deliberate test and Attah's Levelt-style planning template, is false even of human speakers. Much human illocution is incremental, with the force of the act settled by retrospective ownership, so the human-versus-LLM difference cannot be located in the presence of an intention. |
| 9 | 39 | 4 | dead | 4 | Identical LLM computation can be the Formulator of a human's communicative intention in one deployment and the whole speaker in another, so speakerhood is not an intrinsic functional property of the system, and intrinsic-property views such as Attah's control conception, Gubelmann's autonomy and Butlin's agency criterion cannot settle the question. |
| 10 | 7 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Both the literalist and the relocating camps grant that a speech act's force is fixed by the psychology of some producer, whether the model or its principal. Cases in which a chatbot's output was held to bind a deployer with no content-specific intention show that force can be fixed by the normative practice of adoption and reliance, with no producer-side intention at all. |
| 11 | 8 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The fiction in chatbot speech sits on the production side, not the consumption side. A model completing an assistant-persona transcript is in the position of an author writing a character's dialogue. Searle's pretended illocutions are therefore the right base case, and users' engagement with chatbots need not be fictional at all. |
| 12 | 10 | 4 | wounded | 3 | Even if an LLM passes Gubelmann's intention test, that gives it no illocutionary authorship, because secretaries, scribes and interpreters pass the same test for the very utterances they produce and are not the speakers of the speech acts those utterances carry. |
| 13 | 11 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Butlin's agency criterion tracks sensitivity to instrumental value, but the speech acts at issue (assertion, apology, promising) are constituted by non-instrumental norms, so there is a distinction between instrumental agency and norm-responsive agency. A system can be a full agent in Butlin's sense and still be unable to perform these acts in its own right, because for such a system every norm-conforming output is only a means to its learned objective. |
| 14 | 21 | 4 | wounded | 3 | The proxy account's assumption that the principal of a machine utterance is its designer or deployer is false in the central case: when a user adopts a chatbot's output, the user is the speaker, and the model functions as part of the user's own cognitive apparatus. |
| 15 | 33 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The debate is about production-constitution (a speech act exists in virtue of facts about the speaker), whereas the relational picture suggests uptake-constitution (illocutionary force is fixed in the hearer's securing of uptake). These two give opposite classifications of fictionalism and of the 'text that humans interpret as speech acts' deflationary option. |
| 16 | 13 | 4 | wounded | 3 | Gubelmann's Kantian criterion of organismhood, on which parts are reciprocally means and ends and the whole organises and maintains itself, does not fix a level of attribution. Applied to maintained human–LLM pipelines it makes the LLM an organ of a larger goal-setting system. This turns 'LLMs cannot speak' into the trivial claim that organs do not speak, and it yields a speech-act author that is neither the LLM nor a human proxy. |
| 17 | 1 | 5 | wounded | 4 | If any chatbot output is an assertion, the asserted content is not what a sampled token sequence says but what the model's output distribution commits to. The token-level framing used by every side is mistaken, and the 'no stable commitment' objection to chatbot assertion misfires. |
| 18 | 46 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The sanction condition on assertion fails for LLMs because of a grain mismatch, not because LLMs cannot be sanctioned: sanctions (RLHF, deprecation, retraining) land on the weights-level policy, while the candidate performer of any given assertion is the conversation-bounded instance. A persistent-identity architecture would therefore satisfy the condition with no change in 'agency' in anyone's sense. |
| 19 | 25 | 4 | wounded | 3 | Reflexive and habitual speech acts, such as the 'sorry' after bumping into someone, the 'thanks' at a door, or a 'yes' given on autopilot, are full speech acts with no prior or accompanying communicative intention, so the premise that speech acts require communicative intention, which Attah and his Bender-style opponents share, is false, and the dispute should move to standing and answerability. |
| 20 | 38 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Sanctionability does not test whether a model is a speaker but fixes which entity is the candidate speaker, and that entity is a persona-lineage, neither the model nor the deployer, so the model-versus-proxy dichotomy is false. |
| 21 | 20 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Whether a chatbot output is an assertion is not settled at the time of output by the system's states or functions; it is conferred by what the system later does with the output, so the same token string can be an assertion or a non-assertion depending on its continuation. |
| 22 | 35 | 4 | duplicate of an attacked thesis |  | Much of the disagreement over whether 'the LLM' performs speech acts is verbal, because 'the LLM' picks out different candidate speakers in different camps (weights, a running inference process, a persona, a deployed product, the vendor), and each camp's verdict is correct for its own referent. |
| 23 | 2 | 4 | wounded | 3 | Proxy accounts of LLM speech acts confuse two grains of principal objective, objectives about this content and objectives about a distribution of contents, and only the former can ground a proxy speech act, so the proxy view survives only for canned output and collapses into the no-speech-act view for open-ended generation. |
| 24 | 5 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Ratification cases show that the speech act a model's output counts as can be fixed after the output, by a human's later adoption of it. So the shared presupposition that the question 'is this output a speech act, and whose?' has an answer at the time of output is false. |
| 25 | 18 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Whether an intermediate status between assertion and non-assertion is available depends on whether the speech act's norms are regulative of a pre-existing behaviour (assertion, advising) or constitutive of the behaviour (promising, apologising). Proto-assertion therefore has no analogue for most of the speech acts that the literature treats alongside assertion. |
| 26 | 24 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The debate over LLM communicative intentions presupposes that an intention precedes and explains the utterance (Levelt's Conceptualizer before Formulator). For LLMs the only persistent state that carries speaker commitment is the public transcript, so the question is really whether intentions can be constituted by discourse record, which makes Attah and the normative deniers disagree less than they appear to. |
| 27 | 30 | 4 | unattacked |  | Barandiaran and Almendros's own category of tasks that are 'purpose-structured and purpose-bounded' but not purposeful gives illocutionary force everything it needs, so the whole intention-and-autonomy debate is aimed at the wrong determinant of speech-act identity. |
| 28 | 42 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Because Butlin and Viebahn ground the sanctionability condition in assertion's being a norm-governed social practice, they are committed to assertoric status being partly response-dependent: if users and institutions sustainably treat chatbots as targets of reproach, correction and retraining, that practice helps make the sanctionability condition true, so whether chatbots assert is partly up to us and is not a fact to be read off the models. |
| 29 | 12 | 4 | wounded | 3 | The human cases that motivate proxy accounts (ambassadors, spokespeople, secretaries) are doubling proxies, where the delegate performs a speech act of their own in addition to the principal's. Machine proxy accounts need a conduit proxy, where the device performs none. The two are different relations, and the proxy view borrows its plausibility from the first while asserting the second. |
| 30 | 37 | 4 | wounded | 2 | The proxy account conflates two relations, endorsing a content and endorsing a generating policy, and only the second is available for LLM output, so proxy 'assertion' is a different act from assertion and is governed by a reliability norm. |
| 31 | 27 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Barandiaran and Almendros's middle category, 'purpose-structured but not purposeful', runs together the illocutionary point of an act type and the purpose of a speaker. Speech-act performance requires only the former, so their own characterisation of LLMs is a description of the kind of system that can perform speech acts of its own. |
| 32 | 9 | 4 | wounded | 4 | Gubelmann's mechanism/organism contrast sorts systems by the wrong axis: what matters is whether a system's purposes were stipulated by a designer's intention or produced by selection, and on that axis LLMs align with organisms against artefacts like the coffee machine, which also undermines proxy accounts. |
| 33 | 29 | 4 | unattacked |  | The individuality condition used to deny LLMs agency and speakerhood is posed at the wrong grain. Speaker-individuality (a locus of accumulating, retrievable commitments within a dialogue thread) is distinct from the metabolic, self-producing individuality of enactivism, and LLM threads can have the first without the second. |
| 34 | 41 | 4 | unattacked |  | The shared assumption that a machine utterance is either the machine's assertion or a proxy assertion by some human is false: there are orphan outputs that no human has adopted, authorised or even seen, which satisfy the conditions for being content that asserting would regulate and yet have no asserter. |
| 35 | 45 | 4 | unattacked |  | The agency requirement for apology is a stand-in for exposure to cost, so what blocks chatbot apologies is that they are cheap talk and not that they lack intentions or moral agency, and the dispute over agency is therefore about the wrong variable. |
| 36 | 15 | 4 | wounded | 3 | There are two kinds of proxy assertion, relayed and generative. In relayed proxy assertion the principal has a determinate content they could believe or know. In generative proxy assertion they do not. LLM output is almost entirely generative, and for generative proxies the assertoric norms cannot be applied to the principal. |
| 37 | 19 | 4 | wounded | 4 | The proxy view implies that the same token output can be one speech act, by a different speaker and with a different force, depending on facts about system prompts, fine-tuning and who deployed it that the hearer cannot access, and so it violates the uptake or publicity condition that makes something a speech act in the Austin–Strawson–Grice tradition on which Arora builds. |
| 38 | 28 | 4 | unattacked |  | Hybrid ('midtended') human–LLM speech should be analysed by distinguishing standing proxy, where the designer speaks through the system (Green and Michel, Arora), from adoptive utterance, where the user retrospectively makes an LLM output their own by ratifying and deploying it.
