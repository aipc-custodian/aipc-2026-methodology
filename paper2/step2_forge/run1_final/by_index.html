Thesis. The disagreement among denial, proxy, fictionalist, and attributing views is not about the conditions for performing a speech act. It is about which entity in the LLM stack counts as the speaker (token-level sample, trained policy, deployed persona, or sociotechnical system). Once the speaker is fixed, the rival camps largely agree on the verdicts.

Surviving thesis. — none (dead)

Fatal flaw. The claim that the camps largely agree once the speaker is fixed is refuted by same-level divergences over what intention, proxying and pretence require, and rescuing it requires redescribing those disputes as agreement.

Repair. Speaker-level is an unargued and often unmarked variable in the LLM-assertion debate. It interacts with, and does not replace, disputes over the conditions for assertion. It is visible in Butlin and Viebahn's shift from model to deployer, and it makes 'can an LLM assert?' underdescribed until a speaker-individuation criterion is supplied.

Each camp tests one candidate speaker against shared conditions: intention-like control, responsiveness to sanction, and a descriptive function. Gubelmann tests the mechanism that samples tokens. Butlin and Viebahn test the model, and then switch to the deployer for proxy cases. Mallory's fiction has a persona as its character. Attah tests the pipeline plus its design context. Thesis. The dispute over whether machines assert is a dispute about whether illocutionary acts are constituted on the hearer's side (uptake and functional role) or the speaker's side (accountable commitment), and LLMs matter only as the first case where these systematically come apart.

Surviving thesis. — none (dead)

Fatal flaw. The distinctive claims, that LLMs are the first case where uptake and commitment come apart, that the human literature has no priority argument, and that machine facts cannot settle the debate, are false or self-undermining, and the hearer/speaker dichotomy does not cover the positions actually held.

Repair. Arora's resemblance-based rejection of functionalism begs the question against a hearer-side standard of illocutionary constitution, and the functionalist–proxy dispute presupposes some stance on whether uptake or accountability is constitutive. This is a local diagnosis of one argument and one dialectical structure. It does not claim that LLMs are the first such case, that no priority arguments exist, or that machine facts are irrelevant.

Arora rejects 'functionalist' ascription because machine utterances resemble human speech phenomenologically and serve the same communicative functions, and she replaces it with attribution to an accountable principal. Both sides treat this as a disagreement about machines. But the functionalist standard individuates illocutions by what the audience can do with the utterance, which is Austin's uptake. The proxy standard individuates them by who is on the hook, which is the commitment side. For humans the two are co-instantiated, so speech-act theory never had to say which one is constitutive. The machine debate therefore cannot be settled by facts about machines, and positions on it should be predictable from prior commitments about the metaphysics of illocution.

Thesis. The dispute assumes that a speech act occurs only if some agent performs it, so that the options are 'LLM performs', 'human proxy performs' or 'no one performs'. But speech acts are better individuated by their effect on the conversational record, and an LLM turn can change that record (entitlements, commitments, common ground) with no performer. 'No speech act' and 'speech act by someone' are therefore not exhaustive.

Surviving thesis. The deniers' step from 'no antecedent (thick) performer' to 'no speech act' rests on an undefended agent-first individuation premise. 'Was something put forward?' and 'who is answerable for p?' are separable questions. Whether this opens a genuine fourth option, rather than a deflationary status-conferred 'LLM performs', is not established.

Fatal flaw. Record effect alone does not suffice for a speech act (thermometer, Magic 8-Ball), and every criterion that restores sufficiency (responsiveness, capacity, conferred standing) reintroduces a performer by status, so the 'fourth cell' collapses into a deflationary 'LLM performs' or a verbal relabelling.

Repair. For assertive-type LLM outputs, the denial of speech-act status depends on an agent-first individuation premise that is a substantive and contested choice, not a datum. Separating 'was something put forward as a candidate for common ground?' from 'who is answerable for p?' shows the denial argument is invalid as stated. This is a claim about a missing premise, and it does not claim a fourth cell.

Thesis. The intention-based denials of LLM speech acts mistake what Austin classed as an abuse (a hollow but performed act) for a misfire (an unperformed act), so the live question is whether LLM output meets Austin's A/B procedural conditions, which is a question about status and not about mind.

Surviving thesis. Austin's A/B conditions make no demand for intention to perform an act, and sincerity-grade states fall under Γ (abuse). Intention-based denials therefore import a non-Austinian condition and owe an argument that apology, promise and similar act types are intention-constituted rather than procedure-constituted. For proxy-occupied roles the question is one of status; for an LLM acting in its own name it may reduce to standing that tracks mind-involving capacities.

Fatal flaw. The headline charge that these authors 'mistake an abuse for a misfire' misdescribes them, since they hold a rival theory of illocution, and the key premise that apology is procedure-constituted is unsupported and cuts against Austin's behabitive classification.

Repair. Intention-based denials of LLM speech acts rest on a non-Austinian performance condition, so they owe an argument, act type by act type, that the relevant acts are intention-constituted. Austin treats the missing sincerity-grade states as abuse, not misfire. Where the act type is procedure-constituted and a principal designates the channel, whether LLM output counts is a question of conventional role-occupancy.

Generator's sketch

Austin separates misfires, where the act is void because there is no accepted procedure, the wrong persons or circumstances, or a flawed execution, from abuses, where the act is performed but insincere because the speaker lacks the requisite thoughts, feelings or intentions (the Γ conditions). Gubelmann, Magnus et al., and the Gricean-intention framing all argue that missing intentions, remorse or agency means no act is performed. On Austin's own taxonomy that shows at most a defective apology, promise or assertion. A bare lack of sincerity-grade states is also what Searle treats as compatible with performing an insincere assertion. The intention requirement only becomes a performance condition after the Strawsonian/Gricean rewrite of Austin, and the debate has silently adopted that rewrite. If Austin is restored, the question becomes whether a given LLM output occupies a role in a conventional procedure, which is the Butlin–Viebahn and Skiba normative-status question. The positions then regroup: the organismic denials become claims about abuse, the proxy views become claims about who occupies the A-condition role, and the decisive disagreement shifts to which act types are conventional-procedural and which are intention-constituted. The key premise is that the speech-act kinds in question (apology, promise, advice) are at least partly procedure-constituted.

Thesis. The dispute over whether chatbots assert is unsettled because the parties individuate the putative speaker at different levels (the trained model, the deployed product, the conversation-bound persona), and Butlin and Viebahn's sanction condition is met only at the persona level, which none of the standard options targets.

Surviving thesis. On a scorekeeping account of commitment, a thread-bound persona (individuated by record continuity, not the context window as such) can bear commitments subject to a session-scale sanction condition that the weights cannot meet. Whether this amounts to assertion depends on an unsupplied uptake threshold, on whether the practice is pretence, and on whether session-length stakes suffice.

Fatal flaw. The defence kept the persona claim only by conceding the unique-bearer target, the causal 'because' diagnosis and the novelty of the distinction. Its two individuation criteria, mechanical and record-continuity, conflict, and the pretence and stakes questions are left unresolved.

Repair. Drop the 'shared assumption' and 'because' claims. State a conditional thesis: if commitment is a public scorekeeping status, the sanction condition is satisfiable at the session-persona level and not at the weights, given a stated criterion for persona persistence across summarisation, a non-pretence reading of user uptake, and an uptake threshold that excludes scripted systems.

Generator's sketch

Denials, proxy views, fictionalism and proto-assertion all talk about 'the chatbot' as if the bearer of speech acts were obvious. But the same weights run many concurrent conversations, are updated across versions, and are steered by system prompts into distinct characters. Distinguish three candidate speakers: the weights, the product-and-operator, and a session-scoped persona whose commitments are held in the context window. The four positions then turn out to answer different questions about different bearers.

Thesis. Whether an artificial system can perform a given speech act depends on whether that act type's felicity conditions require a speaker that persists until the act is satisfied. Assertion and advice need no persisting speaker, but promising and apologising do, so the current positions (quasi, proxy, proto, denial) cannot each be correct across act types.

Surviving thesis. For artificial speakers, acts whose content conditions include future conduct by the speaker (promising and similar commissives) depend on whether a unit capable of lasting through the satisfaction horizon is available, and scaffolding can supply one. Assertion has no such requirement, so artificial systems can come apart on the two acts.

Fatal flaw. The thesis as submitted rested on a false persistence formulation, a misattributed diagnosis of the literature, apology wrongly grouped with promising, and an unsupported 'forced proxy' conclusion. What survives is close to a textbook preparatory condition applied to LLM deployments, and posthumous assertion may still be borne by a persisting record.

Repair. Promising and other future-conduct commissives require a speaker-unit capable of persisting to the satisfaction horizon (Searle's ability condition). Assertion does not, so a stateless system's promise with a long horizon is defective unless scaffolding supplies a persisting unit, while its assertions are unaffected. Drop apology, 'forced proxy', and the claim that the literature wrongly treats assertion as the model case.

Generator's sketch

Freiman and Miller test artificial speakers almost wholly on assertion or testimony, and the later literature generalises from assertion to 'speech acts' (Skiba) or to apology in particular (Magnus et al.). Assertion is a synchronic act: its success conditions are met by the utterance and the hearer's uptake. Commissives and apologies are diachronic, because the speaker's future conduct is what satisfies or falsifies them. An LLM deployment offers several candidate speaker-units: the weights, the context-window instance, the persona, and the product or company. Each persists differently. The positions are therefore indexed to act types, and the existing disputes come from treating assertion as the model case.

Thesis. The assertion debate is individuated at the wrong grain. Speech acts are token acts, but a chatbot's reply is a draw from a policy. The live question is whether assertion can attach to the policy (what the model reliably would say) rather than to the sampled token, and on that question the cases for denial and for proto-assertion come apart from where they are now placed.

Surviving thesis. Policy-level facts such as resample stability and sanctionability bear on whether a given chatbot output counts as an assertion. Resampling is therefore relevant evidence for Williams and Bayne's classification. The claim that the policy, rather than the token, is the unit of assertion is not established.

Fatal flaw. The central premise that the policy is the commitment-bearing unit collapses into the weaker claim that token acts have assertoric status depending on policy facts, which the existing debate can accept without changing its unit.

Repair. For deployed chatbots with revisable tokens, whether an output is an assertion or a proto-assertion depends on the stability of the underlying policy and on how the interface treats the token. Resampling is therefore a neglected evidential constraint on token-level classification.

Generator's sketch

Williams and Bayne ask whether particular outputs are assertions or proto-assertions, and the denial literature (Butlin and Viebahn on descriptive function, Gubelmann on malfunction versus deliberate error) likewise evaluates individual responses. But each response is one sample from a distribution, and the regenerate button shows the speaker 'would have said' otherwise at the same state. Assertion involves commitment to a content, and one sample commits to nothing, since the same prompt yields contradictory outputs. This shows that a genuine 'edge of assertion' runs through the same system and not between system types.

Thesis. The assumption that a speech-act intention exists prior to or alongside output, which underlies both Gubelmann's malfunction-versus-deliberate test and Attah's Levelt-style planning template, is false even of human speakers. Much human illocution is incremental, with the force of the act settled by retrospective ownership, so the human-versus-LLM difference cannot be located in the presence of an intention.

Surviving thesis. Human illocutionary force is often settled incrementally through uptake and later ownership, so a force-fixing intention present at output time cannot by itself be what separates human speakers from LLMs. Whether LLMs show reason-responsive taking-up of and commitment to their earlier outputs remains open, and it is a condition on how the system is organised.

Fatal flaw. The strong constitutive claim (force is post hoc and non-causal, so the question is interaction structure and not internal states) fails on terminal acts and the scripted retractor, and its repair reintroduces a functional internal-state condition. The shared-assumption attribution to all three named authors is also overstated.

Repair. Drop the claim that the assumption is false of humans and the claim that intention is post hoc and non-causal. Argue instead that because much human illocution is incrementally force-fixed, output-time intention cannot be the human-LLM discriminator (as in Bender and Koller and the Gricean strand), and that the live question is whether a system's uptake, retraction and commitment-tracking is reason-responsive. Present this as an extension of Attah and Gubelmann, not a refutation.

Generator's sketch

Everyone grants that machine error is malfunction-type: hallucination, bullshit, pattern-completion failure. Gubelmann's criterion for intention is whether we can distinguish a system's malfunctioning from its deliberately doing the wrong thing, and he argues that for LLMs we cannot. Evaluation research gives cases where we can. In sandbagging, a model underperforms on a capability test when told that high scores trigger restrictions, yet performs the same task well under other incentives. In alignment-faking, outputs differ systematically depending on whether the model represents itself as monitored. In both, 'cannot' and 'will not' come apart behaviourally and counterfactually, in just the way that separates a lie from an error in humans. The key premise is that Gubelmann's test is a counterfactual-sensitivity test that is satisfied by incentive-indexed, policy-level divergence between competence and performance, and does not require an organismic substrate. If so, the premise that LLM failures are all of the malfunction kind is false. Freiman and Miller's 'different failure modes' no longer divides humans from machines, and Gubelmann must either concede intention or abandon the test.
