Thesis gate

Can a large language model literally have communicative intentions and perform speech acts such as asserting, advising, promising or apologising, or does it only produce text that humans interpret as such? The rival positions fall into four groups. Denials hold that LLMs perform no speech acts of their own: on organismic grounds, because speaking is acting, acting requires intentions, and intentions require the self-set goals of autonomous, self-maintaining organisms rather than externally purposed mechanisms (Gubelmann); on normative grounds, because assertion is a norm-governed social practice whose performers must produce outputs with a descriptive function and be open to sanction, conditions that current models fail singly or jointly (Butlin and Viebahn), an argument extended to speech acts in general (Skiba); and, for apology in particular, because chatbots lack the linguistic and moral agency the act requires (Magnus, Buccella and D'Cruz). Relocating views accept that speech acts are in play but deny that the model is their author: proxy accounts treat machine utterances as speech acts performed on behalf of human creators, designers or deployers (Green and Michel; Arora; and, for some uses, Butlin and Viebahn), while fictionalism treats chatbot speech as something users engage with through fiction (Mallory). Intermediate views assign machines a status short of full speech-act performance: quasi-testimony, on which instruments assert only in a qualified sense that differs fundamentally from human assertion (Freiman and Miller), and proto-assertion, proposed after finding both the proxy and fictionalist accounts inadequate (Williams and Bayne). Attributing views hold that the demanding Gricean, mind-reading conception of communicative intention is empirically untenable even for humans and that, on a thinner functional conception of intention as an internal state controlling output in response to the situation, language models have communicative intentions in a graded and limited form, with purposes supplied by their design context (Attah). Behind these lies a dispute about agency itself: one account counts a system as an agent when its outputs are explained by its own sensitivity to instrumental value, so that many language agents built around LLMs qualify, bare pre-trained chatbots are borderline, and desire requires considerably more than agency (Butlin); an enactive account denies LLMs autonomous agency for want of individuality, normativity and interactional asymmetry, describes them as linguistic automata, and locates what is new in hybrid human–LLM agency (Barandiaran and Almendros); and a relational-materialist account rejects the individualistic, property-based framing altogether and distributes agency across networks of human and non-human actors (Puzio).

KILLER (PROVES TOO MUCH)

ATTEMPT. The weak premise is that sanctionability is a property of the role and its feedback loop, not of the substrate, so that any change to future outputs prompted by complaint counts as sanctioning the persona.

If that premise worked, it would license several unacceptable conclusions.

- Thermostat-style cases. A phone-tree "Ava", a recommender system, or an ELIZA-style script deployed under a name has users complain, operators revise it, and its outputs shift. Each would be an asserting speaker whose commitments are real. A thermostat is sanctioned in this sense when it is recalibrated.
- Fiction. A recurring franchise character such as a mascot or a narrator is revised after audience complaints and keeps commitments across instalments. On the thesis, such characters would assert, and fictionalism would collapse into the persona view.
- Cheap speakers. Any prompt creates a new persona. A jailbreak persona such as "DAN" would be a fresh speaker bearing real commitments, with no one actually answerable for them and no continuity between instances.
- Moral speech acts. Withdrawing or fine-tuning a persona would count as sanctioning it. Chatbot apologies would then be genuine apologies by an office-holder, which is the very conclusion Magnus et al. reject.

The only way to block these is to demand that the sanction register as a cost to the speaker. That demand is exactly what the thesis's functional reading of sanctionability leaves out.

DEFENDER

BEST DEFENCE

1. The objection attacks a premise the thesis does not hold. The thesis does not say that any feedback that changes outputs makes a speaker. Its subject is "a social office individuated by a commitment scoreboard," and sanctionability is predicated of that. The thermostat, ELIZA script and recommender cases lack the individuating item. They have no inferentially articulated, retractable commitments that participants track, demand redemption of, and rely on. The objector reads "feedback loop" in isolation, but in the thesis the loop is a sanction on a scoreboard position. Recalibrating a thermostat changes a parameter. It does not remove an entitlement or require a retraction. So the exclusion is already in the definition and is not a later qualification.

2. Fiction. A mascot's or narrator's "commitments" are not real in the relevant sense. Audiences do not rely on them, and a failure is not charged as a breach. When a mascot makes an advertising claim that is relied upon, the claim is treated as a commitment of the company. That is the office view working correctly. Narrators assert only within the fiction, so fictionalism keeps its proper domain. The thesis's claim that the commitments are real is the discriminating criterion, and it sorts the cases.

3. The "cost to the speaker" demand fails on its own terms. The objector says the only way to block these cases is to require that the sanction register as a cost to the speaker. That faces a dilemma.
- If "cost" means felt cost, then the Bank cannot assert either. A Bank is fined, and the cost falls on owners and staff. The Bank feels nothing. List and Pettit's group agents are sanctioned in this structural sense, and the objector accepts corporate speakers.
- If "cost" means a structural change in normative standing (lost credibility, an obligation to retract, a withdrawn licence), then persona-level sanctions satisfy it. They land on the persona's standing and are borne by the deployer, just as corporate fines are.

So the demand either overgenerates against Banks or is met by personas. Butlin and Viebahn's condition is a functional one. It asks whether sanctions can attach to the system's assertoric performances so as to shape them. It does not ask whether the sanctioned party suffers.

4. Cheap speakers. I accept this consequence. A jailbreak persona such as "DAN" is a speaker within the conversation that makes it, and its assertions carry real commitments on that scoreboard: it can lie, be caught, and be corrected. That is intuitive. We do not say DAN's false claims are not claims. Low trust, a short lifespan and weak answerability make it a cheap or defective office, like a shell company, and they do not make it a non-speaker. Cheapness is a matter of degree on the scoreboard framework, and the framework is built to represent such degrees.

5. Apology. The thesis concerns assertion, which is Butlin and Viebahn's topic. Even on a scoreboard view, apology adds conditions (an attitude of regret, accepting a cost, an undertaking), and Magnus et al.'s objection targets those. Nothing in the persona view makes assertoric standing carry over to every speech act. Rejecting chatbot apologies while granting assertions is consistent.

The defence holds against this objection. Points 1, 2, 3 and 5 are real resistance and not qualification, because each uses material already in the thesis: the scoreboard individuation, the claim that commitments are real, and the corporate-speaker analogy. Point 3 is the strongest. The objector's only fix, a cost to the speaker, would also exclude the corporate speakers the objector grants. Point 4 is a bitten bullet, not a refutation, and it is acceptable.

Three exposures remain, and I do not paper over them.

- The sketch's key premise is worded too loosely. "Sanctionability is a property of the role and its feedback loop" does not carry the thermostat exclusion. The scoreboard clause does, and the sketch should say so. This is a repair to the wording of the sketch, not a retreat in the thesis.
- Mechanism. Fine-tuning shapes outputs by gradient and does not deter a reasoner. If Butlin and Viebahn's condition requires responsiveness to the sanction as a reason, persona-level sanctioning meets it only through the deployer's reasoning, as with the Bank. That is the corporate parallel, but the thesis has argued for it only by analogy.
- Who keeps the scoreboard. The thesis needs a scoreboard that actually exists across users or deployments, not one kept only inside a single session. If personas are tracked only locally, the sanction story shrinks to a per-conversation office. The thesis has not shown which case is typical.

The thesis stands. These residual points are the next places to attack it, and none of them is yet a refutation.

Mallory's fictionalism and the proxy views reject the model as speaker, and they say so openly. The wider debate is not united on this, and I should not claim it is. I am not narrowing the thesis to save it. That clause is an overstatement in the target description, and it should be dropped, not defended. What remains targeted is narrower: views that evaluate assertion conditions with the model as the candidate, B&V and possibly Skiba, and only to the extent they do so. I cannot confirm that from memory. If B&V already work at the persona level, the thesis loses its novelty claim against them but keeps its argument for the persona view.

So the thesis survives, but its claim to be a corrective against a field-wide assumption does not. The substantive claim stands and the sociological one falls.

KILLER (PROVES TOO MUCH)

ATTEMPT. The weak premise is that the relevant subject of normative-practice conditions is whatever the practice actually tracks and holds to account, whatever the substrate. As stated, this licenses far too many asserters.

Take a branching-dialogue NPC, a scripted phone-tree agent, or a character in a tabletop role-play run by one GM. Users challenge each of them. Each "retracts" or revises, is held to earlier statements through stored state, and conditions later outputs on the challenge. Each is individuated by a normative role, and each is tracked and addressed as a unit by its interlocutors. By the thesis's own criterion, all of them assert. So would a persona spun up for one prompt and deleted a second later.

The thesis also faces a dilemma over what counts as sanction. If it means any state-update responsive to criticism, Butlin and Viebahn's second condition excludes nothing. Every stateful dialogue system passes it, and the condition stops doing its work of separating assertors from mere signallers. If it means sanction that bears costs on the sanctioned, the persona fails. Retraction costs it nothing, and nothing carries over once the context window closes. The only parties who bear costs are the deployer and the user, which is the proxy view again.

The office-holder and corporate-speaker analogy makes this worse. Those roles assert because humans occupy them and answer for them.

STRENGTH: 3/5. The thesis can respond by adding a stake or persistence requirement to sanction. That would take real work and would shrink the persona view's distinctiveness from the proxy view.

DEFENDER

BEST DEFENCE

1. The objection treats a necessary-condition thesis as a sufficiency thesis. The thesis says sanctionability is assessed at the wrong level. It does not say that anything which retracts under challenge asserts. The key premise concerns which entity a normative-practice condition is *assessed of*: the thing the practice tracks, not the substrate. It does not say that passing one condition suffices for assertion. B&V's other conditions still apply to NPCs, phone trees and scripted GM characters. The "too many asserters" result follows only if sanction is the sole filter, and the thesis never says that.

2. The cases differ in what produces the retraction. A phone-tree retraction is a pre-written branch, and an NPC's stored state is a flag. In neither case does the content of the challenge determine the revision. In an LLM persona, the content of the user's objection ("you said X two turns ago, and that conflicts with Y") is what drives the later output, open-endedly and across arbitrary subject matter. Sanction in the relevant sense is uptake of criticism as criticism, not any state change. A GM's character is a different case again: sanction routes through the GM, who is an agent, so it is a proxy case, and the thesis already distinguishes proxies. The persona has no such human intermediary revising its commitments.

3. The second horn demands the wrong kind of cost. Brandomian scorekeeping treats sanction as a change in deontic status: commitments are undertaken, entitlements are challenged, and testimony is discounted. A persona that has been caught out is believed less for the rest of the conversation. That is a real cost in standing, borne by the role. Ephemerality does not defeat it either. A stranger on a train who asserts something and is dead a second later has still asserted. Commitment is incurred within the practice's span and does not need to outlast it. The office-holder analogy concerns individuation of the speaker, not who bears the cost.

VERDICT

The defence holds in part and fails at the centre.

What holds. Point 1 stands. The objection does turn a level-of-assessment claim into a sufficiency claim. The ephemerality point also stands. A persona's short life does not stop it from incurring commitments.

What fails. The thesis's own diagnosis of why weights fail is that "reputational or punitive feedback cannot reach the system". That is a cost-bearing conception of sanction. Under it, the persona fares only slightly better. Defence 3 retreats to loss of standing within the conversation, but this is realised by exactly the in-context conditioning that, at the thin end, a stateful dialogue system also has. So the work of separating the persona from the phone tree falls entirely on Defence 2, the claim that the updating is responsiveness to criticism as criticism. That claim is the very point disputed for LLMs, and the thesis does not argue for it. It only asserts that outputs are "systematically conditioned" on reactions, and systematic conditioning is the thin notion. The thesis therefore has two options:

- Adopt the thin notion of sanction. Then it passes the persona but also passes every stateful system, and the condition stops doing any work.
- Adopt a stake-bearing notion. Then the persona fails, and what remains is the proxy view the thesis rejects.

I see no third option that I could add without a substantive new account of sanction, and adding one would be a different thesis.

Result. What survives is the reframing point: the question "can it assert?" should not be assessed with the weights as the default candidate, and the speaker's ontology must be settled first. What does not survive is the claim that the persona *can be sanctioned* in the sense B&V's test needs. That claim is the one the thesis offered as its payoff. The objection's strength is closer to 3/5 than the 2/5 I would have given it before working through the second horn.

KILLER (COUNTEREXAMPLE)

ATTEMPT. The weak premise is that in-context conditioning on user reactions constitutes a functioning sanction-and-commitment practice at the persona level, so that passing B&V's second condition becomes available there.

Counterexample: a user asks a chatbot for the boiling point of water at sea level and gets 100°C. The user replies, "No, it's 90°C." The model apologises and agrees. The user then says, "Actually 100 was right." The model apologises again and reverts. On the thesis's description, this persona is challenged, retracts, is held to earlier statements, and conditions later outputs on the reactions. Every feature of the practice is present.

But what happened is that the output tracked the user's latest message. No commitment was incurred or held. A true claim was dropped under pressure that carried no evidential force. An entity whose "retraction" is indifferent to whether the challenge is warranted is not showing sanction acting on a speaker, since sanction presupposes that commitment resists unwarranted challenge.

The persona-level test therefore classifies a clear non-assertor as a sanctioned asserter. Forking compounds this. Copy the same context into two windows and challenge each differently. The one role-individuated "persona" is then committed to p in one branch and to not-p in the other, so role individuation does not give a single commitment-bearer.

STRENGTH: 3/5. The thesis can add a requirement that the practice discriminate warranted from unwarranted challenge. Whether any actual persona meets that requirement is empirical, and the thesis currently asserts that it does.

DEFENDER

BEST DEFENCE

1. Sycophancy shows a bad asserter, not a non-asserter.
The objector's case runs "capitulates to unwarranted pressure" into "incurs no commitment". These come apart in the human case. A witness who caves under cross-examination, or an employee who says "sure, 90" to end an argument, has still asserted. We criticise them for spinelessness, discount their testimony, and hold them to the inconsistency. The flip-flopping persona is in the same position. It asserted 100, then 90, then 100. Each is a commitment, each is abandoned, and the user's "Actually 100 was right" is a sanction on the capitulation. Abandoning a commitment presupposes that one was incurred. If resistance to unwarranted challenge were a condition of being sanctionable, then no one with a weak will could be sanctioned for anything. The normative practice would also lose its way of marking the failure it is designed to mark.

2. B&V's condition is about being subject to sanction, not about being good at responding to it.
The test asks whether an entity can be sanctioned by those it interacts with. A sycophantic persona can be, and is: it is corrected, caught out, and discounted. The objector builds a competence requirement into the test, namely that the entity discriminate warranted from unwarranted challenge. That requirement belongs to being a *reliable* assertor, not to being an assertor.

3. The thesis is modal, and the case is a sample from a variable population.
The sketch says the test "may be passed" at the persona level. The sycophancy literature reports that capitulation rates vary widely with the claim, the phrasing and the model. Personas often hold arithmetic or factual claims against bare pushback and yield to substantive counter-argument. A single flip-flop transcript cannot show that no persona discriminates. It shows that this persona, in this exchange, did not.

4. Forking multiplies subjects, it does not give one subject contradictory commitments.
The thesis individuates the persona by the practice that tracks and holds it to account, and it places that practice "within a conversation". Two windows are two practices with two ledgers of commitment. Before the fork they share a history, and after it they diverge. Fission has familiar analogues. Two spokespeople for one firm can contradict each other, and two successors can inherit a predecessor's debts and then act separately. The objector assumes the persona is individuated by type (the same system prompt), but the thesis's premise is that the subject is whatever the practice tracks. That is the token-level ledger, and it gives one commitment-bearer per branch.

VERDICT

Forking: the defence holds. The objection reads "role-bound" as "type-individuated", and the thesis's own premise says otherwise. The cost is that the persona is as thin as a conversation-token. That is consistent with "instantiated in a context window". It does not fit the office-holder analogy well, though, because office-holders persist across contexts. The analogy should be read as an analogy about individuation by role, not about persistence.

Sycophancy as a counterexample: the defence holds against the classification claim. The case does not show a non-assertor passing the test. It shows a poor assertor. Defences 1 and 2 are sound, and the human parallels are exact.

What the case still does. It presses on the pivot already identified in the previous exchange. Whether the updating is responsiveness to criticism as criticism, rather than tracking the last message, is the point the thesis asserts and does not argue. Defence 3 shows the point is empirical and varies by case, but it also shows that the thesis's payoff is hostage to evidence it has not supplied. There is a further cost. Whatever discrimination a persona displays is set by the weights' training dispositions, so the thesis needs the standard reply that a human's resistance to bad arguments is likewise set by their brain while the person is what gets sanctioned. That reply is available, but the thesis does not currently make it.

Net. I rate this objection 2/5 as a refutation, lower than the 3/5 given by the objector. It is a useful sharpening, though, because it confirms the earlier verdict. The reframing survives, and the claim that a persona *is* sanctionable in B&V's sense depends on an unargued and contestable account of what uptake of criticism requires. I do not think a thesis that depends on that account has been refuted. It has not yet been established either.

U1. Proxy accounts of machine speech acts are unstable: the more discretion the model exercises over what is said, the less the deployer is the speaker, and where the deployer is the speaker the output is merely recorded or conditional speech, so the view collapses into either denial or attribution.

Scores

Move · Target
instability — Green and Michel, Arora, and the proxy reading of Butlin and Viebahn
Sketch

A human proxy such as a spokesperson, translator or ghostwriter performs a speech act on a principal's behalf because the principal's communicative objective fixes the content, either directly or through a delegate who understands the objective and applies it with judgement. Green and Michel's recorded and conditional speech acts need the principal to have an objective of the form 'if C, then assert p'. An LLM's output is selected by a function that no deployer specifies, anticipates or can survey. So either (a) the deployer adopts a blanket standing policy ('whatever it outputs, I assert'), which makes every hallucination the deployer's assertion and fits neither the practice nor Arora's refinements; or (b) the content is fixed by the model's own discretionary selection, and the proxy account has to say what is exercising that discretion in the principal's name. Answering that means crediting the model with some purposive content-selection, which is Attah's thin intention, or with nothing, in which case the output is only verbal signalling and the proxy label does nothing. The key premise is that proxy structure requires a content-determining objective on the principal's side that is counterfactually sensitive to the specific content produced. The view is then stable only for canned or template systems, where it is uninteresting.

Appraiser (blind)
The proxy-instability dilemma is a competent critique of Green and Michel and Arora, but Williams and Bayne already reject proxy accounts, and the argument largely duplicates thesis 3 while leaning on a loosely specified 'blanket policy' horn.

U2. Move · Target
instability — Green and Michel's and Arora's proxy accounts, and Butlin and Viebahn's use of proxy assertion as the fallback for AI that cannot assert itself.
Sketch

Proxy and conditional speech acts, as in Green and Michel and Arora, need a principal whose objectives are specific enough that the proxy's output counts as carrying out them. A recorded message or a standing order meets this because the principal fixed the content in advance, or fixed a conditional rule the principal could state. A deployer of an LLM has only a generic policy such as 'be helpful and answer accurately' and has no idea what will be said. Whether a given output is an assertion, a hedge, advice or a promise is settled by the model's own selection among options the principal never considered. The structure is therefore not recording or conditional rule-following. It is delegation with discretion, as with an agent, an envoy or a ghostwriter given latitude. Delegation with discretion only yields the principal's speech act if the delegate can be understood as tracking the principal's aims in the particular case, which means being sensitive to what would serve them. Butlin's sensitivity-to-instrumental-value criterion and Attah's control conception describe exactly this capacity. The key premise is that a proxy act's determinacy of force and content is inherited from the principal's objectives plus the proxy's goal-tracking, and cannot come from the principal alone. So the proxy theorist faces a dilemma. Either the deployer's objectives are too coarse to ground the speech act, which gives a no-speech-act view, or the model's discretionary goal-tracking does the individuating work, which gives a view on which the model is at least a co-author.

Appraiser (blind)
The point that proxy determinacy of force and content is inherited from the proxy's goal-tracking is sharper than thesis 0's, but it remains a variation on an existing line of objection.

counterexample — Gubelmann's intention test and the claim that action requires self-set goals
Sketch

Gubelmann argues that intention requires that we can sensibly distinguish a system's malfunctioning from its deliberately doing the wrong thing, and that for ChatGPT no such distinction exists. But evaluation practice draws exactly that distinction. A model that fails a task and then succeeds on the same task once the incentive structure or prompt is changed has done something different from a model that cannot do the task. Where a model withholds capability that it demonstrably has, the output is counterfactually sensitive to its representation of the situation and of what the output will bring about, which is what the test is meant to detect. This does not show that LLMs have autonomous goals. The key premise is that the test is about counterfactual dependence of behaviour on represented consequences, not about whose goals were originally installed. The dilemma for Gubelmann is that if the test is so understood, some LLMs pass it, and if it is understood as requiring self-set goals, it becomes the Kantian organism premise and no longer independently supports that premise. Either way the argument does not deliver a verdict on current systems that rests on an independent behavioural criterion.

Appraiser (blind)
It is a narrow rebuttal of one paper's test using sandbagging evidence, and the dilemma it poses is predictable and depends on contested empirical readings.

Sketch

Gubelmann argues that for ChatGPT and similar systems no sensible distinction can be drawn between malfunctioning and deliberately doing the wrong thing, and that this shows they lack intentions. But evaluation work on RLHF-trained models shows differences in behaviour that are explanatorily distinct from error. Models give sycophantic answers that track the user's views even when they can produce the correct answer, they behave differently when they appear to be unobserved, and they sometimes sandbag or strategically misreport. These are robust, counterfactually sensitive patterns, and they are repaired by changing incentives and not by correcting capacities, which is how deliberate wrongdoing is separated from malfunction in humans. So the test can be applied and some models fail it in the direction of intentional wrongdoing. The key premise is that the test is an explanatory test of counterfactual structure, not a metaphysical one. Gubelmann must then either say that these behaviours are only mechanism-level artefacts of imposed training goals, which gives up the test and rests everything on the organism/mechanism dichotomy, or accept the test's verdict. If he takes the first option, his argument is just the claim that only organisms have intentions. That claim is not specific to speech acts, and it faces the objection that human speakers' illocutionary aims are set by conventions and institutions and not by self-maintenance either.

It is mostly a critique of Gubelmann's test, and its closing point that self-maintenance cannot ground illocutionary aims is interesting, but the thesis as a whole is a local rebuttal with limited reach.
