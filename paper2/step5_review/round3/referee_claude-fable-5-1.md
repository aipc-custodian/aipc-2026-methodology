I'll search for prior work that may anticipate the paper's main claims before writing the report.# Referee Report: "The Unit of Assertion Failure"

## Summary

The paper takes as its antecedent that chatbot outputs are assertions and asks what bears the defects of hallucination, sycophancy and insincerity. It argues (i) that verdicts are grounded in, and the defects borne by, the trained policy (weights + decoding rule) rather than the sampled string; (ii) that sanction regimes are judged by the policies they induce and that "lineages" of effective policies meet a weakened sanction condition; (iii) that disagreement among resampled outputs is not evidence of insincerity; and (iv) that whether outputs assert the policy's calibrated confidence is open, but the thesis survives either answer.

## Prior work: what I checked and what anticipates the paper

I searched for philosophical work on AI assertion, proxy assertion and sanction; for ML and philosophy work on honesty/lying detection via resampling and neutral-context belief elicitation; for work on the gap between internal confidence and linguistic assertiveness; and for reliabilist treatments of LLM outputs. Findings:

**Uncited philosophical literature the paper must engage.** The proxy-assertion debate is central to §3.5 but almost entirely absent. Williams & Bayne note that proxy-assertion was first proposed by Nickel (2013), who suggests that machines can qualify as "speech actants to a substantial degree" but that "ultimate responsibility for artificial speech does not lie with machines". Also missing: Freiman & Miller's (2020) Oxford Handbook chapter, which critically surveys arguments for and against the view that technological instruments, devices, or machines can assert or testify; Arora (2024), which argues that the view that some such machine utterances can be "proxy assertions", made on behalf of the designers and/or deployers of such machines is more tenable; Löhr's (2026, *APQ*) "Why Proxy Accounts of AI Speech Acts Fail", arguing that the proxy account neglects the relational nature of speech, suggesting that no human can be held responsible for most of what a chatbot says; and Massaguer Gómez & Gaitán Torres (2026, *Synthese*), "Who asserts when no one asserts?", which holds that the mediated character of LLM outputs—the fact that responsibility traces back to developers and deployers rather than to the system itself—must be communicatively available within the exchange. Gubelmann (2024) and Green & Michel (2022) should also appear. The "deployer assumption" is attacked without citing the people who actually defend it.

**ML work that anticipates the force/warrant criterion (§2.2).** The gap between internal confidence and linguistic assertiveness is already a named construct. "Epistemic Integrity in LLMs" defines epistemic calibration as the process of aligning a model's expressed confidence, conveyed through linguistic assertiveness, with its actual reliability or correctness. "The Polite Liar" makes almost exactly the paper's R1/R2 point: a model can be perfectly calibrated—its confidence scores precisely matching its accuracy rates—while systematically violating epistemic norms in its communication. The numbers align, but the language deceives. This happens because calibration metrics evaluate probability distributions, not speech acts. Neither is cited.

**Work that anticipates or challenges §4.** The MASK benchmark operationalises lying as divergence between pressured output and belief elicited in neutral prompts, i.e. the paper's x₀ methodology: it measures when models answer questions in a way that indicates a robustly-held belief that is consistent across various related questions. More troubling, there is empirical work that contradicts the practical reading of §4. One study reports: Without exception, we observed that deception is significantly less stable than honesty. Deceptive answers are more likely to flip given input paraphrasing, output resampling, or activation noise. Another frames the contrast the other way from the paper: while a hallucinating model is consistently incorrect, deception is a strategic inconsistency. And the paper's claim (§5.3) that no probe-based measure of the policy's attitude "is available" is asserted against work that claims exactly that: the "Hypocrisy Gap" paper uses a sparse linear truth probe that distinguishes true from false claims under neutral prompts, then projects the mean CoT latent trajectory onto the same truth direction under sycophancy-inducing prompts; and a May 2026 preprint asks whether a model agreeing with a user's false belief is failing to detect the error, or noticing and agreeing anyway, and claims to show the latter. The author has Levinstein & Herrmann to lean on, but must argue rather than assert.

**Reliabilist framing (fn. 1–2).** A 2026 preprint already applies the generality problem to LLM process individuation: at arrangement scale, this raises reliabilism's generality problem. Pretraining, filtering, RLHF, retrieval, tools, prompts, and institutional uptake permit many process descriptions. Worth a citation, not a threat to novelty.

**Verified citations.** Kalai et al. 2026 is real (Nature volume 653, pages 1047–1051, published 22 April 2026); Mácha 2026 is real and its thesis is as reported (LLM hallucinations are best explained as a truth representation problem: Current models lack an internal representation of propositions as truth-bearers); Gómez 2026 is real but the author's surname is "Massaguer Gómez"—the reference should be corrected. I could not verify Li et al. (2026, arXiv:2605.05403) or the Chalmers DOI.

I did not find a philosophy paper that states this exact "unit of assessment" thesis. The framing is new; several of its components are not.

## Objections, ranked by seriousness

**1. The bearer argument (§2.5) rests on an undefended principle, and the principle is in tension with the paper's own definitions.** This is the paper's main claimed contribution, and it is the weakest link. (a) The policy is defined as weights *plus decoding rule*, and the sampler is the decoding rule. So the random draw is part of the producer, not external to it. The paper cannot then say "the seed is no part of the producer's response to reasons" without quietly adding a "response to reasons" rider that does all the work—and that rider is precisely what needs defending, since the question is whether a stochastic draw can be an act-level failure. (b) The determinism parallel backfires. Under determinism the Tuesday speaker's "weighing of reasons" is fixed by prior state exactly as the draw is fixed by the seed. Either internal-state differences suffice (then RNG state counts) or they don't (then the human act doesn't bear the defect either). The paper needs a principled line between reason-responsive and non-reason-responsive internal differences, and must show the sampler falls on the right side. (c) The author concedes that rejecting Difference-making shrinks (L) to its grounding part, and also concedes that distribution-level assessment is "standard in machine learning." So without the principle, the distinctive philosophical claim evaporates.

**2. The negative part of (L) is close to definitional, so the same-string/different-policy test risks begging the question.** The paper stipulates hallucination = force exceeding c\*, where c\* is a reference-class frequency. Of course a reference-class frequency is a policy property, and of course the string doesn't fix it. An opponent who holds that hallucination is false-and-fabricated output will say the test simply changes the subject. The paper flags "overassertion" as an alternative label, but then the title claim becomes "overassertion, as I define it, is a policy property"—true but thin. The author needs an independent argument that this is the concept that matters for *assertion*, not just a stipulation. Relatedly: hedged strings ("probably p") express different content from p, so "summing over strings that express p" cannot straightforwardly deliver q for hedged outputs; and the privileging of unit temperature for q is arbitrary given the paper's own admission that post-trained distributions are miscalibrated—c\* ends up doing all the work and q is idle.

**3. §4 is formally correct but nearly trivial, and its headline is overstated against empirical evidence.** Step 1 (spread screens off sincerity from resampling) is a tautology; Step 3 (every insincere policy has a sincere twin) follows immediately. Step 2 concedes that resampling *can* be evidence given background assumptions—but that is what evidence always is. So "not evidence of insincerity" reduces to "not evidence absent priors," which is true of everything. Meanwhile the cited empirical result that deceptive outputs flip more under resampling is exactly the kind of background regularity that would make resampling evidence in practice. The thesis should be reformulated as a point about what resampling can *discriminate in principle*, and the empirical literature acknowledged.

**4. The lineage proposal (§3.2–3.3) establishes very little.** The conclusion is doubly conditional (on theories admitting the antecedent, and on theories weakening (iii)), and the author admits the self-recalibrating thermometer meets the weakened condition too. So "a lineage meets the weakened sanction component" says only: the thing that persists through correction is the thing corrected. Individuation is also underspecified—does swapping one vendor's model for another in the same role continue the lineage? The professor analogy breaks because her identity is not constituted by the updates. Either sharpen this section or cut it to the criterion claim of §3.1.

**5. The Chalmers reconciliation (§2.6) papers over a real tension.** If the thread speaks and the abstract policy cannot perform, then the policy bears a defect of a performance it did not perform. The paper's own gloss—"the human case has both levels; the chatbot case only the trait-like one"—implies there is no act-level assertion defect at all, which sits awkwardly with the antecedent that outputs are assertions (acts).

**6. R3 (§5) is a manufactured option.** No one holds that an output asserts a confidence value no hearer could recover from the string. R2 is the live reading. §5 could be compressed to a page.

**7. The "single-output assumption" needs a named proponent.** For sycophancy, no one holds it—Sharma et al. measure covariation across prompts. Hicks et al.'s "bullshit machine" and Mácha's architectural diagnosis are already dispositional/policy-level. Who is the target?

## Minor points

- Correct the Gómez reference; add Nickel, Freiman & Miller, Arora, Löhr, Massaguer Gómez & Gaitán Torres, Gubelmann, Green & Michel.
- Fn. 3's lie definition from Evans et al. covers lies, not insincerity generally; the paper's "selective decoupling" is broader.
- §3.4's "proper vs improper scoring" point is good and could be made earlier; it is the clearest version of the criterion claim.

## Assessment

**Is it good?** Competently argued, clearly written, well-informed on the ML side, and the same-string/different-policy test is a neat device. But the philosophical payload is thinner than the abstract promises: the grounding claim is near-definitional, the bearer claim rests on an undefended principle, §4 is formally trivial, and §3 is heavily conditional.

**Is it novel?** The *framing*—asking what the unit of assessment is for assertion defects—is new in the philosophy literature and worth having. Several components are anticipated: the calibration-vs-assertiveness gap, neutral-context belief elicitation, and the reliabilist framing all exist in ML and adjacent work. The proxy-assertion literature, which the paper's §3.5 directly bears on, is not engaged at all.

**Is it publishable?** Not as it stands. **Major revisions.** A shorter paper that (i) confronts the definitional-circularity objection head-on, (ii) either defends Difference-making properly or demotes the bearer claim, (iii) restates §4 as an in-principle discrimination result and engages the stability-of-deception findings, (iv) engages the proxy-assertion literature in §3.5, and (v) cuts §5 to a page, would be a useful contribution to the AI-assertion debate and I would be glad to see it again.
