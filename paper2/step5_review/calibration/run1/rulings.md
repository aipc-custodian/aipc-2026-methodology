# Rulings

The paper builds an ingenious family of Closure-based arguments, and the formal derivations are not seriously disputed. Its central step, that K(@p ⊃ p) is a hard fact about the past, faces a published content-shift objection (Geddes, Wawer) aimed at the same construction. The paper also never says how propositions are individuated, and both gaps need an answer. The other concerns about scholarship, the comparison with rival responses, and presentation are matters of emphasis. None of the cited prior work meets the standard for anticipating what the paper claims as new.

## MUST ADDRESS

**Concern** (chatgpt (objections 1, 2, 4 in part, 3 in part); gemini (objection 2); claude (objection 1)): Premiss (10)/(13) says the past fact K(@p ⊃ p) is a hard fact that is fixed. But 'actually' is a modal indexical, so the proposition known at t−1 differs across worlds that share a past. Given @p, that proposition is true only at p-worlds. The fact therefore looks soft, like a past belief that p. The 'true either way' reply on pp. 16–18 and in fn. 19 trades on the diagonal (primary) profile, while the Knowledge Lemma needs the horizontal (secondary) one. The same worry applies to the naming variants in §4.1. The paper also ignores published replies to its own earlier version of this construction.

**Reason:** Everything in §4 turns on whether (13) is a hard fact. The paper's defence of (13) answers an entailment-based soft-fact criterion but never addresses the point that the content of a modal-indexical attitude shifts across worlds with a shared past. If (13) fails, the case for blaming Closure fails with it.

**Checked:** I searched for Geddes, Wawer and Garrett & Joaquin. All three exist, and none is in the paper's bibliography. Geddes (Analysis 2023, abstract and snippets): a past mental state with a modal indexical must differ in content across worlds sharing a past, so Lampert's earlier argument equivocates. Wawer (Synthese 2023, abstract): a countermodel shows the argument defective; it needs a tacit premise about the temporal status of a priori knowledge, and that premise can plausibly be rejected in an Ockhamist framework. Wawer's summary says the earlier argument contains what Lampert calls the knowledge lemma, which this paper reuses. So the objection is real and published, not a referee invention.

## MUST ADDRESS

**Concern** (claude (objection 2); gemini (objection 1); chatgpt (objection 6)): The paper never says what propositions are. On a coarse-grained view, given @p, the proposition (@p ⊃ p) just is p. Then God's belief or S's knowledge amounts to foreknowledge, 'God may remain agnostic' (§4) is false, and fn. 22 is false. On a fine-grained or two-dimensional view, one may know the trivial primary-intension truth without knowing the secondary singular proposition that Closure needs. The same dilemma arises for 'George = 1 ⊃ p'. Closure is also an intensional transfer principle applied to hyperintensionally individuated contents. There is a related worry about moving from a sentence being a logical truth, to the proposition it expresses, to that proposition being known.

**Reason:** The headline claim is that a priori knowledge of a trivial truth that is not foreknowledge blocks freedom. That claim needs the known item to be a priori and also to necessitate p, while remaining distinct from p. The paper takes no stand on how contents are individuated, and that is the one choice that decides whether these hold together. The paper's brief remarks on Chalmers (fn. 16) and Jeshion (fn. 23) do not settle it.

**Checked:** The only literature claims here are Chalmers 2011 and Jeshion 2002. The paper cites both: fn. 16 says Chalmers's challenge is not needed in full generality, and fn. 23 cites Jeshion on naming. I did not verify the content of Lampert & Waldrop's 'Free Will and Intensional Operators' beyond its listing, so I put no weight on claude's description of it.
