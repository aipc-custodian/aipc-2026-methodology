## Preliminary note the editor should know

This manuscript is not unpublished. It appeared as Fabio Lampert, *Mind* 134(533): 1–32 (early online October 2024, issue January 2025), doi 10.1093/mind/fzae058. The University of Vienna repository lists it as published in Mind, Volume 134, Issue 533, early online 21 Oct 2024. It already has a published reply: Jonas Werner, "Open Theism and the Contingent A Priori", Mind, Volume 135, Issue 538, April 2026, pp. 455–464, and the SEP entry on foreknowledge now discusses it under "transfer of necessity." So what follows is a report on the paper as if it were a fresh submission, but the editor should be aware that a normal referee would flag this as a duplicate-submission problem.

## Summary

The paper takes the standard Closure-based argument for theological fatalism (N[God believed p], ◻(God believed p ⊃ p) ⊢ Np) and builds parallels that dispense with foreknowledge. The trick: given @p, the sentence "@p ⊃ p" is a priori (indeed a theorem under real-world validity) yet, as a proposition, is true at exactly the p-worlds. So anyone's factive attitude toward it necessitates p (Belief/Knowledge Lemma). Combine with "no choice about the past" and Closure, and S's trivial past knowledge yields Np. Variants use rigidified descriptions, named truth-values, sets, and pluralities. Since the conclusion is absurd, the author says, something is wrong with the Master Argument too; the culprit is Closure.

## Objections, ranked by seriousness

### 1. (Decisive if not answered) The "hard fact" claim for premise (13)/(10) rests on a primary/secondary-intension conflation, and the paper's own precursor has already been refuted on exactly this point in print

The whole dialectical weight sits on the claim that K(@p ⊃ p) is a *hard* fact about t−1. The argument for this (§4, fn. 19) is: the conditional is "true either way," like a tautology, so its being known doesn't obtain in virtue of the future.

This equivocates. "True whichever way the future goes" is a claim about the *diagonal* (primary intension / S's epistemic state), which is world-invariant. But the entailment to p that the Knowledge Lemma exploits belongs to the *secondary* intension of "@p ⊃ p", which, given @p, is true at exactly the p-worlds — i.e. intensionally it just *is* p. So the fact K⟨@p ⊃ p⟩ decomposes as: [S is in epistemic state E at t−1] ∧ [⟨@p ⊃ p⟩ is true], and the second conjunct, given @p, is p. That is the textbook profile of a soft fact — an intrinsic past state plus a future-dependent condition — structurally identical to "S truly believed at t−1 that p," which the paper itself concedes (fn. 18) incompatibilists treat as soft. Had you not raised your hand, S's intrinsic state would be unchanged, but S would *not* have known ⟨@p ⊃ p⟩, because that proposition would be false. The tautology analogy fails because p ⊃ p is necessary and ⟨@p ⊃ p⟩ is not — a disanalogy the author actually notes and then sets aside.

The contrast with the Master Argument is instructive, not supportive: incompatibilists call God's belief hard because the belief has the *same content* across worlds and the entailment comes from God's essential infallibility (a property of the believer). Here the content shifts with which world is actual. So the author's claim that "anyone who believes God's past beliefs are hard should agree" K(@p ⊃ p) is hard is simply false; the cases are disanalogous in precisely the respect that matters.

The grounding claim in fn. 19 — that tautologies are "much more reasonably" true independently of what happens — is asserted, not argued, and conflicts with the standard Finean view that p ∨ ¬p is grounded in whichever disjunct obtains.

Consequence: the dependence/soft-fact camp (Todd 2013, Swenson 2016, Law 2020/2021, Cyr & Law 2020, Wasserman 2022) rejects (13) at zero cost. Swenson (2016) argues that what's fixed isn't the past in toto, but so much of the past as isn't dependent on the future; Law (2020) advocates junking the fixity of the past altogether and replacing it with the principle of the fixity of the independent. The paper cites Swenson once in a footnote and never engages Law, Cyr & Law, or Wasserman. So the headline conclusion — that Closure is "the core issue in all cases" — is not established. If anything the new arguments are a fresh datum *for* the dependence approach.

Crucially, this objection has already been made in print against the author's own earlier version of the construction (Lampert 2022, *Analysis*), and the manuscript cites none of the replies:

- Geddes, "Resolving a puzzle about the fixity of the past": he shows that the attempt to establish the conflict fails, as it rests on the implicit assumption that a past mental state or utterance involving a modal indexical must have the same content across worlds with a shared past, when in fact it must, given its character, differ in content. That is exactly the point above.
- Wawer, *Synthese* 2023: he explains how Lampert's argument could be corrected if supported with an extra premise regarding the temporal status of a priori knowledge; this tacit assumption is shown to be the weakest link, and it is argued that, despite Lampert's assurance, it can be plausibly rejected in a roughly Ockhamist framework. Wawer's conclusion is that an agent's knowing something a priori might presently be a "soft" fact whose present occurrence depends on what happens in the future.
- Garrett & Joaquin, *Organon F* 2024: they argue that Lampert's argument fails because of its use of the material conditional. (I find this the weakest of the three, but it should be addressed in a footnote.)

A revised version must engage Geddes and Wawer head-on. As it stands, §4's rebuttal of the soft-fact response is a non-sequitur and the paper's central claim is unsupported.

### 2. (Serious) The paper never commits to a theory of propositions, and the argument needs incompatible things from both options

- **Coarse-grained (sets of worlds):** then, given @p, ⟨@p ⊃ p⟩ = ⟨p⟩. God believing it just *is* God foreknowing p; "God may remain entirely agnostic" (§4) is false; and fn. 22's "it goes without saying that S did not know that you would raise your hand" is false too, since S knows a proposition identical to p. The A Priori Belief Argument collapses into the Master Argument and the claim that foreknowledge is dispensable evaporates.
- **Fine-grained / two-dimensional:** then one can hold that S knows something trivial that is distinct from p. But now (a) the Soames/Salmon worry that one does not know the *secondary* singular proposition a priori (only the metalinguistic/diagonal one) bites directly, and the author needs the single object of knowledge to be both a priori and p-necessitating; and (b) Closure transfers N along *metaphysical* entailment, which is an intensional relation, while the premise N(K q) is being motivated for a hyperintensionally individuated q. The author's own co-authored paper makes this the crux: under plausible assumptions, Closure is valid if and only if the 'no choice' operator is intensional. If N is hyperintensional, the paper owes an account of why the fixity-of-the-past intuition supports N applied to fine-grained contents at all.

Either way the manuscript needs an explicit section stating what propositions are and showing the argument survives on that choice.

### 3. (Significant) The strategic moral is less novel than advertised, and the "proves too much" strategy has a well-known ancestor the paper doesn't situate itself against

The moral that foreknowledge per se is not what does the work — that any past fact necessitating p suffices given fixity-of-the-past + Closure — is the standard lesson of the logical-fatalism parallel (past truth of p): Finch & Warfield 1999, Merricks 2009, Fischer & Todd 2011/2013 are all about this. The paper cites them only as sources for the Master Argument's formulation. More importantly, Warfield 1997 runs a structurally similar "equivalence" strategy for compatibilism, and Hunt (2002b) links Warfield's argument with Plantinga's (1986), inasmuch as both exploit the logical equivalence of propositions about the contingent future with God's believing those propositions, and argues that they both fall prey to the same reductio: the closure principles they invoke would equally support the compatibility of free will with divine determinism. The present paper exploits an a priori equivalence between ⟨@p ⊃ p⟩ and p in a closely analogous way, and should say why Hunt-style worries don't transfer. Neither Warfield 1997 nor Hunt 2002 is cited.

### 4. (Significant) The closing case for rejecting Closure is thin and internally strained

"The theoretical costs of rejecting Closure are minimal" sits uneasily with fn. 31, which admits that rejecting it threatens the modal consequence argument (Closure here is essentially Finch & Warfield's Beta-2/"Beta-box," the version designed to survive McKay–Johnson). The claim that Closure is "primarily or even solely used to formulate arguments against free will" is unfair — transfer principles are used in responsibility theory (Fischer–Ravizza), in Kearns, etc. And the paper offers no diagnosis of *why* Closure fails — "proves too much" is not an explanation. The independent reasons gestured at (Kearns, Spencer, Lampert & Merlussi) are imported from elsewhere, so the paper's own contribution to the anti-Closure case is only the reductio, which Objection 1 undercuts. The *Bec* principle in fn. 32 is interesting but undeveloped.

Note also that fn. 20's knowledge-free argument — N(@p ⊃ p), ◻((@p ⊃ p) ⊃ p), ⊢ Np — shows the epistemic wrapping is dispensable. The only thing the wrapping buys is swapping "no choice about logical truths" for "no choice about the past." That is exactly the swap Objection 1 says is illegitimate. So the paper's real content is: Closure + Act + "no choice about a priori truths" ⊢ fatalism — and that is already in Kearns 2011 and Lampert & Waldrop 2023.

### 5. (Moderate) Substantial self-overlap that must be declared

§4.1's Truth-Values Argument is the "Plato/George" construction from Lampert & Merlussi, "Naming and Free Will" — whose preprint uses 'Plato' as the name of p's actual truth value, which is 1 if p is true, and 0 otherwise, and whose stated aim is to show that some virtually indisputable inference rules employed in formulations of the Consequence Argument, as well as in fatalistic arguments, fail with a variety of counter-examples. The set/plurality variants and the Jeshion-style objection reappear in the author's "Rigidity and Factivity" (*Episteme*). The Knowledge Lemma and the A Priori Knowledge Argument are close relatives of Lampert 2022. The paper should state explicitly what is new relative to each (my reading: the theological framing and the Belief Lemma are new; §4.1 is largely not).

### 6. (Minor) Technical and bibliographic

- The derivations of Act Lemma, Belief Lemma and KL*/KL** are correct in K + Act (and real-world validity). The paper rightly notes (§3) that the a priori argument only yields actual-world unfreedom, not incompatibility.
- The English glosses ("only if") invite the indicative-conditional complaint raised by Garrett & Joaquin; a footnote stating that the argument is run on the material conditional and does not depend on the gloss would defuse it.
- Grim's no-set-of-all-truths result is in "There Is No Set of All Truths" (*Analysis* 1984), not the 1985 *Noûs* essential-indexicals paper cited.
- Hoffman & Rosenkrantz 1984 appeared in *Philosophical Review* 93(3), not *Philosophical Studies*.
- Merricks 2011 page range is garbled; "Rhoda" is out of alphabetical order; Pike 1966 is listed but (as far as I can see) not cited.

## Verdict

**Is it good?** Technically yes: clearly written, the logic is sound, and the construction is ingenious. Philosophically, the central inference — from "these arguments are absurd" to "Closure is the culprit" — is not earned, because the far more natural diagnosis (the relevant past-knowledge facts are soft/dependent) is dismissed by an argument that conflates diagonal and secondary content.

**Is it novel?** The theological application and the Belief Lemma are new. The underlying mechanism (@-rigidification + factivity + Closure) and the naming variants are recycled from the author's 2021–2023 papers, and the "foreknowledge isn't the real issue" moral is familiar from the logical-fatalism literature.

**Is it publishable?** The construction merits publication; the conclusion as framed does not. My recommendation on a fresh submission would be **major revisions**: (i) engage Geddes and Wawer and either rebut the content-shift point or concede that the arguments are handled by dependence views; (ii) commit to a theory of propositions; (iii) situate against Warfield/Hunt and Law/Cyr; (iv) declare the overlap with "Naming and Free Will"; (v) either strengthen or retract the "costs are minimal" case against Closure. A reframed paper — "here is a new family of Closure-based arguments; dependence theorists can block them, Closure-rejecters can block them, and here is what the choice costs each side" — would be a solid contribution. For what it's worth, *Mind*'s editors evidently judged differently; I think the acceptance was generous on point (i).
