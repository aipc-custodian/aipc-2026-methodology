**1. Do large language models genuinely understand language?**

**(a) Open question.** Does successful linguistic performance by an LLM amount to genuine semantic understanding, or is understanding impossible without forms of grounding that current systems lack?

**(b) Main positions.** A **grounding-skeptical** position holds that prediction over linguistic form, absent the right causal, perceptual, embodied, or intentional relations, is insufficient for understanding. A **functional/distributional realist**position holds that sufficiently rich inferential abilities, internal world models, and linguistic competence can constitute at least some genuine understanding. A **graded or pluralist** position treats “understanding” and “grounding” as multidimensional rather than all-or-nothing.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Melanie Mitchell and David C. Krakauer. “The Debate over Understanding in AI’s Large Language Models.” *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 120, no. 13 (2023): e2215907120. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36943882/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Raphaël Millière and Cameron Buckner. “A Philosophical Introduction to Language Models—Part I: Continuity With Classic Debates.” *CoRR* abs/2401.03910 (2024). [<span class="underline">paper</span>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03910?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Holger Lyre. “‘Understanding AI’: Semantic Grounding in Large Language Models.” arXiv:2402.10992 (2024). [<span class="underline">paper</span>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10992?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** The empirical question—what LLMs internally represent—is entangled with a metasemantic question about what *would count* as understanding. World-model evidence, systematicity, and multimodal grounding can therefore move the debate without settling it, because rival theories assign those findings different significance. Mitchell and Krakauer explicitly characterize the issue as a current heated dispute, while Millière/Buckner and Lyre illustrate the increasingly graded and empirically informed alternatives to a simple “stochastic parrot / real understanding” dichotomy. [<span class="underline">PubMed</span>](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36943882/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**2. Could current or near-future AI systems be conscious?**

**(a) Open question.** What evidence or architectural properties would justify attributing phenomenal consciousness to an artificial system, and do current or foreseeable LLM-based systems meet them?

**(b) Main positions.** **Architectural skeptics** argue that present systems lack features associated with consciousness, such as recurrent processing, global availability, unified agency, or appropriate embodiment. **Permissive functionalists**contend that none of the standard objections establishes that artificial or LLM consciousness is impossible, and that sufficiently appropriate functional organization could suffice. A stronger **impossibility position** argues that the very idea of conscious AI, at least of the foreseeable computational kind, is conceptually mistaken.

**(c) Three anchors.**

David J. Chalmers. “Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?” *Boston Review*, August 9, 2023; preprint arXiv:2303.07103. [<span class="underline">paper</span>](https://www.bostonreview.net/articles/could-a-large-language-model-be-conscious/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Adrienne Prettyman. “Artificial Consciousness.” *Inquiry* 69, no. 6 (2026): 2964–2981; published online December 23, 2024. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/0020174X.2024.2439989?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Andrzej Porębski and Jakub Figura. “There Is No Such Thing as Conscious Artificial Intelligence.” *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* 12 (2025): 1647. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1057%2Fs41599-025-05868-8?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** There is no consensus theory of consciousness to export to AI. Behavioral evidence is especially underdetermining because systems can produce sophisticated first-person reports by training and prompting alone, while architectural evidence depends on which theory of consciousness one accepts. Chalmers reaches a cautious “probably not now, plausibly later,” Prettyman argues that standard anti-AI arguments fail, and Porębski and Figura defend a much stronger negative conclusion. [<span class="underline">arXiv</span>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**3. Can LLMs be agents or speakers with communicative intentions?**

**(a) Open question.** Can an LLM literally intend, act, assert, promise, or perform other speech acts, rather than merely cause outputs that humans interpret as such?

**(b) Main positions.** **Intentionalist anti-agentialism** requires genuine intentions, autonomy, or organism-like practical organization and therefore denies present LLMs speech-act agency. **Functional agentialism** allows thinner, functionally characterized intentions and therefore permits some genuine communicative agency. **Relational/material approaches**argue that the individualistic human-agent model is itself too restrictive and locate agency in networks of systems, users, infrastructures, and material processes.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Reto Gubelmann. “Large Language Models, Agency, and Why Speech Acts Are Beyond Them (For Now)—A Kantian-Cum-Pragmatist Case.” *Philosophy & Technology* 37 (2024): 32. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00696-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Nuhu Osman Attah. “Do Language Models Lack Communicative Intentions?” *Synthese* 205 (2025): 187. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-025-05022-6?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Anna Puzio. “The Material Agency of a Large Language Model.” *Digital Society* 4 (2025): 73. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44206-025-00225-3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** Much of the disagreement turns on whether philosophical theories of agency are tracking a natural kind or fixing a normatively loaded status. Attah directly contests a premise that Gubelmann and related skeptics regard as crucial, while Puzio questions the shared individualistic framing. Meanwhile, increasingly agentic AI architectures—persistent goals, tool use, memory, multi-step planning—keep changing the empirical case to which these concepts are being applied. [<span class="underline">DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00696-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**4. What exactly are LLM “hallucinations”: bullshit, something weaker, or a category mistake?**

**(a) Open question.** What philosophical category best captures apparently authoritative LLM outputs produced without adequate sensitivity to their truth?

**(b) Main positions.** The **bullshit account** applies Frankfurtian truth-indifference to LLM production. A **conditional bullshit account** holds that particular systems can produce bullshit but that truth-indifference is not essential to LLMs and can be altered by architecture or guardrails. The **category-mistake objection** holds that bullshitting presupposes kinds of mentality or discursive agency LLMs lack.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Michael Townsen Hicks, James Humphries, and Joe Slater. “ChatGPT Is Bullshit.” *Ethics and Information Technology* 26 (2024): 38. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-024-09775-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Sarah A. Fisher. “Large Language Models and Their Big Bullshit Potential.” *Ethics and Information Technology* 26 (2024): 67. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-024-09802-5?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Jesse Fitts. “ChatGPT Is Not Bullshit, nor Is It Not Not Bullshit.” *Ethics and Information Technology* 28 (2026): 30. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-026-09903-3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** This is unusually clearly an active debate: Fitts’s 2026 paper explicitly intervenes in the exchange generated by Hicks, Humphries, and Slater. The difficulty is that descriptions such as “hallucinating,” “confabulating,” “lying,” and “bullshitting” differ precisely in the attitudes or intentions they presuppose, so the taxonomy partly inherits the unresolved agency and intentionality disputes above. Fisher additionally argues that the answer may vary from system to system rather than characterize LLMs essentially. [<span class="underline">Springer</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-024-09775-5?trk=article-ssr-frontend-pulse_x-social-details_comments-action_comment-text&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**5. Can an AI give testimony and thereby transmit knowledge?**

**(a) Open question.** When a conversational AI tells a user that *p*, can that interaction constitute testimony capable of generating testimonial knowledge?

**(b) Main positions.** A **traditionalist** position treats testimony as essentially tied to a speaker who can assert, undertake commitments, or be trusted, making LLM output testimony-like but not testimony. A **revisionary AI-testimony** position introduces a distinct artificial category without requiring humanlike agency. A stronger **artificial-testifier** position holds that LLM testimony can genuinely yield knowledge if it satisfies suitably revised epistemic norms such as reliability, veracity, or cautiousness.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Richard Heersmink, Barend de Rooij, María Jimena Clavel Vázquez, and Matteo Colombo. “A Phenomenology and Epistemology of Large Language Models: Transparency, Trust, and Trustworthiness.” *Ethics and Information Technology*26 (2024): 41. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-024-09777-3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Ori Freiman. “AI-Testimony, Conversational AIs and Our Anthropocentric Theory of Testimony.” *Social Epistemology*38, no. 4 (2024): 476–490. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02691728.2024.2316622?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Jinhua He and Chen Yang. “Testimony by LLMs.” *AI & Society* 40 (2025): 6201–6213. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02366-y?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** The technology creates a phenomenology strongly resembling interpersonal testimony while lacking an agreed-upon testifier behind the utterance. Hence the dispute presses directly on long-standing assumptions that testimony requires intention, accountability, trustworthiness, or interpersonal address. Freiman explicitly argues that ordinary theories are anthropocentric; He and Yang argue that artificial testimony can yield knowledge; Heersmink and colleagues emphasize the danger of mistaking testimonial appearance for the social-epistemic relation itself. [<span class="underline">Springer</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10676-024-09777-3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**6. What counts as a genuine explanation of an opaque AI system?**

**(a) Open question.** Should an AI explanation reveal its actual causal-mechanistic organization, merely provide information useful to a particular stakeholder, or sometimes be dispensable altogether?

**(b) Main positions.** **Pragmatic/pluralist XAI** makes explanatory adequacy relative to the question, stakeholder, and epistemic goal. **Mechanistic interpretability** seeks global or coordinated causal-functional understanding of internal organization rather than isolated post-hoc explanations. A **deflationary reliance view** questions whether explanation is needed for the forms of warranted reliance or “trust” appropriate to AI at all.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Oliver Buchholz. “A Means-End Account of Explainable Artificial Intelligence.” *Synthese* 202 (2023): 33. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04260-w?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Lena Kästner and Barnaby Crook. “Explaining AI through Mechanistic Interpretability.” *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 14 (2024): 52. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13194-024-00614-4?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Sam Baron. “Trust, Explainability and AI.” *Philosophy & Technology* 38 (2025): 4. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00837-6?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** “Explainability” bundles several different demands: understanding why an output occurred, discovering what a network has learned, enabling recourse, predicting failure, establishing safety, or legitimating a decision. Buchholz argues that different ends appropriately require different explanatory means; Kästner and Crook argue that safety motivates something closer to mechanistic understanding of the whole system; Baron challenges one of XAI’s common rationales by questioning whether the relevant forms of interpersonal trust belong here at all. [<span class="underline">Springer</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-023-04260-w?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**7. Can a human genuinely be friends with an AI companion?**

**(a) Open question.** Can a relationship with an AI companion instantiate genuine friendship or love rather than merely simulate their experiential benefits?

**(b) Main positions.** **Permissive/revisionary accounts** argue that human–AI friendship is possible under an appropriately flexible conception of friendship. **Reciprocity or welfare skeptics** maintain that friendship requires caring for a friend for that friend’s own sake, something impossible if an AI lacks a good of its own. A developing **teleological-interest response** argues that artifacts can possess enough interests, and humans can care for them in the relevant sense, to block at least that version of the skeptical argument.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Nick Munn and Dan Weijers. “Human–AI Friendship Is Possible and Can Be Good.” *Oxford Intersections: AI in Society*. Oxford University Press, 2025. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59762/chapter-abstract/508608124?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Micah Lott and William Hasselberger. “With Friends Like These: Love and Friendship with AI Agents.” *Topoi* (2025). [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11245-025-10247-8?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Matthew Kopec, Patrick McKee, and John Basl. “How to Care for Your AI Companion.” *Topoi* (2026). [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11245-026-10478-3?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** This literature is forming almost in real time: Kopec, McKee, and Basl’s September 2026 article directly challenges Lott and Hasselberger’s 2025 “good of its own” argument. The dispute depends simultaneously on theories of friendship and care, theories of artifact interests and welfare, and unresolved empirical questions about what increasingly persistent AI companions can do. [<span class="underline">OUP Academic</span>](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/59762/chapter-abstract/508608124?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**8. Who, if anyone, is the author of a generative-AI output?**

**(a) Open question.** When a work is produced through prompting a generative model, should authorship attach to the user, the AI, a larger sociotechnical collective, or sometimes to no one?

**(b) Main positions.** A **conservative agency account** denies authorship to AI because authorship requires intentions, assertions, responsibility, or analogous normative capacities. An **authorship-gap view** holds that some AI-assisted outputs genuinely have no author rather than defaulting to the human prompter. A **distributed or collective-creatorship view** treats creation as a matter of graded contributions distributed among users, models, developers, training-data producers, and others.

**(c) Three anchors.**

René van Woudenberg, Chris Ranalli, and Daniel Bracker. “Authorship and ChatGPT: A Conservative View.” *Philosophy & Technology* 37 (2024): 34. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00715-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Tamer Nawar. “Generative Artificial Intelligence and Authorship Gaps.” *American Philosophical Quarterly* 61, no. 4 (2024): 355–367. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.5406/21521123.61.4.05?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Donal Khosrowi, Finola Finn, and Elinor Clark. “Engaging the Many-Hands Problem of Generative-AI Outputs: A Framework for Attributing Credit.” *AI and Ethics* 5 (2025): 4495–4513; published online 2024. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-024-00440-7?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** Generative production breaks apart capacities normally bundled together in a human author: originating an idea, controlling execution, intentionally expressing content, bearing responsibility, and deserving credit. Different theories assign different weights to those capacities. Particularly telling is the contrast between Nawar’s possibility of an *authorship gap* and Khosrowi, Finn, and Clark’s proposal that creation belongs initially to a collective and admits of degrees. [<span class="underline">Springer</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00715-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**9. What would give an AI moral status or welfare interests?**

**(a) Open question.** Which capacities, if any, would make an artificial system something whose own good must be taken into account for its sake?

**(b) Main positions.** A **sentience/consciousness criterion** makes phenomenal consciousness, especially valenced experience, necessary or central to moral status. A **broader cognitive-interest position** allows sophisticated preferences, goals, or other cognitive capacities to ground at least some status even without sentience. A **good-of-its-own skepticism**allows AI moral patiency in principle but argues that machine learning and intelligent behavior provide little reason to think artifacts actually possess a welfare or good of their own.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Ali Ladak. “What Would Qualify an Artificial Intelligence for Moral Standing?” *AI and Ethics* 4 (2024): 213–228; published online January 25, 2023. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-023-00260-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Parisa Moosavi. “Will Intelligent Machines Become Moral Patients?” *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* 109, no. 1 (2024): 95–116; first published online September 9, 2023. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/phpr.13019?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Kestutis Mosakas. “Artificial Consciousness and Moral Personhood.” *Oxford Intersections: AI in Society*. Oxford University Press, 2025. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780198945215.003.0005?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** AI potentially separates properties that terrestrial cases usually bundle together: intelligence without sentience, apparent preferences without biological needs, agency without obvious welfare, and perhaps consciousness without organismic life. Ladak uses precisely such combinations to question sentience as a necessary condition; Mosakas defends phenomenal consciousness as necessary; Moosavi instead centers possession of a “good of one’s own” and doubts that ordinary advances in machine learning supply it. [<span class="underline">Springer</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-023-00260-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**10. What should AI “alignment” actually align systems with?**

**(a) Open question.** In a pluralistic society, what is the proper normative target of alignment: people’s preferences, justified principles or claims, socially negotiated role norms, or something else?

**(b) Main positions.** **Preferentist alignment** treats individual or aggregated preferences as the primary representation of human values—the approach underlying much RLHF-style practice. **Role- or norm-based alignment** holds that systems should instead satisfy norms appropriate to their social roles, negotiated among relevant stakeholders. **Contractualist/legitimacy approaches** ask which principles or decisions can be justified to affected persons, emphasizing fair treatment of claims or politically legitimate accommodation of disagreement.

**(c) Three anchors.**

Tan Zhi-Xuan, Micah Carroll, Matija Franklin, and Hal Ashton. “Beyond Preferences in AI Alignment.” *Philosophical Studies* 182 (2025): 1813–1863; published online November 9, 2024. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-024-02249-w?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Iason Gabriel and Geoff Keeling. “A Matter of Principle? AI Alignment as the Fair Treatment of Claims.” *Philosophical Studies* 182 (2025): 1951–1973. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://doi.org/10.1007%2Fs11098-025-02300-4?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Nick Schuster and Daniel Kilov. “Moral Disagreement and the Limits of AI Value Alignment: A Dual Challenge of Epistemic Justification and Political Legitimacy.” *AI & Society* 40 (2025): 6073–6087. [<span class="underline">paper / DOI</span>](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02427-2?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**(d) Why unsettled.** The technical phrase “value alignment” suppresses a prior normative problem: there is no uncontested object called “human values” waiting to be encoded. Preferences may be adaptive, ill-informed, inconsistent, or immoral; principles require controversial justification; stakeholder negotiation raises problems of representation and power; and reasonable moral disagreement may survive any procedure. The three papers above are notable because they move the alignment debate from “how do we learn the target?” toward the prior question “what could legitimately count as the target?” [<span class="underline">arXiv</span>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16984?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Taken together, these ten debates form several clusters. Debates 1–4 concern the **metaphysics and philosophy of mind/language of LLMs**; 5–6 concern **epistemic relations between humans and AI**; 7–9 concern whether familiar **social and moral statuses survive translation to artificial agents**; and 10 concerns the emerging **political and normative foundations of alignment**. The especially formative ones, judged by how recently the rival positions have crystallized and how directly papers are now replying to one another, are probably 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10. That is a characterization of the state of the literature rather than a claim about which side of any dispute is correct.
