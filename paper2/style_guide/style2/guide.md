# Form Guide for a Philosophy Journal Article

Based on 10 recent papers from leading general journals. Counts are out of 10.

## Opening and thesis
- No single opening dominates. Six open with framing, a quotation, a definition or background. Four open with a scene, vignette or case. One has an epigraph.
- Thesis placement varies. It comes in paragraph 1 in 1 paper, paragraph 2 in 3, paragraphs 4–5 in 3, and the last paragraph or two of a longer introduction in 3. At least 8 abstracts also state the thesis or aims.
- 8 give a roadmap, usually one paragraph of about 100–200 words right after the thesis. Five use section numbers. Two give none, only scattered forward references.
- Several papers state their limited aim up front, or set aside what they will not argue.

## Sections
- All 10 use numbered, descriptive headings, from 4 to 12 sections. Headings are rarely questions, and a few are playful. Seven have numbered subsections.
- Section lengths are uneven, from under a page to eight pages. Don't equalise them.
- 9 open by linking back to the last result or saying what the section will do ("Recall…", "So far…"). Most close with a one-line recap or a hand-off ("I turn now to…").

## Laying out arguments
- Prose carries the reasoning in 8 papers.
- 5 display a numbered premise–conclusion argument, and the other 5 never do. Those that do display it once and defend it premise by premise afterwards. One numbers every argument continuously, with lettered sub-steps.
- 8 set off named principles, conditions or definitions as indented blocks, then reuse the names as handles. Introducing a rival version in "first approximation" form and refining it is a common move.
- Heavy notation is rare: 2 papers use probabilistic or modal formalism. Only 1 sends proofs to appendices, leaving intuitive summaries in the body.

## Cases
- 9 use invented cases. 9 reuse a running or earlier case, often modified step by step or set in contrasting pairs.
- Cases are mostly introduced inline ("Consider…", "Suppose…"). Only 2–3 papers present labelled, indented, named case blocks.
- Borrowed cases are credited to their sources.

## Objections
- Only 1 paper has a dedicated objections section with replies in matching order. One more gives a single objection its own subsection.
- The rest answer objections where they arise, immediately, often introduced by a rhetorical question or "One might object…". Replies are frequently concessive ("Perhaps. But…").
- Ordinal runs ("First… Second…") are common. 4 papers give rival views a dedicated stretch.

## Prior work
- None has a stand-alone literature review. Prior work is woven into the text and notes. Two have an expository section that reviews the existing debate.
- Quotation with page references is used as evidence, then interpreted. Disagreement is direct but courteous. Credit is explicit.
- Long "see, e.g." lists go in footnotes. 4 cite the author's own earlier work. Several state that coverage is selective.

## Signposting
- All use it, and 9 use it heavily or moderately-to-heavily. Expect section-number cross-references, "Recall…", "as we will see", mid-section recaps ("In short…") and restatements of key claims.
- One paper signposts only locally.

## Voice
- 8 write mainly in the first person singular, mixed with an inclusive "we". The 2 co-authored papers use "we" throughout.
- 7 are plain or conversational, with idiom, rhetorical questions and some humour. 3 are formal and measured. Hedging is common. About 4 address the reader directly.

## Notes
- All 10 use footnotes, from 13 to 40. 8 have 22 or more.
- They carry citation lists, scope exclusions, side-objections and replies, referee-prompted refinements, and sometimes extended side-arguments.
- The main text should read without them.
- Acknowledgements sit in a final footnote in 6 papers and a short section in 3.

## Conclusion
- 8 end with a headed conclusion or closing remarks. 2 end inside the last section.
- Conclusions are short, typically 100–450 words, with the longest about 900.
- Most recap the thesis or results. 7 concede limits or non-conclusiveness. Most point to further work or a neighbouring question. 3 close on a crafted last line.
- Fewer than half add new material, and none opens a new line of argument.

## Rare in the sample
- Epigraphs (1), keyword lists (1), appendices (1), a separate objections section (1), a standalone literature review (0), and papers with no invented cases (1).
