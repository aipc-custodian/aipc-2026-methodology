Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, austin-jl, assertion — The theses reread Austin's misfire/abuse taxonomy against intention-based denials that LLMs perform speech acts, so the speech-acts debate and the Austin entry are the core, with assertion covering the insincere-assertion and Searle/Gricean framing.

Austin's A/B conditions make no demand for intention to perform an act, and sincerity-grade states fall under Γ (abuse). Intention-based denials therefore import a non-Austinian condition and owe an argument that apology, promise and similar act types are intention-constituted rather than procedure-constituted. For proxy-occupied roles the question is one of status; for an LLM acting in its own name it may reduce to standing that tracks mind-involving capacities.

What is left. Austin's taxonomy puts sincerity-grade states under Γ, so a denial that an LLM output is an apology or promise because it lacks intention or remorse needs a further premise that those act types are intention-constituted. The LLM literature I could see supplies intention-based (Gubelmann, Magnus et al.) or norm-based (Butlin–Viebahn, Skiba) premises, and none of the snippets or abstracts engages Γ directly. The thesis still has to answer three things: the reading on which Austin presupposes agency outside the infelicity taxonomy, the point that a system with no capacity for sincerity cannot be 'insincere', and the Strawson/Bach–Harnish view that non-ceremonial acts are intention-constituted. No work seen anticipated the thesis, but I could read the full text of none of them, so that is a statement about what I saw and not about the whole literature.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Austin treats sincerity failure as an 'abuse', not a 'misfire'. The act is still performed but is infelicitous, so sincerity-grade states fall outside the conditions for the act's existence. — Austin, as reported by the entry (Green)

Here I have promised all right, but the act is not felicitous because it is not sincere. My act is, more precisely, an abuse because although it is a speech act, it fails to live up to a standard appropriate for speech acts of its kind.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Austin's felicity conditions are procedural. An accepted conventional procedure, executed by the right persons in the right circumstances, is what makes the act. Searle follows in a weaker form, allowing some acts without constitutive rules. — Austin 1962; Searle 1969 ('force conventionalism')

There must exist an accepted conventional procedure having a certain conventional effect, that procedure to include the uttering of certain words by certain persons in certain circumstances…

SUPPORTED (1/5) — For status-bound acts, what matters is social standing or authority and uptake, not the speaker's mental state. Strawson's intentionalism itself concedes that conventions suffice for appointing and excommunicating. — Austin; Searle and Vanderveken (preparatory conditions); Strawson (concession)

a person cannot marry a couple unless she is legally invested with the authority to do so.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — The entry's own account defines speech acts through speaker meaning and overt commitment. It argues that conventions cannot substitute for the intention to put a sentence forth as an assertion. It says it is hard to see how one could perform a speech act with no intention of performing a speech act at all. This is a rival intention-based account, but it does not conclusively close off the thesis. — Strawson 1964; Green (entry author, 1997, 2013); Grice-style speaker meaning

Just as no convention could make it the case that I believe that P, so too no convention could make it the case that I intend to put forth a certain sentence as an assertion.

ADJACENT (3/5) — The somniloquist case, the point that speech acts need speaker meaning, and the 'trying to ask' cases show that the words plus a procedure are not always enough. The speaker must also intentionally undertake a publicly accessible commitment. — Green (entry author)

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment

PRECEDENT · ENTERTAINED (2/5) — A society could have a convention that uttering certain expressions constitutes an illocutionary act, even on stage or in jokes. This is a procedure-constitutes-act position. Green replies that it does not work for assertion, which needs intentions. — Hare 1989 (reply to Davidson); Green 1997 (rejoinder)

there could be a society with a convention that utterance of a certain expression constituted performance of a certain illocutionary act

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speaker meaning need not involve intentions to affect an addressee (infants, photocopiers, photos). Speaker meaning is instead construed as overtly manifesting one's commitments or state of mind. This bears on whether mind-involving capacities are what matter for non-human speakers. — Davis 1992; Green 2019

Davis (1992) provides a range of cases such as speaking to pre-linguistic infants, uncooperative photocopy machines, and photos of deceased loved ones.

Nearest positions. Force conventionalism: acts are constituted by conventional procedure and constitutive rules, with sincerity as a separate matter of abuse — Austin (strong), Searle (weaker); Intentionalism: acts are explained by manifest communicative intentions, with conventions only for special institutional cases — Strawson 1964, with Grice-style speaker meaning; Green's overt-commitment account: a speech act is performed by overtly undertaking a norm-governed commitment, either by invoking a convention or by manifesting an intention — Green (the entry's author)

The entry is silent on. LLMs and other artificial speakers, proxy-occupied roles, and any 'standing' that tracks mind-involving capacities. It also never labels Austin's conditions A/B/Γ and never says that Austin's conditions omit an intention-to-perform requirement. It does not frame intention-based denials of apology or promise as importing a non-Austinian condition.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — austin-jl

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry reports Austin's two kinds of infelicity. Misfires are failures of procedure, persons or circumstances, in which the act is not achieved. Abuses are insincere performances in which the act is performed. This matches the thesis's split between A/B conditions and Γ (sincerity-grade states). — Austin 1962b, as reported by the entry

Second, there are abuses: in these cases, the act is performed, but insincerely, perhaps for example in instituting a marriage of convenience.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry's examples of misfires are all procedural or status failures (wrong formula, already married, wrong officiant), not failures of intention. This fits the 'procedure-constituted' reading of act types. — Austin 1962b: 15–16, as reported by the entry

…if we…utter the formula incorrectly, or if…we are not in a position to do the act because we are…married already, or it is the purser and not the captain who is conducting the ceremony, then the act in question, …marrying, is not successfully performed at all

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry credits Austin with a general challenge to simple sentence–act and sentence–truth pairings, and says the same sentence can perform several acts at once. A referee could use this against any rigid claim that an act type is constituted by one condition, whether procedure or intention. — Austin, as reported by the entry

the possibility that we might sometimes do more than one thing in using a performative puts pressure on the idea that there is a simple connection between sentences and the various things we do in using them.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry lists intention-oriented speech-act theorists (Bach and Harnish, Searle, Schiffer, Strawson on illocutionary acts, Hornsby) only as references, with no description of their views. They are the likely source of the 'intention-based denials' the thesis targets. — Bach and Harnish 1979; Searle 1968, 1969; Schiffer 1972; Strawson 1964a; Hornsby 1988

(For discussion of Austin’s distinction amongst locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts see Bach 1975; Bach and Harnish 1979; … Searle 1968, 1969; Strawson 1964a, 1973; …)

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry flags that Austin separates serious from non-serious uses of language, and that Derrida and Searle disputed the priority this gives. This is the closest the entry comes to discussing what makes an utterance count as a genuine act. The entry sets it aside. — Austin 1962b: 104; Derrida 1977; Searle 1977

Jacques Derrida challenged the standing of the distinction and the priority that Austin seemed to accord to some of what he counted as serious uses.

Nearest positions. Austin's misfire/abuse distinction: misfires void the act, abuses (insincerity) leave it performed — Austin 1962b, as reported in the entry; Austin's rejection of a simple constative/performative sorting, with the illocutionary act as the primary object of assessment — Austin, as reported in the entry; Intention-oriented speech-act theory (Searle, Bach and Harnish, Schiffer, Strawson) — named only in the entry's bibliography, not described

The entry is silent on. Nearly everything specific to the thesis. The entry does not use the A/B/Γ labels or say that A/B impose no intention requirement. It does not discuss whether apologising or promising is intention-constituted or procedure-constituted, or any 'intention-based denial' of speech acts. It says nothing about proxy-occupied roles, LLMs or artificial agents acting in their own name, or standing that tracks mind-involving capacities.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — On Austin's own treatment, insincerity (the lacking state or intention) is an infelicity of the 'abuse' kind. It does not stop the act from being performed. The entry also lists flaw-type misexecutions and misinvocations as separate infelicity types. — Austin (1962), as reported by the entry

an assertion is insincere in case of lying as a promise is insincere when the appropriate intention is lacking (Austin 1962 [1975: 40]). This is an infelicity of the abuse kind.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Austin held that illocutionary acts are conventional, that is, they can be made explicit by the performative formula. On his view the act is performed once the procedure is invoked and uptake is secured, which is a procedure-constituted picture. — Austin; Dummett; Searle (1969); Kot'átko; Kölbel

Austin held that illocutionary acts as opposed to perlocutionary acts are conventional, in the sense that they can be made explicit by the so-called performative formula

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Austin's felicity conditions, and the idea that a failed presupposition makes an act void in the way a wrong legal formula does, are a broader view that the thesis applies to the intention-based denial question. The entry does not draw that application. — Austin (1962), as reported by the entry

an infelicity of the same kind—flaw-type misexecutions—as the use of the wrong formula in a legal procedure

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Rival, intention-based accounts treat illocutionary types as constituted by communicative or reflexive intentions, not by procedure. Strawson says convention plays little role for ordinary illocutionary types, and Stainton says convention is not necessary for assertion. Davidson and Bach & Harnish say convention is not sufficient. — Strawson (1964); Searle (1969); Bach & Harnish (1979); Grice; Davidson; Stainton

If Strawson and Stainton are right, convention isn't necessary for making assertions.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry leaves open whether intention is needed at all for assertion. It also notes that Austin's uptake requirement is less plausible for speaker–content acts like asserting, since Bill can assert that p even though Lisa left the room. — The entry's own question; Austin on uptake

must he intend it to be an assertion? Must it be made voluntarily?

ADJACENT (2/5) — Commitment accounts treat assertion as a normative status taken on (accountability, liability to challenge). This is the nearest thing in the entry to the thesis's 'standing' and 'status'. They are not tied to proxies or to machines. — Peirce; Brandom; Alston; Green; Searle

Making an assertion has normative consequences.

Nearest positions. Communicative-intention analyses of illocutionary acts (reflexive intentions) — Grice, Strawson (1964), Searle (1969), Bach & Harnish (1979), with Sperber & Wilson's criticism; Convention-based accounts of illocutionary force versus the objections that convention is neither necessary nor sufficient — Austin, Dummett, Searle, Kölbel versus Strawson, Stainton, Davidson, Bach & Harnish; Commitment or status accounts of assertion — Peirce, Brandom, Alston, Green, Searle

The entry is silent on. The entry says nothing about LLMs or other artificial speakers, proxy-occupied roles, or 'standing' tied to mind-involving capacities. It does not discuss apology, and it never uses Austin's A/B/Γ labelling. It does not argue that denials of speech-act performance for lack of intention import a non-Austinian condition. It names only promising and asserting as acts whose constitution is disputed.
