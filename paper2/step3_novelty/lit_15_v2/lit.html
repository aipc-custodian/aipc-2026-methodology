This check can show that something HAS been argued. It cannot show that nothing has. Read `not found` as carefully as the findings — a clean result means only that these searches came back empty, and much of the literature is paywalled and invisible to it.

Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: assertion, speech-acts, computing-responsibility — The thesis concerns the norms of assertion (belief/knowledge) and whether proxy speech by deployers of LLMs counts as assertion; the speech-acts entry covers commitment and conditional speech acts, and computing-responsibility covers the neighbouring question of whom to hold responsible for machine outputs.

If assertion is governed by an epistemic-position norm, then deployers of unreviewed generative systems do not assert the outputs. What they do is endorse a process or take on a guarantee. The thesis covers all unreviewed automated output whose content is fixed by a transducer rather than the principal's state, and it implies that much ordinary institutional 'telling' is not assertion.

What is left. Nothing I found meets the ANTICIPATED standard, so no part of the thesis has to move. The rival views must be answered, though. Green & Michel and Arora hold that deployers or designers can proxy-assert machine output. The thesis's conditional claim therefore survives only if it shows that those accounts transmit commitment to a content the principal has no means of identifying. It must also show why a reliability-level commitment is not already what they mean by 'epistemic responsibility'. It also has to say how it differs from Butlin & Viebahn's conduit/non-conduit distinction. I could not read that distinction in full.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Norm-of-assertion literature supplies the premise: assertion is governed by an epistemic norm (knowledge, justification, belief, truth) relating the speaker's epistemic position to the content. The thesis's conditional can build on this. — Williamson (KNA); Lackey, Douven, Kvanvig (JNA); Bach, Hindriks (BNA)

Williamson (1996, 2000) initiated this debate by proposing that assertion is governed by a single norm... One must: assert p only if one knows p.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Commitment accounts treat assertion as taking on accountability or guarantee for the content. The thesis's contrast between 'endorse a process / take on a guarantee' and asserting echoes this, but the entry treats guaranteeing as constitutive of assertion, not as an alternative to it. — Peirce; Alston; Brandom; Watson; Moran; Hinchman; Carson

some authors write that assertions guarantee (Peirce; Watson 2004: 66), assure (Moran 2005, Hinchman 2013) or warrant (Carson 2006) that their content is true.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Rescorla denies any norm governs the making of assertions; Greenberg denies a sui generis norm; Cappelen calls assertion a defective theoretical category. These deny the thesis's antecedent or block the inference from norm to non-assertion. — Rescorla 2009a; Greenberg 2020; Cappelen 2020

Rescorla (2009a), who defends a commitment account of assertion... argues there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions.

ADJACENT (4/5) — The norm literature separates being subject to a norm from conforming to it: an assertion that violates the norm is still an assertion. A referee will press this against a thesis saying a deployer who lacks the epistemic position does not assert, rather than asserts improperly. — Williamson; Goldberg; Montminy (Individuation assumption)

it is being subject to the norm that characterizes assertion, not conforming to the norm. An assertion that violates the norm is still an assertion.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Selfless assertions, where a speaker states content she does not believe, for example a teacher reporting consensus. These cases show that a gap between the speaker's own state and the content can be compatible with proper assertion. — Lackey 2007; Montminy; Turri; Milić

Intuitively, it would be appropriate for Stella, as a teacher, to assert (31). However, (KNA) predicts that (31) is incorrect

ADJACENT (2/5) — Testimonial and transmission-focused norms: assertion is tied to the hearer's entitlement to believe and to the speaker authorizing the hearer to defer challenges back to the speaker. Relevant to whether an institution can be the speaker. — Goldberg 2015; García-Carpintero 2004 (KNA-T)

the speaker authorizes the hearer to defer back to the speaker the responsibility of meeting challenges to the assertion.

PRECEDENT · PASSING (1/5) — Indirect and non-explicit assertion, and the question whether an utterance whose content is not what the speaker says counts as assertion proper. This is only loosely related to the thesis, which concerns who the speaker is, not how the content is expressed. — Fricker 2012; García-Carpintero 2016

One question is whether an utterance is an assertion proper that p if that content is not exactly what is expressed

Nearest positions. Knowledge/justification/belief norm of assertion (Williamson; Lackey; Douven; Bach), which supplies the 'epistemic-position' antecedent; Commitment accounts on which asserting is taking on accountability or a guarantee (Peirce, Alston, Brandom, Watson), which absorb the thesis's 'guarantee' alternative into assertion; Selfless-assertion cases and the subject-to versus conform-to distinction (Lackey; Williamson/Goldberg), which block the move from lacking the epistemic position to not asserting

The entry is silent on. Automated, machine-generated, or transducer-produced output and non-human speakers. The entry also does not discuss institutional or corporate speakers, deployers or principals, or the question whether someone who relays or endorses another process's output asserts it. It does not discuss a distinction between asserting and endorsing a process.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Assertion is distinguished from conjecture by an epistemic norm: assertion purports to manifest knowledge and exposes the speaker to the challenge 'How do you know?', with an obligation to retract if it cannot be met. This is the epistemic-position norm the thesis takes as its antecedent. — Williamson 1996; Green 2017; Green's own account in §5.3

One who asserts P lays herself open to the challenge, “How do you know that?”, and she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Speech acts require overt, intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment, and speaker meaning is a necessary condition for non-conventional speech acts. This gives a route to denying that a deployer asserts content they did not mean or commit to. — Green (§§4.3, 5.3); Grice; Bertolet 2017

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

ADJACENT (2/5) — Utterances of sentences without speech act performance: the entry says an utterance of a sentence is not sufficient for a speech act (the somniloquist), and that Davidson's joker, storyteller or actor can produce an utterance without asserting. This is a near neighbour for the claim that producing words does not amount to asserting them. — Green; Davidson 1979

while talking in my sleep I might say, “I hereby promise to climb the Eiffel Tower,” without thereby making any promise.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Conventionalist accounts on which some speech acts (appointing, excommunicating) are performed by invoking institutional conventions. A referee could say institutional 'telling' is performed by convention and so is not governed by the epistemic norm, or is assertion by convention. — Austin; Searle 1969; Strawson 1964 (allows special-case conventions)

This position is compatible with holding that in special cases linguistic communities have instituted conventions for particular speech acts such as appointing and excommunicating.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Performatives and assertion: some accounts hold that performative utterances are assertions, others that they are declarations or illocutionary-convention based, which is a neighbouring case of taking on a commitment ('guarantee') by saying. — Ginet 1979; Bach and Harnish; Reimer 1995; Searle and Vanderveken 1985

Searle and Vanderveken (1985) characterize performatives as speech acts having the force of declarations.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Commitment can attach to a content without the content having been asserted, so a deployer may be committed (by guarantee) without having asserted. — Green on illocutionary validity

That is not to say that I have also asserted R : if assertion were closed under deductive consequence I would assert infinitely many things just by virtue of asserting one.

Nearest positions. Knowledge/'How do you know?' norm distinguishing assertion from conjecture — Williamson 1996, Green 2017; Speech acts as overt, intentional undertakings of commitment grounded in speaker meaning — Green, Grice, Strawson 1964; Convention-based speech acts and performatives as declarations or illocutionary conventions — Austin, Searle, Searle and Vanderveken, Reimer

The entry is silent on. Automated, machine-generated, or delegated output: who is the speaker when content is fixed by a transducer, agent-based or institutional speaking on behalf of a principal, and whether corporate or institutional 'telling' counts as assertion. The entry does not apply the epistemic norm to deployers, nor discuss the epistemic-position norm as applied to non-human producers (the nearest it comes is Davis 1992's mention of speaking to photocopy machines, which concerns audiences, not producers).

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — computing-responsibility

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry's nearest topic is responsibility for automated outputs: developers and deployers are responsible for the ethical implications of what their systems do in context, because they know the design decisions and choose to deploy. This is an accountability-for-systems view, not a speech-act or assertion view. — Martin 2019; Gotterbarn 2001 (positive responsibility)

developers and the companies that decide to sell a computer technology into a particular context are responsible for the ethical implication of the use of these technologies in that context

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Artifacts inscribe their designers' and users' intentions, so output content is shaped by the system and not simply by the principal's current state. Content is fixed partly by the mediating artifact. — Verbeek; Johnson; Johnson and Powers

The software has inscribed in its design the various intentions of the developers, managers and clients; it is poised to behave, given a particular input, according to their ideas

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Opacity and automation bias mean that users and deployers do not understand or check the outputs they rely on. This supports the 'unreviewed' and 'no epistemic position' premise. — Angwin et al. 2016 (judges and the risk-assessment tool); Parasuraman & Riley; Cummings

The judges were basing their sentencing on the suggestion of an algorithm that they did not fully understand.

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Responsibility is distributed among humans and technologies, and accountability is a practice that is tied to organizational context and not to individual mental states. This is a broader view into which 'endorsing a process or guaranteeing' could fit as an application, but the entry does not draw that application. — Nissenbaum 1994/1997; Johnson and Powers 2005; Floridi 2016

Moral responsibility from this perspective is not located in an individual or an interpersonal relationship, but is distributed among humans and technologies.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Floridi and Sanders separate accountability from responsibility, so an agent can be answerable for an action without being responsible in the full sense. Structurally this resembles taking a guarantee without making an assertion. — Floridi and Sanders 2004

artificial agents should be acknowledged as moral agents that can be held accountable, but not responsible

Nearest positions. Developers and deployers bear responsibility for the ethical implications of a system's outputs in context — Martin 2019; Gotterbarn 2001; Responsibility is distributed across human and technological components and organizations, with accountability as a practice — Nissenbaum; Johnson and Powers; Floridi 2016; Accountability can be decoupled from responsibility for artificial agents — Floridi and Sanders 2004

The entry is silent on. Assertion, speech acts, epistemic-position or knowledge norms of assertion, and whether deployers 'tell' or 'say' anything through generative output. The entry covers only moral responsibility, and its few mentions of 'information' concern content moderation and platform liability (the Google/YouTube case), not testimony or assertion. Generative language systems and the claim that institutional 'telling' is not assertion go unaddressed.
