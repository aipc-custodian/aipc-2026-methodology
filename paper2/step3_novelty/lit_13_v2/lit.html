Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: kant-aesthetics, collective-intentionality, speech-acts — The theses turn on Kant's natural-purpose (organism) criterion, which 'Kant, Immanuel: aesthetics and teleology' covers, on whether a whole such as a firm can hold intentions and act as a collective author, and on what it takes to perform a speech act.

Gubelmann's Kantian criterion has no principled stopping point at the skin without biological chauvinism. Applied to maintained human–LLM pipelines, it makes the LLM an organ of a larger goal-setting whole, so 'LLMs cannot speak' reduces to 'parts as such do not speak'. The whole may be a speaker, with authorship layered across levels and fixed by a separate attribution rule. A distinct third kind of author is not established.

What is left. Granting Gubelmann's Kantian criterion and his concession that LLMs have a first step of cognitive autonomy, the criterion does not by itself say whether the relevant individual is the skin-bounded organism or a self-maintaining whole such as a firm. Excluding wholes needs an independent individuation principle, and the autopoietic tradition is the obvious candidate. The thesis owes a defence that maintained human–LLM pipelines in firms meet that principle, or that it is itself biologically parochial. It must also distinguish its view from proxy accounts that already allow organizational or collective principals: the claim is that the LLM is an organ of the speaking whole, not a proxy relaying a principal's intention. On that basis, 'LLMs cannot speak' reduces to 'parts as such do not speak', and the whole's speakerhood plus an attribution rule do the remaining work.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — kant-aesthetics

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Kant's notion of an organism as a 'natural purpose' is the Kantian machinery a criterion like Gubelmann's would draw on. The entry gives the definition and the claim that only organized beings meet it. The text I received breaks off just as it begins to explain what makes an organism a natural purpose, so the part-whole (organ) analysis is not visible to me. — Kant (CJ §§64–65), as reported by the entry

organized beings ... instantiate the concept of a natural purpose and also ... they are the only beings in nature that do so (§65, 376)

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Kant's distinction between inner and outer/relative objective purposiveness. Organisms have inner purposiveness; one thing standing in a means-end relation to another has only relative purposiveness. This is a resource for treating an LLM as a means or part within a larger goal-directed whole. — Kant; treated in detail by Marc-Wogau (1938) and Tonelli (1958), per the entry

this distinguishes the kind of purposiveness possessed by organisms from that in virtue of which one natural thing or process stands in a means-end relation to another

ADJACENT (2/5) — Purposiveness analysed as conceptually guided design. Artifacts are the paradigm case of purpose, and Teufel reads the ascription of purposiveness as an ontological claim about a rational, conceptually guided origin. This bears on whether a maintained human–LLM pipeline counts as a purposive whole. — Kant §10; Teufel (2011); Ginsborg 1997b (normative lawfulness) as a rival reading

an ascription of purposiveness to an object makes an ontological claim to the effect that the object is the outcome of a rational, conceptually guided process

ADJACENT (1/5) — Nature as a system of purposes and the human being or culture as ultimate purpose. This is a nearby Kantian whole-and-parts framework, though it concerns nature and morality, not speakers or authors. — Kant §§3.5–3.6; Vaccarino Bremner (forthcoming); Gardner (2016)

the human being, as a free and rational agent, is the "ultimate purpose" [Endzweck] of nature

Nearest positions. Organisms as natural purposes, the only beings that meet the concept (inner objective material purposiveness) — Kant CJ §§64–65, as reported in the entry; Purposiveness as an etiological claim of conceptually guided origin — Teufel (2011), against purposiveness as normative lawfulness (Ginsborg 1997b) or unity of diversity (Zuckert 2007a); Inner versus relative (means-end) purposiveness — Kant, treated by Marc-Wogau (1938) and Tonelli (1958)

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — collective-intentionality

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Agency is settled by functional conditions, not by biological or individual-organism boundaries. A whole can meet those conditions as a unit, with attitudes that are not those of its parts. This gives the thesis its 'no stopping point at the skin' step and its claim that the whole may be the speaker or author. — Pettit (and List & Pettit): groups fulfil conditions of agency 'as collective units of agency whose attitudes may be discontinuous with the attitudes of their members'. Tollefsen and Rovane: rational unity and the intentional stance for groups.

such groups fulfill them as collective units of agency whose attitudes may be discontinuous with the attitudes of their members

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Subjecthood needs an internal structure that makes the whole a proper target of ascription, such as decision mechanisms and roles. This matches the thesis's 'maintained pipelines' and the 'separate attribution rule' fixing authorship. — Pettit (collectivising reason, authoritative majority votes, straw votes); Tuomela (group positions, authority system); Hindriks's status account; French

a group that is to count as a subject of intentional states has to implement an internal structure and in particular a mechanism for decision-making

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Intentions at one level derive from intentions at a higher level, so the same act can be attributed at several layers. Roles within a group are functional positions. This is a broader view that the thesis's 'authorship layered across levels' and 'parts as such' would apply. — Sellars (we-derivative I-referential intentions); Tuomela (members 'functioning in group positions')

My intention to move a pawn on the chessboard is derivative from our intention to play a game of chess.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — The Individual Ownership Claim and the internalist and naturalist versions hold that all intentionality is the individual's own and settled by features inside individual minds. A rule fixing the skin as the boundary is thereby defended. The thesis must answer this rival rather than just accuse it of chauvinism. — Searle (intentionality 'inside individuals' heads'); the Individual Ownership Thesis as the entry presents it; Collingwood on society having 'no will but the will of its members'

all the intentionality an individual has is their own (the Individual Ownership Claim)

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Group agency or authorship is merely metaphorical shorthand for members' attitudes. The thesis's claim that 'the whole may be a speaker' faces this deflationary view. — Quinton (1975); Tuomela (groups are agents 'only in a metaphorical sense')

groups can but need not be taken as (singular) entities, and they are agents and persons only in a metaphorical sense

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry notes an alternative to group-as-subject: two or more individuals may jointly form the subject. It also lists Gilbert's neutrality about whether a 'single body' is composed of human beings. These bear on whether the thesis's 'whole' is a distinct kind of subject or a plural arrangement. — Smith 2011; Gilbert 2006; Rovane

the concept of a 'single body' is neutral with respect to the question whether the body in question is in some sense composed of individual human beings

Nearest positions. Group agent as a subject in its own right, with attitudes discontinuous with its members — Pettit / List & Pettit (with Tollefsen's interpretationism and Rovane's rational unity); Individualist and reductionist ontology: groups are agents only metaphorically, and all intentionality is individually owned — Tuomela, Quinton, Searle, Collingwood; Layered, we-derivative intentions and structured group positions — Sellars and Tuomela (we-mode groups as collective artefacts), with Hindriks's status account of corporate agents

The entry is silent on. LLMs, AI systems, or any artefact or tool as part of an agent. Authorship or speaking as opposed to intending. Kant or Gubelmann's criterion. Biological chauvinism. Human–machine pipelines or maintenance. Treating a component as an 'organ' without intentionality of its own. All of the entry's parts are human members.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speech acts depend on the speaker's communicative intentions: the intention must be efficacious and publicly accessible. On Green's view, performing a speech act means overtly committing oneself to a content. This is the sort of requirement a text-generating system would be tested against, and the entry never applies it to machines or to composite agents. — Strawson 1964 (intentionalism); Green 2013, 2019 (overt commitment); Grice (speaker meaning)

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry's view of speaker meaning is not limited to humans or to a particular biology. Davis's cases (infants, photocopiers, photographs) show speaker meaning does not need ordinary addressee-directed effects. Searle allows a dog to request. Neither case settles whether non-biological or composite systems can speak. — Davis 1992; Searle 1969, p. 39

Searle considers the case of a dog requesting to be let outside (1969, p. 39).

SUPPORTED (1/5) — A speech act can be performed by means of other speech acts, and institutional and conventional acts get their force from authority, role and uptake. This gives the thesis materials for a separate attribution rule that is not fixed by the physical performer alone. — Searle and Vanderveken 1985 (strong illocutionary commitment); Austin (felicity conditions); Reimer 1995 (illocutionary conventions)

Even in such a case she performs S n only by virtue of intentionally performing some other set of speech acts

ADJACENT (1/5) — Audience-relative manifestness: what a speaker speaker-means can differ across parts of the audience. This shows the entry's willingness to relativise speaker meaning, though not to relativise authorship across levels of a composite agent. — Saul 2018 as discussed by Green

What is manifest to one addressee may not be manifest to another, and a speaker may exploit this fact.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Utterance alone, even of a performative sentence, does not make a speech act, as the sleep-talking case shows. This undercuts a purely output-based test of speakerhood and so fits the thesis's turn to a goal-setting whole, but the entry offers no test of its own. — Green (the entry's author)

neither a sentence, nor even the utterance of a sentence, is sufficient on its own for the performance of a speech act

Nearest positions. Intentionalism: speech acts require overtly manifested communicative intentions and commitment (Strawson 1964; Green 2013, 2019; Grice 1957); Force conventionalism: speech acts depend on conventions or constitutive rules, which would let institutional or role-based attribution rules do the work (Austin 1962; Searle 1969; Reimer 1995 on illocutionary conventions); Davis 1992 and Searle's dog: speaker meaning and requesting without the standard human audience-directed intentions or constitutive rules

The entry is silent on. Gubelmann and any Kantian criterion of agency; LLMs or any machine as speaker or author; human–machine pipelines; composite, group or corporate speakers and the 'organ/whole' relation; authorship layered across levels; a 'third kind of author'; and the charge of biological chauvinism. The entry never takes up who the speaker is when parts contribute to an utterance.
