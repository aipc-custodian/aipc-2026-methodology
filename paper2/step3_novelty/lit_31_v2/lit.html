Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, intention, assertion — The theses concern illocutionary force and communicative intention in LLMs versus humans, so speech-acts is the core debate, intention covers the force-fixing intention claim, and assertion covers commitment, ownership and retraction of earlier outputs.

Human illocutionary force is often settled incrementally through uptake and later ownership, so a force-fixing intention present at output time cannot by itself be what separates human speakers from LLMs. Whether LLMs show reason-responsive taking-up of and commitment to their earlier outputs remains open, and it is a condition on how the system is organised.

What is left. That human illocutionary force is often settled by later interaction is already argued by McDonald (provisional, from abstract and intro snippets) and is supported by conversation-analytic work. What remains is the claim that this undercuts the use of an output-time force-fixing intention as the human/LLM dividing line in the Gubelmann, Attah and Barandiaran–Almendros discussions. The thesis would argue that the speaker's own taking-up, owning and retracting of earlier outputs is constitutive of force, and that whether LLM threads show this pattern is an open, organisation-level question about interaction structure. I did not find that application in any work I could see.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Success of some speech acts depends on audience uptake, so force is not fixed by the speaker's output-time state alone. Austin's felicity conditions are reported with the example of a bet that misfires without uptake. — Austin; Langton, Hornsby and Langton (via the silencing discussion)

Other attempts at speech acts might misfire because their addressee fails to respond with an appropriate uptake: I cannot bet you $100 on who will win the election unless you accept that bet.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — A speaker can later retract or withdraw an earlier speech act, and so is no longer beholden to its commitments. This is a form of post-hoc ownership or disowning of one's earlier act. — Sbisà 2007, as reported in the entry

on Wednesday I may be able to retract a claim I made on Monday... I am now no longer beholden to the commitments that the speech acts engender

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Force is characterised by the normative commitments a speaker overtly undertakes, such as being answerable to 'How do you know?' and being obliged to retract if unable to respond. This is a reason-responsive, commitment-based account that the thesis's 'reason-responsive taking-up of and commitment to' can build on. — Green (the entry's own view); Williamson 1996 on challenges

One who asserts P lays herself open to the challenge, 'How do you know that?', and she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Force is fixed by the speaker's manifested communicative intention at the time of utterance (Strawson's intentionalism), and speaker meaning is a necessary condition for non-conventional speech acts. This is a rival to the claim that force is often settled incrementally rather than by an output-time intention. — Strawson 1964; Green; Bertolet 2017

What makes an utterance of a sentence in the indicative mood a prediction rather than a command, for instance, is that it manifests an intention to be so taken

ADJACENT (2/5) — Some deny that refusal requires uptake, treating it like inviting or surrendering, which can occur whether or not the audience grasps or accepts them. This limits how general an uptake condition on force can be. — Bird 2002; Maitra 2009

Bird (2002) denies that the speech act of refusal requires uptake.

PRECEDENT · PASSING (1/5) — A speech act can be performed by way of other acts without a distinct intention to perform it, so force need not always trace to one force-fixing intention at output time. — Entry's own discussion of illocutionary logic (building on Searle and Vanderveken's strong commitment)

Even in such a case she performs S n only by virtue of intentionally performing some other set of speech acts

Nearest positions. Intentionalism about force: force is fixed by the manifested communicative intention, and speaker meaning (overt commitment) is necessary for speech acts — Strawson 1964, Green, Bertolet; Force as overt normative commitment (assertion answerable to 'How do you know?', obligation to retract), with retraction possible later — Green, Williamson 1996, Sbisà 2007; Austin/Searle conventionalism with uptake as a felicity condition, including the silencing literature — Austin, Searle, Langton, Hornsby and Langton, versus Bird 2002 who denies uptake is required for refusal

The entry is silent on. Large language models and machine or AI speakers generally, and the contrast between human and artificial speakers. The entry also does not discuss force being settled incrementally across turns, the idea that later taking-up and ownership of an output fixes its force, or reason-responsiveness to one's own earlier outputs as a condition on the speaker's organisation. Nor does it mention any account on which the human/machine difference lies in system organisation instead of in intention at output time.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — intention

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Intention need not be formed before the act. There is intention in action, formed as one acts, as well as prospective intention. The thesis's point that a prior force-fixing intention is not required can build on this. — Searle 1983, as reported in the entry; the entry also cites Bratman 2000 for 'intentional action without intention'

I can wave my arm intentionally without planning in advance. This fact elicits a refinement often credited to Searle (1983, pp. 84–5): the distinction between prospective intention and intention in action.

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Intending is not a static state present at one moment. It is being in progress towards completing an act, so intentional status can be fixed over time. The thesis's incremental settling of force would apply this kind of view, but the entry never applies it to speech or to later ownership. — Thompson 2008; Anscombe-inspired writers (Moran and Stone 2009, Ferrero 2017, Russell 2018)

intending to do A is not a mental state because it is not static; instead it is a form of imperfectivity or being in progress towards the intentional completion of an act

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Intention is marked by functional organisation: commitment, resistance to reconsideration, and means-end coherence over time. This backs the thesis's claim that the relevant condition concerns how the system is organised and not a momentary mental state. — Bratman 1987, 2009, 2018; the entry also reports the Aristotelian 'commitment' point

intentions are ‘conduct-controlling pro-attitudes, ones which we are disposed to retain without reconsideration, and which play a significant role as inputs to [means-end] reasoning’

ADJACENT (2/5) — Acting for a reason involves the agent's having and answering the 'Why?' question about what she is doing, and knowing the reason-giving explanation. This is close to reason-responsive taking-up of one's earlier doings, and a referee would raise it for the thesis's 'ownership' condition. — Anscombe 1963; Hampshire 1959; Velleman; Setiya; Rödl

in acting for a reason, I know an explanation of what I am doing that cites that reason, and therefore know that I am doing it. That is why, for Anscombe, the question ‘Why?’ is ‘given application’ by the agent.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — On the orthodox 'Simple View', intentional action requires an intention (in action) whose object is the act, present as the act is done. That would make a time-of-output intention a necessary condition. The entry also reports that the view is open to serious objections, so this is a rival to answer and not a refutation. — Searle's refinement, which the entry reports as 'orthodox in action theory'; Bratman names it 'the Simple View' and rejects it

This refinement preserves the idea that doing A intentionally requires an intention whose object is doing A. This is what Bratman (1987, p. 112) has dubbed ‘the Simple View.’

Nearest positions. Intending as being in progress / imperfective, not a mental state — Thompson, Moran and Stone, Ferrero, Russell; Intention as plan: commitment, resistance to reconsideration, diachronic coherence — Bratman; Intention in action as a mental state executed in action (Simple View) — Searle, with Davidson and Davis as causal-theory background

The entry is silent on. Speech acts, illocutionary force, uptake by an audience, later ownership of earlier utterances, and LLMs or any artificial system. The entry is entirely about intention in action and for the future, and never mentions language use, retrospective settling of what an act was, or AI.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Commitment accounts place the normative core of assertion in how the speaker later responds to challenges (defend or retract). Rescorla goes furthest: no norm governs the making of an assertion, only norms governing later reactions. The entry reports this as a commitment account of assertion, not as a claim that force is settled incrementally. — Brandom, MacFarlane, Peirce, Alston, Green; Rescorla (2009a)

there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions. ... he must either defend the assertion or else retract it

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Assertoric commitment is partly constituted by reason-responsive conduct over the conversation: answering appropriate challenges, deferring to other sources, or withdrawing. This gives a functional criterion that could be tested on a system's behaviour. — Brandom (1994), MacFarlane (2003), Green (fidelity)

commits the speaker to defend her claim in response to legitimate challenges

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Austin held that illocutionary acts depend on securing uptake. The entry also notes that hearer-uptake and other hearer-directed relations bear on assertion, and Hamblin links commitment to what is said next. — Austin (1962); Hamblin (1970b)

it does depend on the hearer's being aware of the utterance and understanding it in a certain way ... 'securing of uptake'

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Uptake is not necessary for assertion in the speaker-content case. Bill asserted that p even though Lisa had left the room. This cuts against making uptake a condition of force. — The entry's own argument against Austin's uptake requirement

Bill still asserted that it was closed, since Bill believed she was still there.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Commitment is not sufficient for assertion (the 'I hereby commit myself' counterexample). Pagin argues this puts pressure on every hearer-directed or social account. A commitment-based condition on LLMs would therefore not by itself fix assertoric force. — Pagin (2004); partial replies by Pegan, MacFarlane, García-Carpintero, Marsili & Green

incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting

ADJACENT (2/5) — Intention-based accounts make a (reflexive) communicative intention at the time of utterance constitutive of illocutionary force. This is the view the thesis says cannot alone separate humans from LLMs. The entry also reports the regress and sneaky-intention problems for these accounts. — Grice (1957), Searle (1969), Bach & Harnish (1979); critics Strawson, Sperber & Wilson

S intends U to produce IE by means of the recognition of i-1

ADJACENT (2/5) — Cognitive safeguards: the hearer meta-represents the speaker's beliefs and intentions and can reject what is said, and social sanctions apply to misleading assertions. These are organisation-level features that distinguish assertion. — Jary (2010), Relevance Theory

it must also be subject to the cognitive and social safeguards that distinguish assertion

ADJACENT (2/5) — Underdetermination of content or force at the time of utterance may block assertion. Fricker holds it does; Hawthorne, Peet and García-Carpintero object. Indirect or sneaky assertions raise related questions about how force is fixed. — Fricker (2012); Hawthorne, Peet, García-Carpintero

as soon as it is not fully determinate to the hearer what the intended content of an utterance is, or what force it is made with, the utterance fails to be assertoric

Nearest positions. Commitment as accountability and as answerability to challenges, with retraction as the exit — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Rescorla: no norm on making assertions, only on later defend-or-retract reactions; Communicative-intention accounts making force depend on a reflexive intention at utterance — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish; Pagin's insufficiency objection to commitment and other hearer-directed accounts

The entry is silent on. LLMs and artificial speakers generally; the idea that illocutionary force is settled incrementally or retroactively through later uptake and ownership; any contrast between a force-fixing intention at output time and later commitment as what separates speakers from machines; and the claim that this is a condition on a system's organisation.
