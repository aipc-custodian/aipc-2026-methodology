**What is left.** Read etiologically, Gubelmann's contrast puts the output-time dispositions of RLHF-trained models (sycophancy, reward hacking) on the selected side of the artefact/organism line, because they exist through training pressure and not because any designer meant them. Proxy accounts that ground proxy speech in the creators' objectives therefore owe an account of what grounds the proxy relation for outputs that deviate from the designers' intended objective. This is a narrower point than 'no objective exists', since the specified reward and intended goal remain on the designer side. It is also narrower than 'LLMs sit between organisms and artefacts', since selection-style readings of training, SEL/INT functions, and Gubelmann's own §5 openness are already published.

**Not found.** I found no paper that combines (i) an etiological (selection versus intention-content) reading of Gubelmann's Kantian 'externally fixed goals' premise with (ii) sycophancy and reward hacking as unintended selected dispositions that undermine the grounding of proxy speech acts (Green & Michel, Arora). I could not read Gubelmann's full text, so I cannot say whether he answers the natural-selection/upbringing objection to 'external'. I could not read Green & Michel or Arora in full on unintended or deviant outputs. The empirical side was reached only through ML and AI-safety literature, which is adjacent to the phenomenon but not an empirical science of goal attribution. I did not reach psychology, cognitive science or linguistics on how people ascribe goals or authorship to artefacts versus organisms. I did not search derived-intentionality debates (Searle, Dennett), mesa-optimization (Hubinger), or Millikan on artefact functions, and I did not check Kant's own text. Silence on any of these is weak evidence about a large and partly paywalled literature, not a finding of novelty. This was therefore only a partial check of required search 2.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — teleology-biology

- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — An etiological reading of function: a trait's function is what it does that explains why it is there. The entry says this thrust survives in selected-effects accounts. The thesis's move is to use it to separate organisms from artifacts. — *Wright (1973, 1976); selected-effects theorists Wimsatt, Millikan, Neander*
  > the function of X is Z means (a) X is there because it does Z, and (b) Z is a consequence (or result) of X's being there
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — The external/immanent contrast in teleology. Plato's goals are externally imposed by a designer, while Aristotle's are internal to the organism. Kant likewise separates organisms from artifacts by natural purposiveness. This is the nearest historical frame for an 'externally fixed goals' premise. — *Plato vs. Aristotle (Lennox 1992; Ariew 2002; Johnson 2005); Kant, per Ginsborg (2004)*
  > Whereas Plato's teleology is mind-dependent and creationist, Aristotle's is naturalistic and functional.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Whether organisms differ from complex artifacts at all. Ginsborg's Kant holds that mechanical inexplicability does not separate them, and that regenerative and reproductive aspects supply the difference. Cummins-style analysis also treats artifact and organ parts alike. — *Ginsborg (2004); Cummins (1975); Lewens (2004)*
  > this impossibility of explaining organisms in solely mechanistic terms does not itself distinguish them from complex artifacts
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Derived proper function. Millikan, Bouchard and Garson extend selected-effects accounts to traits shaped within a single lifetime, using differential persistence or retention in place of differential reproduction. This is a ready vehicle for treating trained-in dispositions (and training-shaped behavior) etiologically. — *Millikan (1984); Bouchard (2013); Garson (2017)*
  > using “differential persistence” and “differential retention” within an organism's lifespan to play the role that differential reproduction plays in direct natural selection accounts
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The function/accident and function/malfunction desiderata. Unintended dispositions such as sycophancy or reward hacking are a case for 'accidental utility' versus real function, and for malfunction. The entry also reports that the selected-effects accounts struggle with malfunction. — *Teleonaturalist desiderata; Davies (2001); Wouters (2005); Sullivan-Bissett (2017)*
  > They should distinguish genuine biological functions from accidental utility (such as noses supporting glasses), and they should capture the normative dimension of function in order to preserve a function-malfunction distinction.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Dispositional or forward-looking accounts, and cybernetic accounts of goal-directedness in machines. These assign goals to a system via present dispositions or feedback, without a selection history. They are the closest analogues to 'objective-based proxy' accounts. The entry lists them but does not discuss proxy failure. — *Bigelow & Pargetter (1987); Boorse; Walsh (1996); Rosenblueth, Wiener & Bigelow (1943)*
  > Attributions of teleological, or goal-directed, behavior to animals or machines, they argued, meant nothing more than “purpose controlled by feed-back”
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — Selection dynamics complicate selected-effects explanation: frequency dependence and coevolution mean traits are not simply selected for their beneficial effects. Cited debate over whether partial or recent contributions suffice. Loosely parallel to proxy-optimization worries. — *Christie et al. (2022); Okasha (2022); Garson (2022)*
  > a phenotypic trait can be either beneficial or deleterious according to its frequency in the population

**Nearest positions.** Etiological/selected-effects accounts of function (including derived proper function) — Wright; Millikan; Neander; Bouchard; Garson; Plato-style externally imposed teleology vs. Aristotelian/Kantian immanent teleology — Plato, Aristotle, Kant (via Ginsborg); Cummins-style causal-role analysis, which treats artifacts and organisms alike — Cummins; Lewens; Cybernetic and dispositional accounts that assign goals via present organization or feedback — Rosenblueth/Wiener; Bigelow & Pargetter

**The entry is silent on.** The entry never mentions Gubelmann, LLMs, AI or machine-learning systems, sycophancy or reward hacking, or objective/proxy-based accounts of agency. It does not discuss the intentional-design (designer's-intention) account of artifact function or a premise of 'externally fixed goals'. It never discusses where artificial systems sit relative to organisms, apart from the cybernetic account's inclusion of machines.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — content-teleological

- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The entry separates artifact functions grounded in designer intention from the non-intentional functions needed for a non-circular theory of original intentionality. This supports the claim that 'externally fixed goals' sort organisms from watches only on an etiological reading. — *Wright 1973; Millikan, Neander and other etiological teleosemanticists (as the entry presents them)*
  > when we say that moving the cursor is the function of the computer's trackpad, we might mean that this is what the trackpad was designed to do or was intended to do by the computer's designer or user. Of course, if a teleological theory of mental content is to be a non-circular theory of original in…
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Etiological functions are determined by selection history. The relevant selection includes learning, conditioning, meme selection and neural selection, so a training-like process could in principle count. That fits an etiological reading that is not limited to phylogeny. — *Millikan 1984; Papineau 1984; Dretske 1986; Garson 2011, 2019a*
  > Papineau (1984) speaks of learning and Dretske (1986) invokes functions that depend on recruitment by conditioning. Garson (2011, 2019a) argues that the notion of selection should be loosened so that differential retention without differential replication could count as selection
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Design functions as an alternative ground for representation. Shea permits design functions where stabilization is absent, and Millikan's derived and adapted proper functions let learning-produced items have functions, so an objective-based or designed function is already on the table. These are not the 'externally fixed goals' premise itself. — *Shea 2018; Millikan 1984, ch. 2*
  > Shea allows that representational systems may have design functions instead of stabilized functions, so stabilization is not strictly necessary for representation
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Which dispositions count as functional is open. A trait's function is the whole complex causal role for which it was selected, and the content-relevant function is selected dispositions or response functions. This bears on whether unintended behaviours such as sycophancy or reward hacking would count as selected functions, as malfunctions, or as by-products. — *Neander 1995, 2017; Hundertmark 2021*
  > the relevant notion of function is one that permits the possibility of malfunction. That is, it allows for the possibility that a token trait has a function to do Z ... while not doing Z
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Non-etiological and modal accounts of function (Nanay, Piccinini, Schroeder, Bauer) tie content to present or modal contribution to goals rather than history. A referee would raise them against the claim that only the etiological reading does the sorting. — *Nanay 2014; Piccinini 2020a; Schroeder 2004; Bauer 2017*
  > they combine a teleological theory of content with a non-etiological account of function (Schroeder 2004; Nanay 2014; Bauer 2017; Piccinini 2020a)
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Liberalism worries over which systems count as representational. Burge says that if teleosemantics attributes representation too broadly, it is too liberal. Butlin and Schulte offer restrictions (constancy mechanisms, an active consumer). These bear on the thesis's open placement of LLMs relative to organisms. — *Burge 2010; Schulte 2015; Butlin 2020a; Artiga 2016, 2021*
  > teleosemantic theories invariably attribute representational states 'too broadly' – or, in other words, that teleosemantic theories are too liberal.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Swampman-style cases show what happens to systems with no selectional history, such as a system whose function comes only from an external specification. The standard etiological verdict is that such systems lack content. — *Davidson 1987; Millikan 1984; Neander 1996*
  > Swampman's component parts have no functions according to an etiological theory of function and so his 'brain' states have no contents according to standard teleological theories of mental content.

**Nearest positions.** Etiological/selected-effects teleosemantics, extended to learning and conditioning — Millikan, Neander, Papineau, Dretske, Garson; Shea's varitel semantics, which allows design functions as well as stabilized ones — Shea 2018; Non-etiological teleosemantics (modal or goal-contribution functions) — Nanay 2014, Piccinini 2020a, Schroeder, Bauer

**The entry is silent on.** The entry says nothing about LLMs, machine learning, training objectives or proxy objectives, sycophancy, reward hacking, or Gubelmann's 'externally fixed goals' premise. It never discusses whether artifacts trained by an optimisation process (as opposed to designed by intention) can acquire etiological functions. It also never asks whether output-time dispositions that diverge from the intended objective count as selected functions, malfunctions or by-products.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artifact

- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — The organism/artifact line is drawn by where the goal or function originates: externally in a maker's intention, or internally through selection. The entry reports this as Aristotle's distinction and as Neander's and Searle's split between agentive and non-agentive function. — *Aristotle; Neander (1991); Searle (1995)*
  > Those that exist by nature have their origin in themselves, whereas those that exist by craft have their origin in the craftsperson
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — An etiological (selection-history) reading does not sort organisms from artifacts. It is designed to treat both under one account of proper function. — *Millikan (1984); Preston (1998, 2013); Elder (2004)*
  > Neither Millikan's nor Cummins' account is intrinsically intentionalist, since they are designed to fit both the biological and the artifactual cases.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — The natural/artificial boundary that the 'externally fixed' premise relies on is itself suspect. Making artifacts is internal to nature, and artifacts outrun their makers' intentions. — *Baker (2007); Vogel (2003, 2015); Sperber (2007)*
  > the whole process of making artifacts is internal to nature and cannot be legitimately considered separate from it
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Artifact dispositions can diverge from maker intention. The entry says information technology yields artifacts that 'act on their own, beyond any intention their creators may have', and that there is an ineliminable gap between a builder's intention and the consequences of her acts. Unintended by-products ('residue') are also a recognised category. — *Sperber (2007); Vogel (2015); Hilpinen (2011)*
  > There is a gap, in the construction of every artifact, between the intention with which the builder acts and the consequences of her acts
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Objective-based proxy accounts resemble intentionalist and derived-proper-function accounts. On these, a designer's intention fixes function even when the artifact fails or works in a novel way. The entry also flags proper versus non-proper function, malfunction, and phantom functions as the pressure points. — *Millikan (derived proper function); Houkes & Vermaas (ICE, use plan); Neander; Searle*
  > Even if this device works in a completely novel way, and even if it is not capable of performing as envisioned, opening cans is still what it is supposed to do.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Intentionalist accounts struggle to distinguish proper from non-proper functions. Non-intentionalist accounts struggle with novel prototypes and phantom functions. — *Houkes & Vermaas (2010); Preston (2013); Evnine (2016); Holm (2017)*
  > Intentionalist approaches have more difficulty making this distinction, because for these views human intentions are the only mechanism for establishing functions

**Nearest positions.** Etiological/selected-effects function applied across organisms and artifacts, with derived proper function for intention-fixed cases — Millikan (1984); Intentionalist artifact function, where the designer's or user's intended effect fixes function immediately — Neander (1991), Searle (1995), Houkes & Vermaas (ICE theory); Non-intentionalist pluralism (proper plus system function), with intentions themselves reproduced by material culture — Preston (1998, 2013), Elder (2004)

**The entry is silent on.** Gubelmann's argument, LLMs, machine-learning training, and sycophancy or reward hacking are not discussed. The entry does not treat an optimisation or training process as a selection mechanism that could ground etiological function. It also does not discuss goal-directedness of a system (as opposed to the function of a trait or artifact), or where artificial agents sit relative to organisms. The nearest gesture is Sperber's remark that IT artifacts can act 'beyond any intention their creators may have'.
