This check can show that something HAS been argued. It cannot show that nothing has. Read `not found` as carefully as the findings — a clean result means only that these searches came back empty, and much of the literature is paywalled and invisible to it.

Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: promises, assertion, speech-acts — The thesis turns on promising as a commissive whose satisfaction conditions depend on the speaker's future conduct, contrasted with assertion as a synchronic act, and generalises across speech acts, so the promises, assertion and speech-acts entries are the closest fits.

For artificial speakers, acts whose content conditions include future conduct by the speaker (promising and similar commissives) depend on whether a unit capable of lasting through the satisfaction horizon is available, and scaffolding can supply one. Assertion has no such requirement, so artificial systems can come apart on the two acts.

What is left. Granting that artificial speakers can perform speech acts at all (against Skiba and Gubelmann), the thesis claims that whether an artificial speaker can perform a commissive depends on whether a speaker-unit lasting through the act's satisfaction horizon is available. The thesis adds that this unit must be individuated by act type, that scaffolding can supply it, and that assertion has no such requirement. On this basis, positions such as quasi-testimony, proto-assertion and proxy views can differ across act types instead of being fixed for the whole system. I found nothing that conclusively does this, but Kasirzadeh & Gabriel-style remarks about persistent identity and commissives (snippet only, authors unverified) must be credited and read in full. The 'quasi-promise is incoherent' step is not shown to have been made anywhere in what I saw.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — promises

ADJACENT (1/5) — Promises are cast as speech acts (Austin, Searle), with promising as a conventional illocutionary act. The entry treats this only at the level of speech-act theory and says nothing about artificial speakers or about the persistence of a speaker over time. — Austin 1962; Searle 1963, 1965, 1979; Alston; Hanfling; Pratt; Harris & McKinney 2021

Austin takes promising to be an illocutionary act, that is, he takes it that promising is merely a matter of a certain form of utterance, under certain conditions.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Promising and assertion are linked, and the entry lists work comparing them. It also discusses a promising-versus-asserting contrast and notes that a promise differs from a mere statement of present intention. — Watson 2004 ('Asserting and Promising'); van Roojen 2020 ('Promising and Assertion'); Raz, Kolodny & Wallace

the advising of the promisee of one’s mere intention to do the promised act is insufficient ground for the sorts of expectations that are meant to attend promises

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Self-promises and the Hobbesian release problem raise the question of whether a promisor and later self remain the same bindable unit. The entry also covers vows and resolutions as self-binding across time. — Hobbes; Rosati 2011; Dannenberg 2015; Schaab 2021; Liberman 2019

self promises are a way of solidifying our wills, protecting our valued choices from deterioration in the face of time and trials

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry shows that some theories require the promisor to hold or form an intention or commitment. Rational or mental-state requirements on the promisor are relevant to whether an artificial speaker can promise. — Raz (exclusionary reasons); Owens 2008 ('Promising without Intending'); Liberman 2015 (bibliography only)

Promises, says Raz, produce (in the promisor) both first order reasons to perform the promised act as well as second order exclusionary reasons

Nearest positions. Austin/Searle: promising as a conventional illocutionary act, a rule-governed speech act; Normative power views (Raz, Owens, Shiffrin): the promisor binds herself by an act of will; Self-promise and release debates (Hobbes, Rosati, Schaab): whether a self is a stable bindable unit over time

The entry is silent on. Artificial speakers, AI systems or LLMs. It says nothing about any speaker-persistence or 'satisfaction horizon' requirement on commissives, nothing about scaffolding supplying a persistent unit, and nothing about a contrast between commissives and assertion in this respect.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

ADJACENT (3/5) — Assertion and promising are linked by commitment structure. Marsili argues promising illocutionarily entails asserting. Hawley analyses assertion in terms of promising. Watson distinguishes primary commitments: promising commits one to act, asserting commits one to the defensibility of p. A thesis that assertion lacks a future-conduct requirement while promising has one sits against these. — Marsili (2016); Hawley (2019); Watson (2004); Searle (1969, 1979)

Marsili (2016) argues that promising that you will φ “illocutionary entails” asserting that you will φ.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Watson's contrast: promise's primary commitment is to act so as to make φ happen, while assertion's primary commitment is to facts independent of one's actions. This supports the claim that promising, unlike assertion, requires future conduct by the speaker. — Watson (2004)

When you promise to φ, your primary commitment is to (intend to) act so as to make φ happen. By contrast, when you assert that p, your primary commitment is to facts that are independent of your actions

ADJACENT (2/5) — Hawley's account makes assertion a promise to speak truthfully. Every assertion would then carry a commitment about the speaker's own conduct, though it is made and fulfilled or broken at once. This rivals the claim that assertion has no future-conduct requirement. — Hawley (2019); Carson (2006)

whenever you assert, your promise to speak truthfully is simultaneously made and either fulfilled or broken

SUPPORTED (3/5) — Assertoric commitment is partly forward-looking: expectations about what one will say next, and responses to later challenges. This is a temporal persistence requirement the thesis must treat as distinct from the one it posits for promising. — Hamblin (1970b); Brandom (1994); MacFarlane; Geurts (2019)

When you make an assertion, you generate an expectation that you will behave in certain ways and not in others, especially in relation to what you will say next.

Nearest positions. Commitment accounts: assertion as accountability and as commitment to defend and respond to challenges — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Promising and asserting as related commitment-generating acts — Watson (different primary commitments), Marsili (promising entails asserting), Hawley (assertion as promise to speak truthfully); Searle's family of assertives versus commissives, which differ in the direction of commitment — Searle (1969, 1979), Searle & Vanderveken

The entry is silent on. Artificial speakers, AI systems or LLMs. Also silent on persistence through time as a precondition for performing a speech act, on 'scaffolding' that supplies a lasting agent unit, and on a satisfaction horizon. It never asks whether a speaker could assert but fail to promise, or the reverse.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Content conditions are a component of illocutionary force: some illocutions need a suitable content, e.g. promising requires a future act under the speaker's control. This gives the thesis its asymmetry between promising and assertion. — Searle and Vanderveken 1985, as reported by the entry

I can only promise what is in the future and under my control; or, at least, I cannot promise to do anything that it is obvious to myself and my promissee that I cannot do.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Commissives are a distinct Austinian category, defined by speakers committing themselves to courses of action. This separates promising from assertion by kind of commitment. — Austin

commissives (in which speakers commit themselves to causes or courses of action, e.g. promising and betting)

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speech acts are individuated by commitment, and assertion and promising differ in what is undertaken. Assertion is commitment to defend P, with liability to error. Promising is commitment to a future course of action. A speaker who persists over time is presupposed, but the entry does not draw this out. — Green (entry author); Searle and Vanderveken

the characteristic aim of a promise is to commit oneself to a future course of action.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Retraction and release from commitments over time. Commitments persist after the act and can be withdrawn later. This touches on the temporal extension of commitment. — Sbisà 2007, as reported

on Wednesday I may be able to retract a claim I made on Monday. Likewise, you may allow me on Wednesday to retract the promise I made to you on Monday.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Performance of a speech act can be defeated by social or structural factors, such as lack of uptake or loss of standing. A promise-breaker can lose the ability to promise in a community. This suggests acts can fail for reasons about the speaker rather than the content, a parallel to the thesis's capacity-dependence claim. — Entry's Section 9; Langton; Hornsby and Langton

the inveterate promise-breaker will, in time, lead others in his community to be unwilling to accept any promises he tries to make.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Speech acts can be performed by non-human or non-standard agents. Searle allows a dog requesting to be let out. Davis's cases include photocopy machines as targets of speaker meaning. Neither concerns artificial speakers as performers. — Searle 1969; Davis 1992, as reported

Searle considers the case of a dog requesting to be let outside (1969, p. 39).

Nearest positions. Content conditions as a component of illocutionary force (promising requires a future act under the speaker's control) — Searle and Vanderveken 1985; Speech acts as overt commitment, with different acts carrying different commitments (assertion vs. conjecture vs. promise) — Green, the entry's author; Capacity to perform a given speech act can be lost or absent for a particular speaker (promise-breaker, silencing) — Langton; Hornsby and Langton; Pratt

The entry is silent on. Artificial speakers or AI systems as performers of speech acts. Also silent on the persistence or continuity of the speaker as a precondition of promising, on a 'unit lasting through the satisfaction horizon', and on scaffolding (memory, external structures) supplying such a unit. The entry does not say that assertion and promising can come apart for any kind of speaker on grounds of temporal persistence. The only AI link is the 'artificial intelligence' mention in the opening list of fields influenced by speech act theory.
