Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, social-ontology, assertion — The theses concern illocutionary force and who authors a chatbot's speech act, which belongs to the speech-acts entry. Their view of force as a public, status-conferring, deontic practice matches social-ontology, and the entry on assertion covers the assertion and promise vocabulary used in the Moffatt case.

Force is fixed by a public status-conferring practice of adoption and uptake, not by producer psychology. This is supported by divergence tests against objectives-based proxy views, with Moffatt compatible with it but not established as a case of illocution.

What is left. No source I could see anticipates the thesis. Several rivals contradict it (Green & Michel, Arora, Mallory, and the Moffatt tort framing), so as stated it must narrow or fight. What survives is this: a public status-conferring practice of adoption and uptake, and not producer-side objectives, can account for attributing force to the deployer in Moffatt-type deployments, provided that (a) Green & Michel's objectives condition is shown to be content-specific and not satisfiable by a blanket attitude, and (b) the premise that Moffatt is a case of illocution and not only of negligence-based liability is argued directly, not read off commentators' use of 'promise'.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (3/5) — Force conventionalism. Performing a speech act requires an accepted public procedure with a conventional effect, and some acts also need audience uptake. Austin holds the strong version. Searle holds a weaker version on which constitutive rules are characteristic but not universal. This is the nearest 'public practice' view. The entry reports it as a general account of force, not as the thesis's status-conferring adoption-and-uptake account, and it presents it as challenged. — Austin; Searle; Reimer's 'illocutionary conventions'; Millikan's 'natural conventions' (the entry criticises Millikan)

for every speech act that is performed, there is some convention that will have been invoked in order to make that speech act occur.

CONTRADICTED (4/5) — The intentionalist alternative. Force is fixed by manifest communicative intentions, not by extra-semantic conventions. The entry's own account builds force on Gricean speaker meaning, as overt manifestation of commitment, so it does not make force purely public status. — Strawson 1964; Grice; Green (the entry's author); Bach and Harnish

What makes an utterance of a sentence in the indicative mood a prediction rather than a command, for instance, is that it manifests an intention to be so taken

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Intending or trying to perform a speech act is not sufficient. Even when it is common knowledge that I am trying to ask or bequeath, nothing is performed until the act is publicly undertaken. The entry presents this as a public commitment that is not undertaken merely by intending it. Section 2.2 also says some acts misfire without uptake (a bet not accepted). — Green 2013; Austin (uptake); Langton/Hornsby (silencing)

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Divergence tests against coarse proxies. Assertion and conjecture share direction of fit and conditions of satisfaction (the entry's 'aim'-type proxies) but differ in public commitment norms, shown by the challenge 'How do you know?'. Illocutionary point and perlocutions as 'characteristic aims' are the entry's nearest objectives-style proxies, and the entry treats them as too coarse. — Green 2017; Williamson 1996; Searle and Vanderveken 1985; Cohen 1973

an assertion and a conjecture that P have identical conditions of satisfaction, namely that P be the case.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Disputes over whether uptake is constitutive. Bird says refusal does not require uptake. Maitra recasts silencing as failure of understanding, not of the speech act. Dogwhistling creates tension for a purely public account, because manifestness varies across audience members. — Bird 2002; Maitra 2009; Saul 2018; Langton; Hornsby

Bird (2002) denies that the speech act of refusal requires uptake.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Scorekeeping and commitment views. Force is characterised through the normative structure of overt commitment, and illocutionary validity is cashed out in deontic terms of obligations and liability to error. This is close to a status-based account, although it is built on speaker meaning. — Green; Searle and Vanderveken; MacFarlane 2011; Roberts; Lewis

We may elucidate the relevant forms of commitment by spelling out the norms underlying them.

Nearest positions. Force conventionalism: public conventions and uptake fix force. Austin (strong), Searle (weaker), Reimer's illocutionary conventions.; Intentionalism: manifest communicative intention fixes force. Strawson, Grice's speaker meaning, and Green's overt-commitment version.; Normative-commitment and scorekeeping accounts, which characterise force by public commitments and norms. Green, Searle and Vanderveken, Roberts, MacFarlane.

The entry is silent on. The entry never mentions Moffatt. It does not discuss 'objectives-based proxy views' of force as such, a 'status-conferring practice of adoption' as the fixer of force, or divergence tests against such proxies. It also says nothing about whether producer-side outputs (for example from a non-human or institutional producer) count as illocutions.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — social-ontology

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Conferralism: a social status is fixed by other people's attitudes toward the item in a context, not by the producer's own states. This is a broader view of which the thesis's uptake-based account of force would be an application. The entry's example is an umpire conferring 'strike'. — Ásta 2008–2018

A social property is “conferred” on an object, according to Ásta, if the object has the property because of some attitudes of people. A baseball umpire, for instance, confers the property of being a strike on certain pitches.

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (3/5) — Performatives and status-function declarations: utterances create institutional facts, with the status imposed by collective acceptance. The speech act is the vehicle, and acceptance by the community sustains it. — Austin 1961/1962; Searle 1995, 2010; Thomasson 2003

the way status functions get assigned, more specifically, is by the collective acceptance (or recognition) of a constitutive rule. And the process for putting the rule in place involves a particular kind of speech act—a declaration.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Rival: Searle makes social facts depend on collective mental states (acceptance and we-attitudes), not on public practice. Psychologistic views generally locate construction in mental states. The thesis opposes producer psychology, but Searle's view places the weight on community psychology. — Searle; psychologism (Mill, Bratman, Gilbert, Tuomela)

he does not require that rules be implemented in behavior: institutional facts depend only on mental states in a society, not on practices.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Practice theories and pluralism about anchoring give resources for locating the source of a status in public practice. Epstein's heterogeneous anchors include legal facts such as jury instructions and trial results. — Bourdieu, Giddens, Schatzki; Epstein 2014a

Legal categories in the U.S., for instance, are anchored by facts such as jury instructions, trial results, legislative enactments, judicial interpretation, and environmental regularities.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry separates causal from constitutive construction and explores diachronic and historical determinants of social facts. A referee would ask whether the thesis's 'adoption and uptake' is constitutive or merely causal. — Kukla 2000, Haslanger 2003, Kõiv 2019; Epstein 2015, Schaffer 2019

distinguish “causal construction” from “constitutive construction,”

Nearest positions. Searle: institutional facts are set up by collectively accepted status-function declarations, a speech-act and collective-attitude view; Ásta: conferralism, where others' attitudes in context confer social properties; Practice theories (Bourdieu, Giddens, Schatzki) and Epstein's heterogeneous anchoring

The entry is silent on. Illocutionary force and Austinian or Langton-style 'uptake' as such, producer intention versus public status in assessing force, 'divergence tests', 'objectives-based proxy views', AI or chatbot outputs as speech acts, and the Moffatt case. The entry does not discuss any of these.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (3/5) — Assertion is characterized by publicly assigned social statuses: commitment as accountability to the audience, and commitment to defend the claim and to authorize the hearer. These are not characterized by the speaker's inner states. The entry does not itself present this as a thesis that force is fixed by adoption and uptake. — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green, Geurts (as reported)

asserting achieves two different social results at the same time: (i) it commits the speaker to defend her claim in response to legitimate challenges; and relatedly (ii) authorizes the hearer to claim anything that follows from what the speaker asserted.

PRECEDENT · PASSING (2/5) — Austin held that an illocutionary act depends on securing uptake, which is a hearer-side condition on force. — Austin (1962)

it does depend on the hearer's being aware of the utterance and understanding it in a certain way: I haven't warned someone unless he heard what I said.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — For assertion in particular, uptake is not required. The entry gives a speaker-content case in which the assertion is made although the hearer has left. — The entry's own argument against Austin's view, applied to assertion

it is less plausible when the structure is speaker-content ("Bill asserted that p"). It may be said that Bill failed to tell Lisa that the station was closed ... but that Bill still asserted that it was closed

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Producer psychology is a main line in the literature. Communicative-intention accounts say force is fixed by the speaker's reflexive hearer-directed intentions. Belief-expression and self-representation accounts tie assertion to the speaker's mental state. — Grice (Gr-A), Searle (Srl-I), Bach & Harnish (BH-A), Williams, Black, Davidson, Unger, Slote

On their analysis (1979: 42), assuming a speaker S and a hearer H, (BH-A) S asserts that p iff S expresses i. the belief that p, and ii. the intention that H believe that p.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry reports arguments that tell against producer-psychology accounts: sneaky intentions, double bluffing, stonewalling, and the regress of reflexive intentions. — Strawson, Schiffer, Sperber & Wilson, and the entry itself

the speaker may simply be stonewalling, reiterating an assertion without any hope of convincing the addressee of anything.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Social and commitment accounts face a sufficiency objection (a commitment can be incurred without asserting), and even defenders concede that assertion is not fully reducible to social effects. Convention is argued to be neither necessary nor sufficient for force. — Pagin (2004); Strawson, Stainton, Davidson, Bach & Harnish; Marsili & Green (2021) concede

They acknowledge, nonetheless, that assertions are not fully reducible to their social effects.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Conventionalist views hold that force is fixed by public conventions linking sentence type or force-indicators to force, so that force is public rather than psychological. — Dummett, Searle, Kölbel, Kot'átko, Green (expressive conventions)

it is a convention that declarative sentences are used for assertion, interrogative for questions and imperative for commands and requests.

Nearest positions. Commitment/accountability accounts of assertion as a social status — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Communicative-intention accounts (force fixed by speaker's reflexive intentions) — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish; Conventionalist accounts of force, and the objections to them — Austin, Dummett, Searle versus Strawson, Davidson, Stainton

The entry is silent on. The entry does not discuss 'objectives-based proxy views', divergence tests between them and a practice-based view of force, or Moffatt or any case like it. It does not treat force as fixed by 'adoption' of a practice, and it does not discuss any case of doubtful illocutionary status such as non-human or automated producers.
