**What is left.** The conduit/discretion dilemma for proxy accounts of machine assertion has already been drawn (Butlin & Viebahn 2025; Williams, Bayne et al. 2025), and Nickel's original proxy view was already a hybrid of machine speech-actancy and relocated responsibility. What survives is the narrower claim that Arora's and Green & Michel's proxy accounts, taken on their own terms, either (a) just relocate commitment to the operator, which is what attributing views already do, or (b) need a machine-side bearer of deviation, which a deflated second act lacking sanctionability or disownability does not supply. On that claim proxy views depend on attributing views and are not rivals to them. Defending it needs two things: an argument that Arora's sensitivity condition and Green & Michel's objectives and 'some discretion' do not escape the dilemma, and an explanation of why the thesis's claim that the ambassador is itself sanctionable and disownable is not Ludwig's status-function picture.

**Not found.** I found no source that says proxy accounts are 'parasitic on' attributing views, or that the proxy relation adds a second locus of commitment without removing the first. 'Attributing view' does not appear as a standard label in what I retrieved, so the thesis's terminology may not map onto the literature's. I found nothing on 'disownability' or a 'second act' (the thesis's terms) in the proxy literature. I did not read the full texts of Arora, Green & Michel, Ludwig or Butlin & Viebahn, only abstracts and secondary quotations, so how each handles a deflated second act is unverified. I did not search agency law or principal-agent doctrine (authority, ratification, acting beyond authority), Brandomian scorekeeping, Freiman & Miller's quasi-assertion, or Mallory's fictionalism. The search left philosophy only weakly: the empirical hits concern blame and responsibility attribution to AI (psychology and HCI), not how delegated or spokesperson speech works in linguistics, pragmatics, sociology of organisations or legal theory. The empirical side of the search is therefore thin. The 2026 sources and the arXiv preprint appeared only as snippets.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Commitment as accountability: asserting means accepting liability to sanction or blame if the content is false. This gives the thesis its test, that a bearer of commitment must be sanctionable. — *Peirce; Alston (2000); Searle (2010) in mixed form*
  > knowingly [takes] on the liability to ([lay] herself open to) blame (censure, reproach, being taken to task, being called to account), in case of not-p.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Commitment as challenge-responsibility, which can be deferred to another asserter or discharged only by withdrawing the assertion. This is the entry's nearest model of relocating commitment to someone else. — *Brandom (1994); MacFarlane (2003)*
  > to defer this responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony one is relying. One can escape this commitment only by withdrawing the assertion.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Goldberg's account: the speaker authorizes the hearer to defer back to the speaker the responsibility of meeting challenges. This puts the bearer of responsibility inside the assertion relation. — *Goldberg (2015)*
  > the speaker authorizes the hearer to defer back to the speaker the responsibility of meeting challenges to the assertion.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Non-commitment rivals: descriptive, hearer-neutral accounts. Pagin's 'prima facie informative' account is defined by default cognitive patterns on production and comprehension, not by a bearer of liability. Stalnaker's common-ground model likewise does not need one. — *Pagin (2011, 2020); Stalnaker (1974, 1978)*
  > an assertion is an utterance that is prima facie informative
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Commitment is not sufficient for assertion. Relocating commitment to an operator therefore would not make the output an assertion. The objection pressures every hearer-directed account. — *Pagin (2004); defended against by Pegan (2009), MacFarlane (2011), García-Carpintero (2013), Marsili & Green (2021)*
  > Whoever utters (37) felicitously incurs a commitment to the truth of the proposition that there are black swans. However, intuitively (37) would not be an assertion
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Liability understood as being right or wrong on the issue, not as sanctionability. This is a thinner notion that a 'deflated second act' might meet. — *Green (2013, 2017, 2020a)*
  > one who asserts that P is liable to be right or wrong on the issue of P's truth exactly as P turns out to be true or false.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Utterance without assertoric force by someone who represents themselves as asserting: the actor case, impersonation, jokes. The audience is not invited to attribute the belief to the speaker. — *The entry's discussion of self-representation; Davidson (1979, 1984); Bach & Harnish (1979)*
  > the audience not invited to believe that the speaker, that is, the actor, has that belief.
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — Austin on the speaker-content argument structure: an assertion can stand without uptake, so a hearer-directed structure is not essential to asserting. — *Austin (1962), as discussed in the entry*
  > Bill still asserted that it was closed, since Bill believed she was still there.

**Nearest positions.** Commitment as accountability and liability to sanction — Peirce, Alston, with Searle's mixed version; Commitment as challenge-responsibility, including deferral of responsibility — Brandom, MacFarlane, Goldberg; Non-commitment descriptive accounts (prima facie informative; common ground) — Pagin, Stalnaker; with Green's thin 'liability to error' as the contrast case

**The entry is silent on.** Machine or AI-generated assertion, proxies, operators, delegated or ghost-written speech, and attributing assertion to a non-speaker are not discussed anywhere in the entry. Terms like 'proxy account', 'attributing view', 'second act', 'disownability', and 'imputed deviation' have no counterpart in it. The entry covers only the commitment/accountability apparatus the thesis would draw on.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — ethics-ai

- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Responsibility-gap debate: when an autonomous system acts, neither the human nor the machine may be responsible. The proposed remedy is to relocate responsibility to humans via meaningful control or distributed responsibility. This resembles the thesis's first horn, 'relocate commitment to the operator'. The entry applies it to responsibility for action, not to commitment in assertion. — *Sparrow 2007 (the gap); Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018 (meaningful control); Taddeo and Floridi 2018 (distributed responsibility); Danaher and Nyholm 2021*
  > a “responsibility gap” has been suggested (esp. Rob Sparrow 2007), meaning that neither the human nor the machine may be responsible. Perhaps the solution is to keep humans “in the loop” or “on the loop” or in “meaningful control”
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Sceptical accounts say responsibility gaps are not real, or that the real issue is the distribution of risk. The entry also leaves open whether AI genuinely challenges existing allocations of responsibility. — *Tigard 2021; Königs 2022; Da Silva 2024; Simpson and Müller 2016*
  > It remains an open question whether the advent AI systems truly challenge our allocations of responsibility, or rather just generates temporary confusion.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — LLM outputs and testimony: the entry cites Sparrow and Flenady's 'testimony gap' and Hicks et al.'s view of hallucinations as 'bullshit'. These are the entry's only points of contact with assertion-like status of machine outputs. — *Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Hicks, Humphries and Slater 2024*
  > Others have argued that the “hallucinations” that plague LLMs are a form of this “bullshit” (Hicks, Humphries, and Slater 2024) or create a “testimony gap” (Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025).
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The thick notion of moral agency requires the ability to take blame and praise, and a normative condition (acting on reasons). Current AI is said to lack responsibility for its values because it cannot reflect on or change them. This backs the claim that a machine-side bearer of imputed deviation is not supplied. The entry has no equivalent of 'disownability'. — *Müller 2021; Dung 2025; Barandiaran and Almendros 2024*
  > The classical “thick” notion of a moral agent is designed to assign moral responsibility, the ability to take blame and praise.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — The retribution gap is the difficulty of allocating punishment. It is the closest the entry comes to the 'sanctionability' criterion. — *Danaher 2016a*
  > The difficulty of allocating punishment is sometimes called the “retribution gap” (Danaher 2016a).
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Deflationary 'thin' agency. Floridi treats even a reactive LLM as an agent without intelligence. Moor's impact and implicit agents are 'agents' without full responsibility. A referee may ask whether a thin agent could be the machine-side bearer. — *Floridi 2023a, 2023b; Moor 2006; Allen, Varner and Zinser 2000*
  > current AI systems, even a merely reactive LLM, come out as agents, but without intelligence (Floridi 2023a; 2023b, ch. 2)
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — The relational turn: if we treat robots as though they had status, asking whether they really do may be futile. This is a rival way of dissolving the proxy and attribution question, by anti-realism about the bearer. — *Coeckelbergh 2010, 2012, 2018; Danaher 2019b (ethical behaviourism); Gerdes 2016*
  > If we relate to robots as though they had rights, then it might be futile to search whether they really do have such rights
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — AI personal assistants and agents: the entry says responsibility for the assistant's actions must be clarified. This is the nearest approach to an operator or user standing behind machine acts. — *Milano and Nyholm 2024*
  > the ethical and legal responsibility for the actions of the assistant would have to be clarified (Milano and Nyholm 2024)

**Nearest positions.** Responsibility-gap and meaningful-control accounts that relocate responsibility to humans — Sparrow 2007; Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018; Danaher and Nyholm 2021; Testimony-gap and 'bullshit' accounts of LLM output — Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Hicks, Humphries and Slater 2024; Thin versus thick agency, including agency without intelligence — Floridi 2023a; Moor 2006; Dung 2025; Sceptics about responsibility gaps — Tigard 2021; Königs 2022; Da Silva 2024

**The entry is silent on.** The entry says nothing on assertion, commitment, speech acts, deontic scorekeeping or entitlement. It has no 'proxy' or 'attributing' view of machine outputs, no notion of 'imputed deviation', and no 'second act' or 'disownability' criterion. It never asks whether proxy accounts are parasitic on attributing accounts. Its treatment is confined to moral and legal responsibility for actions and risk.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — collective-responsibility

- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — The dilemma between reducing group responsibility to individuals and positing an irreducible group bearer is the entry's organizing split. Methodological individualists hold that responsibility is really the individuals' and any group talk is metaphor. Non-reductionists hold that the group is a distinct bearer. — *Narveson, Weber, Lewis (individualist side); Cooper, French (irreducibility side)*
  > “Nothing else,” Narveson writes, “can literally be the bearer of full responsibility”
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Representational and authorization accounts place the group's agency in a representative or authorized individual. Hobbes's Leviathan and Tuomela's representative subject are cases. The entry reports these as attempts to ground a collective subject, not as mere relabellings. — *Hobbes; Tuomela; Copp; May (authorization condition)*
  > some individuals be authorized to represent their own actions as the actions of the group as a whole (May 1987, p. 55)
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — A 'like a moral agent' or 'as if' account is criticized as not supplying a real bearer. This parallels the thesis's complaint that a deflated 'second act' does not supply a bearer. — *Thompson (target); the entry's author (critic)*
  > “acting like a moral agent” is not the same thing as being a moral agent. (And if one really is a moral agent then there is no need to go to the lengths of specifying likeness.)
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Accounts of shared intention that are built from individuals' intentions do not themselves yield a bearer of blame. In that sense they are parasitic on individual-level attribution. — *Bratman; Sosa*
  > it does not attach those collective intentions to the kind of collective moral agent that is required of moral responsibility.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Several accounts supply a group-side bearer without free will or a unified self. They make collectives sanctionable through reform, apology, dismantling, or reasons-responsiveness. This challenges the claim that no non-individual bearer can be sanctionable. — *Shockley; Crawford; Smiley 2010; Hess; Silver; Björnsson & Hess*
  > “deserving of some kind of punishment apart from that meted out to its members for their role in harm”
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Collective blame is not deducible from claims about individuals. Cooper argues membership varies, and French argues group-only predicates exist. This resists any 'relabelled individual view' reading of group-side accounts. — *Cooper; French*
  > “the existence of a collective is compatible with varying membership. No determinate set of individuals is necessary for the existence of the collective”
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Responsibility is split between the group and its members. Blame can attach to each at once, as 'programming' versus 'implementing' causes, or the collective can be an enabling condition for individual agents. — *List & Pettit; Shockley*
  > control in this context does not need to reside in the group or in individuals. Instead, it can reside simultaneously in both places

**Nearest positions.** Methodological individualism (responsibility is really individuals', group talk is metaphor) — Weber, Lewis, Narveson, Downie; Representative or authorized subject accounts (agency transferred to or exercised by an authorized individual) — Hobbes, Tuomela, May, Copp; Non-Kantian group-side blameworthiness with sanctions such as reform and apology — Shockley, Crawford, Smiley 2010; also Thompson's 'like moral agents' account

**The entry is silent on.** Machines, AI systems, operators, and proxy/delegated agency generally. The entry also never uses 'proxy', 'attributing view', 'imputed deviation', 'second act', 'sanctionability', or 'disownability'. It does not take up whether disowning or repudiating an act is a condition of attributing it to an agent. The closest it comes is McGary's 'dissociation condition' for dissenting group members.
