This check can show that something HAS been argued. It cannot show that nothing has. Read `not found` as carefully as the findings — a clean result means only that these searches came back empty, and much of the literature is paywalled and invisible to it.

Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, assertion, fiction — The theses concern whether chatbot outputs are serious assertions or Searle/Austin-style non-serious illocutions made by an author, which is the territory of the speech-acts entry (serious versus pretended illocution) and the assertion entry (the realist-versus-denier debate), with fiction covering the make-believe and authorial-pretence framing.

Chatbot outputs are indexed to a represented speaker (the assistant character), so the make-believe need not sit in users, who can read literally. Whether post-training removes that indexing or only narrows the repertoire is an open empirical question. Calling the base case Searle's pretended illocution is not established.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speech acts require speaker meaning, i.e. overtly manifesting a commitment or an intention to be taken as performing the act. A speaker with no such intention (a joker, actor, or storyteller) does not thereby perform the act. This bears on whether chatbot outputs are speech acts of anyone, and on whether the pretence sits with the speaker or the audience. — Green (the entry's author), following Strawson and Grice

We perform a speech act, then, when we overtly commit ourselves in a certain way to a content—where that way is an aspect of how we speaker-mean that content.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry's own examples of actors and jokers show that an utterance in assertoric form need not be an assertion. This is the entry's only treatment of pretended or simulated illocutions. It supports the thesis's point that pretence can attach to the producer or role rather than the audience. — Davidson 1979, discussed by Green 1997 and Hare 1989

every joker, storyteller, and actor will immediately take advantage of the strengthened mood to simulate assertion.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Uptake and audience response can determine whether an illocution succeeds. The make-believe may therefore be located on the audience side or the speaker side. Hearers can fail to grant or deny uptake. — Austin; Langton; Hornsby and Langton; Bird; Maitra

Other attempts at speech acts might misfire because their addressee fails to respond with an appropriate uptake

ADJACENT (1/5) — Dogwhistling: different parts of the audience take different speaker-meanings from the same utterance, and what is manifest to one addressee may not be to another. This is a structural analogue to users reading literally while the output is indexed to a represented speaker. — Saul 2018; Green

What is manifest to one addressee may not be manifest to another, and a speaker may exploit this fact.

Nearest positions. Speech acts require speaker meaning / overt manifestation of commitment, so who the speaker is matters — Green, following Grice and Strawson; Force and the actor/joker case: simulated assertion is not assertion; no force indicator can guarantee force — Davidson 1979, with replies from Hare 1989 and Green 1997; Uptake-dependence of illocutions, with audience response partly constituting the act — Austin, Langton, Hornsby and Langton, Bird, Maitra

The entry is silent on. Chatbots, language models, AI-generated text and post-training are never discussed. The entry also has no discussion of Searle's pretended illocutions, or of 'represented' or fictional speakers beyond actors and jokers in passing. It says nothing on whether a non-agent output can carry illocutionary force, or on make-believe located in producer versus audience. (The bibliography lists Shoham 1993 on agent-oriented programming, but the body text never discusses it.)

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry's only treatment of performed, non-sincere speech is the actor on stage. The actor represents himself as asserting and as believing, but the audience is not invited to attribute the belief to the actor. This is the nearest analogue to a speaker-indexed, make-believe utterance. It is discussed only to show that 'represent oneself as believing' must be weaker than world-representing and stronger than stage representing. — The entry's own discussion, in the context of Black and Davidson on self-representation

it must also be stronger than the sense of "represent" by which an actor can be said to represent himself as believing something on stage... But the audience not invited to believe that the speaker, that is, the actor, has that belief.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Force is separable from sentence form and from convention. Davidson, and Bach and Harnish, say any conventional force-indicator can be used where the corresponding force is missing, including impersonation and theatrical performance. This supports the thesis that a fluent assertoric-looking output need not carry assertoric force by virtue of its surface features. — Davidson (1979, 1984); Bach & Harnish (1979)

any conventional sign could be used (and would be used) in insincere utterances, where the corresponding force was missing, including cases of deception, jokes, impersonation and other theatrical performances.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Pagin's cognitive account makes assertion prima facie informative. The hearer's default uptake is tied to surface features such as declarative form. This bears on the claim that users 'can read literally': users take the default hearer stance regardless of what the producer is doing. — Pagin (2011, 2020)

it is the cognitive patterns associated with surface features, on the production and comprehension sides, that characterize assertion.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Stalnaker's model has content entering the common ground by acceptance for the purposes of conversation, without belief. The roles of assertion are shared with assuming and conjecturing. This is a precedent for pretence-like acceptance without sincere assertion. — Stalnaker (1974, 1978, 2002)

What is added to the common ground is only for the purpose of conversation, and need not be actually believed by the participants.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Searle's account of assertion rests on constitutive rules, preparatory and sincerity conditions, and reflexive intentions. The entry gives no pretence or 'pretended illocution' account. This is what the thesis says is 'not established', so the entry leaves that point open rather than confirming or denying it. — Searle (1969, 1975b, 1979); Searle & Vanderveken (1985)

The fifth rule is the crucial one, and is held to be constitutive of assertion.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Hearer-directed accounts, namely self-representation and commitment, locate assertoric force in what the speaker presents or answers for. A chatbot output with no accountable speaker would fail those conditions, which pressures the claim that outputs are indexed to a represented speaker. Pagin's objection to commitment sufficiency also shows hearer-directed accounts can be separated from assertion. — Black (1952); Davidson (1984); Brandom (1994); Peirce; Green; Pagin (2004)

in asserting that p the speaker represents herself as believing that p.

Nearest positions. Self-representation account: asserting is representing oneself as believing or knowing, with the actor-on-stage contrast as limiting case — Black (1952), Davidson (1984), Unger (1975), Slote (1979), Green (2007); Force is not carried by conventional indicators, since any such sign can be used in impersonation or theatre without force — Davidson (1979, 1984), Bach & Harnish (1979); Commitment and constitutive-rule accounts of assertion, where sincere assertion is distinguished by accountability or rules — Searle (1969), Brandom (1994), Green, Pagin (2004) on insufficiency

The entry is silent on. Chatbots, language models and any artificial speaker are never mentioned. The entry also has no account of pretence or make-believe (Walton-style), nor of a Searlean 'pretended illocution', nor of who bears the make-believe, speaker or audience. It does not address post-training or its effect on the repertoire of outputs. It does not say whether a hearer reading literally can be correct when the speaker does not assert.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — fiction

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Outputs are attributed to a represented teller, not just to the real author. Currie's 'fictional author' is a character constructed within our make-believe who tells the story as known fact. Lewis's footnote lets the teller be a raver, a translator and so on, and says to consider worlds where the telling really is whatever it purports to be. — Currie 1990; Lewis 1978 (fn. 7); Byrne 1993 (idealised Author)

that fictional character constructed within our make-believe whom we take to be telling us the story as known fact

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Walton places the make-believe in consumers. A work is fiction if it is a prop prescribing imaginings by readers. The entry says authors may also pretend, but that what is crucial is the requirement on consumers. — Walton 1990

what is crucial to a work of fiction is that this is a requirement on consumers of fiction

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Searle's pretended-illocution account is reported as inadequate: pretense is neither necessary nor sufficient for fiction, since mimicry and irony also involve pretended illocutions. Kripke and Lewis used pretense without defining fiction by it. This backs the claim that calling the base case Searle's pretended illocution is not established. — Currie 1990: 17; Searle 1975; Lewis; Kripke

not only is pretense not necessary for something to be (part of) a work of fiction, it is also not sufficient

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Readers may engage with texts without adopting the fictive or imaginative mode. Matravers says experience is the same whether or not the reader believes the text is fiction. Friend says readers differ in expectations and reading strategy. Walton says one can read without asking what the author meant. This lends some support to users reading literally, though none of it concerns chatbots. — Matravers 2014; Friend 2012; Walton 1990

The experience of reading de Quincey's "The Revolt of the Tartars" is the same whether we believe it is non-fictional, believe it is fictional, or ... we are ignorant

ADJACENT (2/5) — Searle locates the pretense in the author's illocutionary intentions, not in the audience. Currie, and Lamarque and Olsen, locate fictionality in the author's intention that readers make-believe. Both are author-side accounts a referee would raise. Genre-based classification (Friend) is another way context fixes the status of an output. — Searle 1975; Currie 1990; Lamarque & Olsen 1994; Friend 2012

the identifying criterion for whether or not a text is a work of fiction must of necessity lie in the illocutionary intentions of the author

Nearest positions. Pretended illocution by the author — Searle 1975 (adopted by Kripke and Lewis without defining fiction by it); Fiction as prop in consumers' games of make-believe — Walton 1990; Fictive utterance with a fictional author or teller constructed in our make-believe — Currie 1990, 1998

The entry is silent on. Chatbots, language models, AI-generated text, post-training or fine-tuning, and any interactive or non-authored speaker persona. The entry also does not discuss whether a persona's repertoire narrows or its indexing is removed, or any empirical question of that kind. Its fictional-author and narrator discussion concerns authored literary works only.
