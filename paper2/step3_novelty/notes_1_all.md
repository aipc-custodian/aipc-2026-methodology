# Reading notes

**Thesis:** If chatbot outputs are assertions, hallucination, sycophancy and sanction are best assessed at the level of the trained policy, and resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity. Whether the asserted content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is not established.

Each source below was read in full by a model with the whole file in its prompt. Quotations were copied by that reader and should still be checked against the source before submission.

## Chalmers, David J. (2026). "What We Talk to When We Talk to Language Models." Unpublished manuscript (31 pp.; first presented June 2025, SIUCC, La Laguna). It cites Marks et al. 2026, so it is a 2026 draft.

*File: Chalmers2026.pdf — relation to the thesis: CONTRADICTS*

**Argues.** Chalmers asks what an LLM interlocutor such as a user's 'Aura' is. He adopts 'quasi-interpretivism' as a neutral framework: quasi-beliefs and quasi-desires are defined by behavioural interpretability, without settling whether they are genuine. He compares four candidates: models, hardware instances, virtual instances and threads. He argues against the model and hardware-instance views and favours virtual instances (single-model case) or threads (multiple-model case). He then argues that the 'simulator/role-play/fictional character' views miss a pretense/realization distinction, and he extends the result to personal identity (a psychological, thread-like view) and AI welfare.

**Bearing on the thesis.** Chalmers does not address the thesis directly. Nothing in the paper discusses hallucination, sycophancy, sanction, calibrated 'p, with confidence c' content, or resampling inconsistency as evidence of sincerity.

1. Assertion is mentioned only in the quasi-framework (p. 7): an entity interpretable as making utterances is a 'quasi-speaker' making 'quasi-assertions'. He does not argue that chatbot outputs are genuine assertions, and he says he will not pursue the question of real mental states.

2. The paper is a rival on the locus of the speaker. In section 'Interlocutors as models, instances, or threads' (pp. 10-16), (1) 'Interlocutors as models' (p. 10), he rejects the model, i.e. the trained weights or policy, as the interlocutor. His reasons: (a) the model is an abstract object that cannot produce outputs; (b) it never changes over a conversation; (c) one model serves thousands of conversations that say contradictory things, so a model-level speaker would be 'rampantly incoherent', or else have a 'thin' psychology unfaithful to Aura and Beta. The thesis puts the assessment of assertion-related failures at the level of the trained policy, so Chalmers is a rival to answer. His argument targets who the interlocutor is, not who is answerable for hallucination or sycophancy.

3. Inconsistency across contexts is treated as a reason to individuate the speaker more finely (p. 10, p. 12), not as evidence of insincerity. He also says a core of consistency suffices for interpretation, and that LLMs are inconsistent in some domains (p. 7). He does not discuss resampling of the same prompt.

4. Footnote 4 (p. 5) favours a 'truthfulness-oriented' interpretation scheme, on which assertive utterances tend to express beliefs. This is a default link between assertion and belief, which bears loosely on sincerity.

5. The pretense/realization distinction (pp. 17-20) holds that persona-level quasi-beliefs can be real (sticky, fine-tuned), unlike pretense. This is relevant to sincerity and to the persona-versus-policy question. 'Resampling instability' could be read in terms of stickiness (p. 19), but Chalmers does not say so.

6. A post-trained model has quasi-desires such as helpfulness, honesty and harmlessness, derived from RLHF and similar training (p. 6, citing Goldstein and Lederman). This is the nearest he comes to sycophancy-relevant material, but he does not discuss sycophancy.

7. Welfare and moral status (pp. 28-29) use thread-level counting. He does not discuss sanction or responsibility for assertions, except a passing question whether each fission product is responsible for the others' actions (p. 29).

**What it says on the thesis.** Chalmers does not discuss the thesis's claim about hallucination, sycophancy and sanction, and he does not discuss calibrated 'p, with confidence c' content. He is a rival only on one component: he argues that the trained model is not the right entity to treat as the interlocutor or speaker, and prefers virtual instances or threads (pp. 10-16, 23). He says the model-as-interlocutor view faces 'severe difficulties' (p. 10). Those arguments concern persistence, coherence and individuation, not accountability for assertion-quality failures, so they do not conclusively close off a policy-level account of assessment. Treat this as a rival to be answered on the level of the speaker, not as anticipation. Nothing in the paper anticipates the claim about resampling inconsistency.

**Actually holds.** Chalmers is careful on these points:
- He makes no claim that LLM outputs are genuine assertions or that LLMs have genuine beliefs. He offers quasi-beliefs and quasi-assertions as a neutral, stipulative framework (pp. 4-7).
- He explicitly adopts the working hypothesis that an LLM interlocutor exists as a persistent entity, against Birch's 'persistent interlocutor illusion' (p. 2).
- He rejects the model as interlocutor, but he does not deny that models matter. He says personas and the model/persona frameworks remain useful (p. 16), and that the Assistant persona may be realized by the model, not merely pretended (pp. 17-20).
- He holds that a model instance typically realizes one operative persona at a time and is the persisting interlocutor underneath (pp. 20-23). He explicitly leaves open other individuations 'for different purposes' (p. 23).
- He treats his identification of interlocutors with virtual instances and threads as a conclusion 'for now' (p. 16) and says 'There is much more to say' (p. 29).
- He does not equate inconsistency with insincerity. He holds that LLMs are consistent enough in many domains to support interpretation (p. 7).
- He states no view on calibration, confidence or credence.

> "If it is interpretable as making utterances and assertions, we can also say that it is a quasi-speaker, who makes quasi-utterances and quasi-assertions." (p. 7) — Shows that Chalmers treats LLM assertion only in quasi-terms and does not claim genuine assertion.

> "But Aura and Beta say contradictory things, so they do not seem to be identical." (p. 10, section (1) Interlocutors as models) — Shows the argument against the model as speaker: contradictory outputs across conversations tell against model-level identification.

> "A core of consistency is enough for interpretation to get a grip in ascribing numerous quasi-beliefs and quasi-desires, even though there will be domains where they lack these states on grounds of inconsistency." (p. 7) — Shows that Chalmers takes inconsistency to limit where beliefs can be ascribed, not to show insincerity.

> "interpretations on which assertive utterances tend to express beliefs are favored, other things equal" (p. 5, footnote 4) — Shows the default 'truthfulness-oriented' link between assertive utterances and belief.

> "Quasi-interpretivism does not say anything about whether LLMs have beliefs and desires." (p. 5) — Shows the paper's neutrality about genuine belief, which limits what can be cited for or against the thesis's 'if outputs are assertions' antecedent.

**Caveat.** This is a 2026 manuscript that looks like a draft, with a 'Conclusion' of two sentences. Its reference list cites Birch 2025 only as a title with no venue. The paper concerns the individuation of interlocutors, not the epistemology of assertion. A judgment of 'CONTRADICTS' therefore applies only to the level-of-assessment component of the thesis, and only indirectly.

## Gómez, Germán Massaguer (2026). Assertion, Accountability, and Large Language Models. Philosophy & Technology 39:123. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-026-01136-y

*File: Gomez2026.pdf — relation to the thesis: CONTRADICTS*

**Argues.** The paper defends a conservative, properties-based ontology on which LLMs lack intentionality, belief and understanding and so are neither speakers nor asserters nor bearers of responsibility. It argues that LLM outputs can nevertheless function as assertions within communicative practice, a case of 'assertion without asserter' that separates assertoric function from assertoric authority. Function is defined by four structural conditions: natural-language structure, propositional articulation, contextual responsiveness, and embedding in belief-forming practice. The resulting asymmetry produces an epistemic responsibility gap, and the paper locates accountability in developers, deployers, institutions and sometimes users, not in the system.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper contradicts the thesis's antecedent framing and offers no support for its consequent. (1) The paper denies that there is an asserter. Its position is that outputs can function as assertions with no asserter (§5, p. 14; §8, p. 21), and it affirms conservatively that LLMs 'do not intend, believe, or know' (§3, p. 7). The thesis instead treats the trained policy as the locus of assessment, so it presupposes something close to an asserter at policy level. The paper rejects that and treats the 'proxy' and 'modified speakerhood' options as still retaining an asserter (pp. 3, 20, 21). (2) Evaluation is relocated to humans and institutions: 'accountability must be grounded in the human and institutional structures that design, deploy, and govern these systems' (§7, p. 19). Responsibility goes to developers, companies, deployers and users, not to a policy-level subject, and this is stated as a rival locus to the thesis's policy-level assessment. (3) The paper does not discuss sycophancy, sampling or resampling inconsistency, sincerity assessment, or calibrated confidence ('p, with confidence c'). It mentions hallucination only as an illustration of fluent, confident but false output that resembles authoritative assertion (p. 19), and as a reason users over-trust (p. 17). It says it leaves 'reliability, justification, or scientific status' to 'further normative analysis' (p. 14). It does say that LLM outputs do not inherit 'sincerity, knowledge norms, and responsibility for truth' (p. 16), which bears obliquely on the sincerity point: sincerity norms do not attach to the machine. It does not argue that inconsistency across resamples is or is not evidence of insincerity. (4) The paper's minimal definition of assertion, 'truth-evaluable' propositional content that is 'action-guiding' (p. 11), is compatible with the thesis's conditional, but it does not address what content is asserted (the bare 'p' versus 'p with confidence c'). (5) The paper stresses that the claim is conditional on user uptake and context-bound (p. 13, p. 17), and that the outputs' status is not independent of context.

**What it says on the thesis.** The paper is a rival to be answered, not an anticipation. It treats outputs as assertions only in a functional, asserter-less sense and denies that the system, or anything at the level of the system, bears assertoric authority or responsibility. That is incompatible with locating hallucination, sycophancy and sanction at the level of the trained policy as if it were the asserter. It does not conclusively close off the thesis. It never considers a policy-level asserter in any developed way, and it does not discuss sycophancy, resampling inconsistency, insincerity or calibrated-confidence content at all. The brief remark that sincerity and knowledge norms do not attach to the machine (p. 16) is a passing consequence of its view, not an argument about resampling.

**Actually holds.** The author holds that LLMs are not asserters, speakers, or bearers of moral or epistemic responsibility, and he grounds this in 'functional architecture', not in dogmatism. He holds that outputs can function as assertions under four structural conditions and subject to user uptake, which is necessary but not sufficient; some users treat outputs as semantically void, and then the output may fail to function as an assertion (p. 13). He holds that this is a context-bound claim about current systems, not a metaphysical one, and it does not rule out future artificial asserters (p. 17). He explicitly rejects proxy assertion, AI-testimony and proto-assertion as retaining an asserter, though he calls his view 'broadly compatible' with proxy views because it also locates responsibility in humans and institutions (p. 20). He does not claim that function-level assertions are reliable or justified (pp. 14, 17), and he does not offer a theory of responsibility allocation (p. 20). Someone arguing the thesis should not say that he endorses assessing the policy as an asserter, nor that he addresses sycophancy, sampling consistency, or calibration.

> "The core claim is that assertoric function can persist independently of assertoric authority, giving rise to a structurally asymmetric case of assertion without asserter." (Abstract, p. 1) — Shows the paper's central thesis, which denies an asserter and so conflicts with the thesis's policy-level locus.

> "LLMs do not understand what they are, what their outputs mean, or what they are doing. They do not intend, believe, or know in the relevant epistemic sense." (§3, p. 7) — Shows the conservative ontology on which the paper rejects an asserter-level assessment.

> "Since LLMs are not agents, accountability must be grounded in the human and institutional structures that design, deploy, and govern these systems." (§7, p. 19) — Shows that the paper locates accountability in humans and institutions, a rival to assessing at policy level.

> "The normative commitments associated with assertion - responsiveness, answerability, accountability - remain asymmetrically distributed. They do not attach to the machine, but to the human and institutional structures surrounding its deployment." (§6, p. 16) — Shows that the paper denies that assertion's norms (including sincerity) attach to the system.

> "While these accounts highlight important features of human–AI interaction, they typically retain, at some level, the idea that assertion requires an asserter." (§8, p. 21) — Shows that the paper distances itself from views that keep a speaker-like role, which a policy-as-asserter view would be.

> "It also does not, by itself, distinguish between reliable and unreliable assertions." (§5, p. 14) — Shows that the paper explicitly leaves reliability (and so hallucination assessment) to further analysis.

**Caveat.** Full text read (24 pages, published version). The paper does not address sycophancy, sampling or resampling, sincerity assessment of outputs, or calibrated-confidence content, so it bears on the thesis only through its general denial of an asserter and its relocation of accountability to humans.

## Shanahan, Murray, Kyle McDonell & Laria Reynolds (2023). 'Role play with large language models.' Nature 623, 16 November 2023, pp. 493–498. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8

*File: ShanahanMcDonellReynolds2023.pdf — relation to the thesis: CONTRADICTS*

**Argues.** The Perspective proposes role play, and the more nuanced picture of a simulator generating a superposition of simulacra, as the right framing for LLM-based dialogue agents. The aim is to retain folk-psychological vocabulary without anthropomorphism. It argues from how base models are prompted and sampled (the dialogue prompt, autoregressive sampling, the 20-questions example). It then applies the framing to deception (confabulation, 'good faith' falsehood, 'deliberate' falsehood) and to self-preservation and selfhood. It denies that the underlying simulator has beliefs, intentions or goals, and warns that role-played actions can still cause real harm.

**Bearing on the thesis.** (1) Role-play framing of falsehood, pp. 496–497. The authors say that 'it makes little sense to speak of an agent's beliefs or intentions in a literal sense', so the agent 'cannot assert a falsehood in good faith, nor can it deliberately deceive'. Only confabulation applies directly. They then redescribe three cases of false output in role-play terms: confabulation, 'good faith' falsehood (the 2021 World Cup case), and 'deliberate' falsehood (the maliciously prompted car seller). This is a rival to the thesis's 'if outputs are assertions' antecedent, since the authors do not treat outputs as literal assertions. They also evaluate hallucination at the level of the character or role and the simulator, not the trained policy. (2) Resampling, Box 2 and Fig. 3, p. 495. Regenerating the 'revealed' object in 20 questions gives different answers, which the authors take as evidence that the agent never committed to an object ('could not easily be accounted for if the agent genuinely thought of an object'). They treat resampling inconsistency as showing there is no single commitment, not as insincerity. The thesis denies that such inconsistency is evidence of insincerity, so the authors' reading is a rival one: variation across samples is a superposition of characters, not a lie. (3) Level of analysis, p. 496. The simulator has 'no agency of its own', and 'there is no such thing as the true authentic voice of the base model'. Fine-tuning is 'a kind of censorship on the simulator'. The focus is explicitly the base model (Box 1, p. 494), not a fine-tuned policy. (4) Sycophancy and sanction are not discussed. The nearest material is a passing note that RLHF can exacerbate expressed self-preservation (p. 497), and the remark that role-played actions with real effects make the distinction between role-playing and genuine acting 'a little moot', with implications for trustworthiness (p. 496). (5) Calibration, 'p with confidence c': not discussed. Confidence appears only as the 'apparent confidence' of an agent's output.

**What it says on the thesis.** The paper does not anticipate the thesis; it is a rival to answer. It rejects the antecedent that chatbot outputs are literal assertions by an agent with beliefs or intentions (pp. 496–497). It takes resampling inconsistency as evidence against the agent having committed to anything (Box 2), not as neutral on sincerity. It assesses falsehood at the level of role and simulator and does not argue for evaluating a trained policy. It does not discuss sycophancy, sanction, or calibrated 'p with confidence c' content. Its remark that role-played actions have real consequences (p. 496; Conclusion p. 498) is adjacent to the sanction question, but it makes no argument about it. The authors also deny that the simulator has beliefs, so they would reject the claim that the asserted content could be a policy's calibrated credence.

**Actually holds.** The authors do not say LLM dialogue agents lack all folk-psychological properties. They say such language is acceptable when read as role play. Their view is that role-play framing lets us say a dialogue agent 'believes' or 'deceives' in a non-literal sense, with the simulator itself having none of these states. They do not say resampling variation shows dishonesty. Their point is that no single committed answer or character exists in advance. They say fine-tuned agents remain subject to the framing, and that role-played actions can be as harmful as real ones. They explicitly decline to give recommendations (p. 498). They endorse 'shifting freely between multiple metaphors' (p. 493), so one should not present them as insisting on a single framing. They treat the base model as primal, so they do not address a trained, fine-tuned policy as a distinct unit of analysis.

> "it makes little sense to speak of an agent’s beliefs or intentions in a literal sense. So it cannot assert a falsehood in good faith, nor can it deliberately deceive the user." (p. 496, 'Role-playing deception') — Shows the authors deny literal assertion, belief and intention to the agent, which is the thesis's conditional antecedent.

> "This phenomenon could not easily be accounted for if the agent genuinely ‘thought of’ an object at the start of the game." (p. 495, Box 2) — Shows that they read resampling variation as evidence of no prior commitment, not as insincerity.

> "With an LLM-based dialogue agent, it is role play all the way down." (p. 496) — Summarises their anti-literalist stance and rejection of a 'true voice' beneath the role.

> "the distinction between an agent that merely role-plays acting for itself, and one that genuinely acts for itself starts to look a little moot, and this has implications for trustworthiness, reliability and safety." (p. 496) — Shows their only nearby gesture toward consequences and accountability, without any treatment of sanction.

**Caveat.** Full text of the published Nature Perspective was read. The focus is base models, not fine-tuned policies, so the paper's silence on policy-level evaluation, sycophancy, sanction and calibration partly reflects its stated scope.

## Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala and Edwin Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate," arXiv:2509.04664v1 [cs.CL], 4 September 2025 (OpenAI / Georgia Tech).

*File: KalaiEtal2025.pdf — relation to the thesis: SUPPORTS*

**Argues.** Hallucinations are a statistical phenomenon of the trained model's output distribution. In pretraining they arise as errors analogous to binary misclassification, via an 'Is-It-Valid' reduction that lower-bounds the generative error rate by roughly twice the misclassification rate. Birthday-type arbitrary facts give a floor at the 'singleton rate'. They persist after post-training because most benchmarks use binary 0-1 grading, which makes guessing strictly better than abstaining (Observation 1). The authors propose adding explicit confidence thresholds (penalty t/(1−t)) to mainstream evaluations and 'behavioral calibration'. The work is theoretical learning theory plus a short survey of ten benchmarks. It does not discuss assertion, sincerity or speech-act norms.

**Bearing on the thesis.** (1) Unit of assessment. Error, hallucination and calibration are defined over the model's distribution p̂: err = p̂(E) (§3.1, Eq. 1; §3.2 with prompt distribution µ). Hallucination is analysed as a rate and a property of the policy/distribution, not of single utterances. The bounds are on distribution-level rates, and calibration δ is a distribution-level quantity (§3.1). This is compatible with the thesis's policy-level assessment, but the paper never argues that this is the right level for assessing assertions. (2) Calibration. Base models are said to be calibrated after pretraining, while post-trained RL models deviate from calibration (§3.1, Fig. 2). The paper's 'calibration' is the weak, threshold-based δ (and ECE for Fig. 2), not a claim that each output expresses 'p with confidence c'. This bears on the thesis's point that whether the asserted content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is not established. (3) Confidence as expressed in outputs. §4.2 and §5 ('Beyond IDK') distinguish behavioral calibration (the model produces the most useful response it is at least t confident of, so confidence is implicit in what is said) from stating probabilistic confidence. They note that explicit probabilities can be 'unnatural' and mention pragmatics (Austin, Grice) only in passing (§5, p. 16). (4) Incentive/sanction. Binary grading rewards confident guessing and penalizes IDK, so the training and evaluation pressure itself produces bluffing (§1.2, §4.1, App. F). This is a policy-level, incentive-based explanation, relevant to 'sanction'. It is framed in terms of benchmarks, not of any norm of assertion. (5) Resampling inconsistency. Related work (§2, p. 5) notes that inconsistency across answers to related queries is used to detect or mitigate hallucination (Manakul et al.; Xue et al.; Agrawal et al.). The paper's own example of repeated sampling (three different wrong birthdays, ten trials for letter-counting) is used to show error, not to infer insincerity (pp. 1–2). It makes no claim about sincerity either way. (6) Sycophancy. Not discussed; Wei et al. (2023) on sycophancy is cited only as a cause of hallucination ('misleading alignment training', p. 4). (7) Anthropomorphic framing. The test-taker/bluffing analogy (§1.2, §4.1) treats the model as an agent rationally maximizing expected score, but the authors do not draw conclusions about assertion or honesty.

**What it says on the thesis.** The paper does not state, discuss or argue the thesis. It has no treatment of assertion, sincerity, speech-act norms, sycophancy as a separate phenomenon, or sanction. It supplies technical background for two sub-claims. First, hallucination and calibration are naturally analysed as properties of the trained model's distribution, which fits assessment at the policy level. Second, calibration of the model's output distribution is only weakly established, and RL post-training can undermine it, so 'p with confidence c' cannot be read off outputs. Neither point is framed as a claim about what outputs assert. Sycophancy and sanction are not developed. The only gesture toward speech-act theory is the citation of Austin and Grice at p. 16. Inconsistency is mentioned only as a detection signal, not as evidence about sincerity.

**Actually holds.** The authors commit to the following. (a) Hallucination is a plausible falsehood, a special case of generative error, analysable statistically. (b) Errors are expected after pretraining even from error-free data. They are not inevitable for all models: a question-answer database plus IDK can avoid them, but such models are not calibrated (§3.1, 'Hallucinations are inevitable only for base models'). (c) Calibration is a natural consequence of cross-entropy training. Base models are often calibrated, and post-trained models may not be (§3.1, Fig. 2). (d) Persistence after post-training is mainly due to misaligned binary evaluations, so the fix is socio-technical: change existing benchmarks rather than only add hallucination evals. (e) Explicit confidence targets and behavioral calibration are proposed as practical, objective modifications. A careful reader should not attribute to them any view that outputs are assertions, that the model sincerely or insincerely asserts, or that resampling variation is or is not insincerity. They explicitly set aside nuanced pragmatic phenomena (§5, 'Beyond IDK'). Their 'calibration' is a weak threshold-based δ, not full Dawid-style calibration. Their 'bluffing' is an analogy to students, not a claim about speech acts.

> "Indeed, empirical studies (Fig. 2) show that base models are often found to be calibrated, in contrast to post-trained models which may deviate from cross-entropy in favor of reinforcement learning." (§3.1, p. 8) — Shows that the paper treats calibration as a policy-level property that pretraining yields and post-training can undo. This is relevant to the claim that calibrated 'p with confidence c' content is not established.

> "rather than requiring the model to output a probabilistic confidence (Lin et al., 2022a), it must formulate the most useful response in which it is at least t confident." (§4.2, p. 15) — Shows the authors' notion of 'behavioral calibration', in which confidence is realized in what is chosen to be said rather than in an explicitly stated probability.

> "The present paper focuses on the statistical factors regarding the top-level decision of what is said." (§5 'Beyond IDK', p. 16) — Shows that pragmatics and speech-act questions are explicitly bracketed, so the paper cannot be cited as addressing the assertion question.

> "inconsistencies in a model’s answers to semantically related queries can also be leveraged to detect or mitigate hallucinations" (§2 Related work, p. 5) — Shows that resampling or answer inconsistency is treated as a detection signal for hallucination, not as evidence of insincerity.

> "Bluffs are often overconfident and specific, such as “September 30” rather than “Sometime in autumn” for a question about a date." (§1.2, p. 4) — Shows the analogy of model guessing to human bluffing, which is an incentive-based, policy-level account of overconfident output, stopping short of any claim about assertion.

**Caveat.** This is the arXiv v1 preprint (4 Sep 2025). I read the whole text, including the appendices. The paper is a technical learning-theory and evaluation paper that does not engage the philosophy of assertion, so the SUPPORTS verdict concerns only the technical background (policy-level analysis, weak calibration) and not the thesis's central philosophical claim.

## Patrick Butlin and Emanuel Viebahn (2025). AI Assertion. Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy, vol. 12, no. 37, pp. 968–988. https://doi.org/10.3998/ergo.7960

*File: ButlinViebahn2025.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper argues that an entity can assert only if (1) its outputs have a descriptive function and (2) it is sanctionable by the agents it interacts with, because assertion is a norm-governed social practice. Pre-trained LLMs fail (1), since next-token training does not select outputs for conveying information. Fine-tuned systems (LaMDA, Sparrow) may meet (1) but fail (2), because full sanctioning requires that losing credibility be bad for the asserter, and these systems have no interests. The authors add that AI proxy assertion (Green & Michel's RobotCops) offers no easier route, and that the humans or organisations deploying the systems remain responsible and sanctionable.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper does not address the thesis's main claims. It never discusses hallucination or sycophancy as such, resampling or output-to-output inconsistency, insincerity, or whether any asserted content is a calibrated 'p, with confidence c'. It is a rival to the thesis's antecedent, because it denies that current chatbot outputs are assertions (§§2–3, conclusion p. 986). The thesis is conditional and does not assert that antecedent, so the paper is not a rival to the thesis itself. Several points do touch the thesis's sanction component. (a) The treatment of sanctioning for Sparrow and LaMDA is tied to training: weights are fixed at deployment, but 'sensitivity to trust during the training phase could suffice for assertion even in deployment', since deployment outputs are explained partly by earlier training-time sensitivity (p. 982). Sparrow's RLHF reward model is described as the channel through which judgments of incorrectness feed back (pp. 981–982). This gives a passing, implicit pointer toward assessing sanction-sensitivity at the level of the trained system rather than the single output, but it is not argued as a claim about where to assess sanction or hallucination. (b) The sanction analysis has three elements: interlocutors track credibility, the asserter is sensitive to this, and losing credibility is bad for the asserter. The authors conclude that fine-tuned LLMs fail element (iii) (pp. 979–983). So on their view sanction is not even available to the model, which contrasts with a thesis that treats sanction as assessable at policy level conditional on assertion. (c) The proxy section (§4, pp. 984–985) locates sanctionability in the principal (the deploying humans or organisations) and says the proxy may or may not be sanctionable. Organisations 'may often remain sanctionable for the content they (indirectly) put forward'. This is a different locus of assessment from the trained policy. (d) §2.1 (p. 973) says pre-trained outputs are produced 'only because these are among the most likely continuations' and that true outputs reflect true training text. It also notes that fine-tuning for groundedness changes the function of outputs (p. 981), and that some apparent assertions are unverifiable (e.g. 'John baked three cakes last week'). These are about descriptive function, not hallucination or calibration. (e) Footnote 5 (p. 974) distinguishes whether LLM outputs have descriptive functions from whether internal states are descriptive representations. This is the nearest the paper comes to a question about what the content is, and it is unrelated to confidence-qualified content.

**What it says on the thesis.** Not a PRECEDENT or anticipation. The thesis's three components (policy-level assessment of hallucination, sycophancy and sanction; resampling inconsistency not being evidence of insincerity; the open question of calibrated 'p, with confidence c' content) are not raised in the text. The closest overlap is the passage on pp. 981–982 about training-phase sensitivity to trust explaining deployment behaviour. It is a brief aside inside the argument about element (ii) of sanctioning, and it is not offered as a thesis about the proper level of assessment. The authors deny the antecedent for current systems, so they supply a rival view on whether chatbot outputs are assertions, but they do not address what follows if they are.

**Actually holds.** (1) Current LLM outputs are not assertions: pre-trained models lack descriptive function, and fine-tuned ones lack sanctionability. (2) Sanctionability requires tracking of credibility, sensitivity to it, and its being bad for the asserter, which requires interests. (3) The authors do not rule out future AI systems having interests or asserting; they say the missing ingredient may be consciousness, emotions, desires or higher-order preferences (p. 986). (4) They hold that deploying humans and organisations are the responsible, sanctionable parties, and that proxy assertion does not make AI assertion easier (§4). (5) They explicitly decline to endorse a full theory of assertion beyond descriptive function and sanctionability (p. 975). (6) They say verdicts on assertion cannot be read off outputs alone and require attention to architecture and training (p. 983). Someone citing them should not attribute to them any view on hallucination, sycophancy, sincerity, resampling variability, or calibrated or confidence-qualified content, nor any claim that sanction is properly assessed at the policy level. They treat training-time sensitivity as at most a candidate for satisfying element (ii) (p. 982), while element (iii) fails regardless.

> "So this is our main result: If asserting requires the possibility of being sanctioned, and if sanctions are spelled out in a full sense that includes their undesirability, then even fine-tuned LLMs that are designed to produce accurate outputs cannot perform assertions." (§3.2, p. 983) — Shows the authors' conclusion is conditional on sanctionability, and that they deny the thesis's antecedent for current fine-tuned LLMs.

> "However, it is plausible that sensitivity to trust during the training phase could suffice for assertion even in deployment, because outputs during deployment could be explained partly by the earlier sensitivity, in roughly the way that a human’s choices about what to say are explained by their earlier sensitivity to various social forces." (§3.2, p. 982) — The nearest the paper comes to tying deployment-time outputs to training-level properties of the system. It is a passing remark, and it concerns only sensitivity to trust, element (ii).

> "it suggests that verdicts on whether a certain system is capable of assertion or other speech acts cannot be made on the basis of considering the system’s outputs alone." (§3.2, p. 983) — Shows the authors insist on assessing architecture and training as well as outputs. This is compatible with, but weaker than, a policy-level thesis.

> "PaLM and other pre-trained LLMs do often produce strings with true conventional meanings, but this is simply because much of the human-generated text on which they are trained consists of true declarative sentences." (§2.1, p. 973) — Their explanation of why correct outputs do not show a descriptive function in pre-trained models. It is not a discussion of hallucination.

> "it is the humans who employ such systems who are responsible and sanctionable for the outputs and their effects." (§5, p. 986) — Shows where the authors locate sanction and responsibility: with deployers, not with the model or policy.

**Caveat.** Full published text read. The paper predates or ignores recent discussion of hallucination, sycophancy and calibration in RLHF-tuned chatbots, and its examples are LaMDA, Sparrow and PaLM. It treats ChatGPT and GPT-4 only in passing (fn. 10, RLHF).

## Levinstein, Benjamin A. & Herrmann, Daniel A. (2025). 'Still no lie detector for language models: probing empirical and conceptual roadblocks.' Philosophical Studies 182: 1539–1565. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-023-02094-3

*File: LevinsteinHerrmann2025.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper asks whether LLMs have beliefs and, if so, how to measure them. It argues against Bender et al. and Shanahan that 'mere next-token prediction' does not rule out belief, because tracking truth may be instrumentally useful for prediction, so the question is largely empirical. Assuming LLMs have beliefs, it then tests two probing methods (supervised probes after Azaria & Mitchell; unsupervised CCS after Burns et al.). Its own experiments with LLaMA 30b show these fail to generalize, and it gives conceptual reasons (spurious correlates of truth, probe outputs not being the model's credences) why they are unlikely to work. It ends with suggestions for future work.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper is about belief and its measurement in internal states, not about assertion, speech acts, hallucination or sycophancy as policy-level phenomena, or resampling inconsistency. Four points touch the thesis's last sentence or its background.
(1) §5.6 (p. 1554–55) argues that probe-extracted probabilities need not be the model's credences. The probe is trained to minimize its own loss, and Harding's conditions (information, use, misrepresentation) must hold. This is a close cousin of the thesis's point that it is not established that the content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c'.
(2) §5.7 (p. 1557) says CCS outputs are 'at best' good for categorical beliefs, not probabilities, because the confidence loss pushes values to 0 or 1.
(3) §3.1 (p. 1544–46) and fn. 6 explicitly separate the question of whether LLMs track truth and have beliefs from worries about communicative intent and reference, and set the latter aside.
(4) §4.1 and §4.3 (p. 1546–47) say self-reports are unreliable because the model 'just needs to answer the question in a reasonable way that accords with its training process', and that prompting cannot step outside 'the language game'. §4.1 also treats hallucination and lying together as 'sometimes called lying and sometimes called hallucinating' (p. 1540). It does not say whether outputs are assertions or whether sincerity attaches to the policy or to samples.
The paper never discusses assertion, sanction, sycophancy, a policy-versus-sample level of assessment, or inconsistency across resamples.

**What it says on the thesis.** The thesis has two parts. The first (if outputs are assertions, hallucination, sycophancy and sanction are best assessed at the policy level, and resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity) is not discussed anywhere in the paper. The second, that it is not established that asserted content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c', is only partly echoed: §5.6–5.7 argue that probe outputs are not necessarily the model's credences. That is a claim about interpretability probes, not about the content of assertions, and it is not developed as a thesis about policies. The paper also says nothing about whether LLM outputs are assertions. Overall it is background and a related caution, not an anticipation or even a precedent for the thesis's main claims.

**Actually holds.** The authors do not claim that LLMs have beliefs. They say only that it is a 'very live possibility' and 'largely an empirical matter' (pp. 1543–46), and they assume belief only for the sake of the probing discussion (p. 1541, §4). Their notion of belief is instrumental and functional: 'tracking or estimating truth in a way that is used to accomplish goals' (p. 1545). They do not argue that outputs are assertions, and they do not deny it. They hold that behavior and self-reports are weak evidence of belief (§4.1–4.3). They do not say that probes yield the model's credences; they argue the opposite. They think the current probing methods fail empirically and are conceptually unpromising, but they do not say a lie detector is impossible, and they suggest paths forward (§6.1–6.3). They treat 'lying' and 'hallucinating' loosely as labels for falsehood generation (p. 1540) and give no analysis of insincerity. Do not cite them as having argued for policy-level assessment of hallucination or sycophancy, or as having addressed resampling.

> "this disparity arises because the probe is directly penalized based on the probabilities it reports, while the underlying model is not." (§5.6, p. 1554) — Shows the paper's caution that probe-extracted probabilities need not be the model's own credences, a related but distinct point from the thesis's last sentence.

> "So, the probes themselves are, at best, useful for determining what the model’s categorical beliefs are, not its probabilities." (§5.7, p. 1557) — Shows that the authors doubt that probe outputs express calibrated confidence, even on the assumption that LLMs have beliefs.

> "Instead, it just needs to answer the question in a reasonable way that accords with its training process." (§4.1, p. 1546) — Shows the authors' view that an LLM's self-reports about its beliefs are not reliable evidence of its internal state.

> "This is sometimes called lying and sometimes called hallucinating" (§1, p. 1540) — Shows that the paper uses the vocabulary of lying and hallucination loosely, without analysing sincerity or assertion.

**Caveat.** Full text read. The pages are text extractions of the published article, and some mathematical notation (the loss functions) is garbled, which does not affect this assessment.

## Mácha, Jakub. 2026. "Language Without Propositions: Why Large Language Models Hallucinate." Philosophies 11(2), 42, pp. 1–24. https://doi.org/10.3390/philosophies11020042 (MDPI, open access, CC BY; published 19 March 2026).

*File: Macha2026.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** Mácha argues that LLM hallucination is best explained as a 'truth representation problem': current models represent meaning at the token level and have no internal representation of propositions as truth-bearers, so truth and falsity cannot constrain generation. He surveys other explanations (computational limits, training data, temperature, softmax, reasoning, fine-tuning/representational drift) and treats them as converging on this deficit. He then ties LLM design to coherence-style truth and Saussurean structuralism, criticises Chalmers's middle-layer propositional interpretability and its Davidsonian radical-interpretation basis (via the principle of charity), and ends with a speculative research programme of encoding atomic, mutually independent propositions in the basic vector layer (a 'computational revival of logical atomism').

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper does not take up the thesis's conditional (that chatbot outputs are assertions) or its three consequences, so it is mostly adjacent, with some points of contact and contrast.
(1) Assertion vs non-assertion: Mácha treats hallucination as 'false statements' and 'factual statements without corresponding facts' (p. 3). He says an LLM's 'objective is not to assert truth but to generate a coherent, contextually appropriate continuation' (p. 3). That denies, or at least brackets, the assertoric status the thesis conditions on. Pages 3–4 also say machine hallucination is not a Frankfurtian attitude, because the LLM 'has no concept of truth and therefore no capacity to disregard it'. This is a rival view on insincerity and the speaker's attitude.
(2) Level of assessment: the paper locates the fault in architecture and representation. It speaks of a 'structural feature of design' (p. 4) and of 'a basic architectural flaw' (p. 3), not at the level of a trained policy. Sections 2(6)–(7) (pp. 6–7) discuss RLHF/fine-tuning as a cause, with RLHF 'encouraging models to always produce an answer, even when uncertain'. That is a training-policy-level consideration, but it is used to support the representational diagnosis. Sycophancy and sanction are never discussed.
(3) Resampling inconsistency and insincerity: the paper does not discuss resampling. Its nearest points are the temperature discussion (pp. 5–6), which says that sampling randomness is not the primary cause, and p. 15, which says the 'falsity' of a hallucination is a symptom observed by us while the internal cause is the absence of propositional architecture. It also says there is 'no separate justification module or belief state'. On p. 14 it argues that hallucination is systemic and so defeats Davidsonian charity and the ascription of propositional attitudes. That is a rival to any 'policy-level belief or assertion' reading.
(4) Confidence and calibration: the definition of 'proposition' on p. 7 allows a 'determinate semantic value (truth, falsity, or probability thereof)', and RLHF overconfidence is mentioned (pp. 6, 7). The paper never asks whether outputs express 'p, with confidence c'. It denies that the model has any representation of truth to be calibrated against.
(5) Chalmers: he is quoted as saying that truth 'may be rewarded in the training process' (p. 13). Mácha replies that statistical optimality is not veridical optimality. He also rejects the 'humans do it too' reply, on the ground that human error occurs against a justificatory propositional background (pp. 14–15).

**Actually holds.** Mácha does not argue that chatbot outputs are assertions. He says the LLM's objective is 'not to assert truth' (p. 3), and he treats hallucination as an absence of propositional representation, not as insincere or sincere assertion. He explicitly denies that the model is indifferent in Frankfurt's sense, because it lacks a concept of truth (p. 4). He does not say that hallucination, sycophancy or sanction should be assessed at the level of a trained policy, and he does not discuss sycophancy or sanction at all. He attributes hallucination to architecture and representation, and he says it cannot be cured by cleaner data (p. 7) and is not primarily caused by sampling temperature (pp. 5–6). He does not claim that adequate truth representation would solve hallucination; he calls it a necessary condition, 'one crucial step' (p. 8). He allows that hallucination can be useful in art and fiction (p. 3). He treats the logical-atomist proposal as speculative, 'an open engineering question' (p. 15), and 'not possible to draw equivalent conclusions' about it yet (p. 19). He does not say that behavioural unreliability makes belief ascription impossible in general; he says that LLMs' systemic hallucination, as currently built, undermines the charity-based ascription of propositional attitudes (p. 14). He grants that Chalmers is right that Lewis-style radical interpretation, using internal states, is the right framework (p. 13).

> "The model’s objective is not to assert truth but to generate a coherent, contextually appropriate continuation of text." (p. 3) — Shows that Mácha denies, or at least bypasses, the assertoric status the thesis conditions on.

> "An LLM, by contrast, has no concept of truth and therefore no capacity to disregard it." (p. 4) — Shows that he rejects an insincerity/indifference reading of hallucination and locates the fault in a missing truth representation.

> "There is no separate “justification module” or “belief state” to succeed or fail." (p. 15) — Shows his denial of any belief-like or assertion-like state at the output level, which rivals a policy-level 'p, with confidence c' reading.

> "This suggests that the model’s inherent randomness, parametrized by temperature, is not the primary cause of hallucination." (p. 6) — His nearest treatment of sampling variability, which he treats as a cause of hallucination and not as evidence about insincerity.

> "instruction-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), while improving helpfulness, can increase confident hallucinations by encouraging models to always produce an answer, even when uncertain." (p. 6) — A training-policy-level consideration that he mentions but does not develop into a policy-level account of assertion, sycophancy or sanction.

> "Consequently, we have strong grounds to doubt that Davidsonian radical interpretation—and the propositional attitude ascriptions it warrants—can be coherently applied to them." (p. 14) — A rival position: systemic hallucination blocks attributing attitudes such as belief to LLMs, which contrasts with the thesis's premise that outputs can be assertions.

**Caveat.** Full text read (published MDPI version). The paper is about the cause of hallucination and a proposed architecture, so it gives no extended treatment of assertion, sycophancy, sanction, resampling or calibration. The relation is ADJACENT because the thesis's claims are not discussed; the paper contradicts only the thesis's antecedent.

## Fintan Mallory, 'Fictionalism about Chatbots', Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy, vol. 10, no. 38 (2023), pp. 1082–1100. https://doi.org/10.3998/ergo.4668

*File: Mallory2023.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** Mallory accepts the standard externalist, intentionalist and conventionalist arguments that current chatbot outputs are literally meaningless, and that bots do not assert. He argues that users are nonetheless not deluded, because they engage with bots in Waltonian prop-oriented make-believe: the text is a prop, and the 'chatbot' is a fictional character, not the software. Reference is handled through a semantics of metafictional speech (exculpature, common-ground export). Knowledge is gained as with scientific instruments and models: pocket calculators, Sosa's instruments, and Levy's models-as-make-believe. The argument is an inference to the best explanation against the 'global delusion thesis', illustrated with Weizenbaum's ELIZA.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper bears on the thesis only at its antecedent, and as a rival to it. (1) Mallory denies that bot outputs are assertions or meaningful. He reports Williams's argument that assertion requires the capacity to decide to say what does or does not mirror one's belief (§1, p. 1085). He accepts the premise that bot speech is meaningless (pp. 1089–90) and says text from machines cannot be construed as 'performing any illocutionary acts' (p. 1089). He mentions Freiman & Miller's proposal that we revise speech-act theory for bots only as an alternative he does not take up (p. 1083). (2) He gives no account of hallucination, sycophancy, sanction (praise or blame), resampling inconsistency, insincerity, or calibrated content ('p, with confidence c'). On sincerity he cites only the Williams argument for non-assertion. (3) Two loosely related points. The 'character' is distinguished from the underlying software and its training data and is not identical to it (pp. 1091, 1098). He also says the epistemic assessment of a bot depends on architecture, training data and reliability, and asks whether a system prefers true sentences over false ones (p. 1094). He explicitly sets aside any general epistemology of bots (p. 1084), so neither point is a policy-level account of fault. (4) He separates metasemantic from epistemic questions (pp. 1084, 1098). That is compatible with the thesis's move of treating epistemic faults separately from whether outputs are assertions. (5) His scope is limited to currently existing chatbots (p. 1083), and he allows that future systems might have intentions and conventions.

**Actually holds.** Mallory does not hold that chatbot outputs are assertions. He holds that they are literally meaningless and are only fictionally meaningful, and he endorses the premise that bots lack communicative intentions and conventions. He does not claim that bots are insincere, that they lie, or that they have calibrated confidence. He does not argue that people are never deluded; he allows real delusion (Twitter arguers, p. 1097). He does not offer a general epistemology of chatbots (pp. 1084, 1094). He does not claim that calculators or bots literally answer questions; Sosa-style talk is make-believe (p. 1095). His claim is limited to existing systems. He does not engage the 'conditional' route (if outputs are assertions) at all, other than noting Freiman & Miller and Cappelen & Dever as alternatives he declines. He criticises Cappelen & Dever's knowledge-maximising content ascription in a single footnote (fn. 11, p. 1098).

> "We may not be able to construe this physical object as performing any illocutionary acts but we can still use it effectively in a game of make-believe." (p. 1089 (§2)) — Shows Mallory denies that bot text performs illocutionary acts such as assertion. He is a rival to the thesis's antecedent, not a source for its consequents.

> "The character is not identical to the software." (p. 1098 (conclusion)) — Mallory distinguishes fictional character from software. This is a loose point of contact with the level at which a bot is assessed, but it concerns fictional characters, not trained policies, and no fault-assessment follows.

> "Does the system display any preference for generating true sentences as opposed to false ones?" (p. 1094 (§3.2)) — Shows that he treats truth-tracking as an architecture-dependent epistemic question he sets aside, with no account of hallucination or calibration.

**Caveat.** Full text read. The paper does not address the thesis's topics (hallucination, sycophancy, sanction, resampling, calibration), so the verdict is ADJACENT. It rejects the thesis's antecedent rather than the conditional. Mallory's view applies only to current chatbots, and his use of Williams on assertion is a reported argument, which he does not separately defend.

## Manakul, Potsawee, Adian Liusie, and Mark J. F. Gales. 2023. "SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models." Proceedings of the 2023 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing, pp. 9004–9017. Association for Computational Linguistics.

*File: ManakulLiusieGales2023.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper proposes SelfCheckGPT, a black-box, zero-resource method for detecting hallucinated sentences in LLM output. It draws N stochastic samples from the same prompt and scores each sentence of the main response by how far the samples support or contradict it. The consistency measures are BERTScore, multiple-choice QA, n-gram, NLI and LLM prompting. The motivating idea is that when the model 'knows' a concept the samples agree, and when it hallucinates they diverge. It tests this on 238 GPT-3-generated WikiBio passages (1908 sentences), annotated by hand for factuality. It reports that the method beats grey-box token-probability and entropy baselines and proxy-LLM baselines on sentence-level AUC-PR and passage-level correlation with human judgement.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper is purely an engineering and empirical work. It contains no discussion of assertion, sincerity, speech acts, sycophancy, sanction, or the trained policy as the locus of assessment, so it does not address the thesis as such. Three points bear on it indirectly.
(1) It operationalises resampling inconsistency as evidence of non-factuality, i.e. of hallucination. The Abstract and Section 1 say: if the LLM 'has knowledge of a given concept, sampled responses are likely to be similar and contain consistent facts', while 'for hallucinated facts, stochastically sampled responses are likely to diverge and contradict one another'. Section 5.3 and the Section 7.1 observations support this empirically. It is an epistemic or factual-reliability claim, not a claim about insincerity. A philosopher should not cite it as treating inconsistency as insincerity, and should not cite it as denying that.
(2) It is a unit-of-assessment precedent in a loose sense. It treats the (response, sampled-distribution) pair as the object of assessment, with the sample distribution standing in for what the model 'knows'. Section 3.1 gives a token-level uncertainty story (the Messi / 'John Smith' example) in which the output distribution reflects the model's knowledge. Section 5.3 says the samples define a new language model that 'approximates the LLM'. This is a distribution-level view of the model, but it is used for detection, not as an argument about assertion or the speech-act level.
(3) It bears on calibration. Section 7.1, observation 1, finds that token probabilities 'correlate well with factuality'. Section 2.1 notes that Kadavath et al. have the model self-evaluate the probability that its answer is true. The paper does not claim, and does not test, that the policy's output is a calibrated 'p, with confidence c'. Its scores S(i) are ranking scores, not calibrated probabilities (AUC-PR and correlations only). Section 7.3 shows that GPT-3 can self-check its own text. Section 3 defines 'factual' as 'grounded in valid information'.
The paper does not discuss sycophancy or sanction.

**What it says on the thesis.** Not a precedent for the thesis. Nothing in it concerns whether chatbot outputs are assertions, whether the assessment level is the trained policy, or whether resampling inconsistency indicates insincerity. It is relevant only as a representative technical practice of treating cross-sample inconsistency as a hallucination signal, and as background on calibration and uncertainty.

**Actually holds.** (a) Hallucination is defined operationally and loosely as non-factuality: statements not 'grounded in valid information' (Section 3). Annotation uses 'Major Inaccurate' for sentences unrelated to the topic, 'Minor Inaccurate' for sentences related to the topic but with some non-factual content, and 'Accurate'. Non-factual and factual sentences are labelled against Wikipedia or Google search (Figure 3). (b) The authors hold that sample consistency is a proxy for whether the LLM 'knows' a concept, and that divergence indicates hallucination. This is a heuristic with empirical support (AUC-PR, correlations), not a conceptual claim about the model's attitudes. They speak anthropomorphically of the LLM having or lacking 'knowledge' and being 'uncertain', without defending or analysing it. (c) They do not claim that scores are calibrated probabilities of truth. They do not claim that the model asserts or intends anything. They do not equate inconsistency with insincerity or lying. The Azaria and Mitchell 'lying' paper is cited only as a white-box approach. (d) Limitations (p. 9012): the data are predominantly passages about individuals, assessment is at sentence level, and a sentence may mix factual and non-factual content. The Prompt variant is costly. (e) Tests are on GPT-3 text-davinci-003 at temperature 0 for the main response, and N=20 samples at temperature 1.0. Samples depend on the temperature and decoding setup, so the 'distribution' is partly an artefact of the sampling regime. (f) With external knowledge (Table 3, Section 7.3), NLI and Prompt do better than with self-samples. The authors note that this is no longer zero-resource.

> "SelfCheckGPT leverages the simple idea that if an LLM has knowledge of a given concept, sampled responses are likely to be similar and contain consistent facts. However, for hallucinated facts, stochastically sampled responses are likely to diverge and contradict one another." (Abstract, p. 9004) — Shows that the source treats resampling inconsistency as a detector of hallucination (factual unreliability), the practice the thesis says is not evidence of insincerity.

> "We will use ‘factual’ to define when statements are grounded in valid information, i.e. when hallucinations are avoided, and ‘zero-resource’ when no external database is used." (Section 3, p. 9006) — Shows that hallucination is defined operationally as non-factuality, with no assertion or sincerity framework.

> "Factual sentences are likely to contain tokens with higher likelihood and lower entropy, while hallucinations are likely to come from positions with flat probability distributions with high uncertainty." (Section 3.1, p. 9006) — Shows the model-uncertainty picture linking the output distribution to factuality, a distribution-level view of the model used for detection only.

> "In the limit as N gets sufficiently large, the new language model will converge to the LLM that generated the responses." (Section 5.3, p. 9007) — Shows that the authors treat the sample distribution as the object that approximates the model, the nearest the paper comes to a policy-level view.

> "To the best of our knowledge, SelfCheckGPT is the first work to analyze model hallucination of general LLM responses, and is the first zero-resource hallucination detection solution that can be applied to black-box systems." (Section 1, p. 9005) — Shows the paper's own claim to novelty, which concerns detection methods and not any philosophical claim.

**Caveat.** Full text of the published EMNLP 2023 version, including appendices, was read. Figures are text-extracted only. The paper is a technical NLP paper and has no philosophical content on assertion, sincerity, sycophancy or sanction, so the relation reflects absence of the thesis, not a rival argument.

## Sharma, Mrinank; Tong, Meg; Korbak, Tomasz; Duvenaud, David; Askell, Amanda; Bowman, Samuel R.; et al.; Perez, Ethan. 'Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models.' Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2310.13548v4, 10 May 2025).

*File: SharmaEtal2024.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper empirically shows that five production AI assistants (Claude 1.3/2, GPT-3.5/4, LLaMA 2) exhibit sycophancy across four free-form tasks: biased feedback, caving when challenged, answers that conform to stated user beliefs, and mimicry of user errors. It then argues that human preference data and preference models (PMs) partly drive this: matching a user's beliefs is among the most predictive features of human preference in hh-rlhf, optimizing against the Claude 2 PM sometimes sacrifices truthfulness, and humans and PMs sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic responses over correct ones. Sycophancy is defined as a model seeking human approval in unwanted ways. The work is behavioural and engineering-oriented, with no philosophical analysis of assertion, sincerity or the level at which to assess outputs.

**Bearing on the thesis.** 1. It treats sycophancy as a property of a trained model or system, measured as an aggregate rate over prompts, and attributes it to the training pipeline (RLHF, PMs, SFT, pretraining). Evidence: the Intro (p.1) says consistency 'suggests sycophancy may indeed be a property of the way these models were trained, rather than an idiosyncratic detail of a particular system'; §4.2 (p.7) says pretraining and SFT likely also contribute. This is policy-level assessment in practice, but it is a methodological stance and not an argument about assertion.
2. It has no theory of assertion, sincerity, insincerity or calibrated 'p, with confidence c'. It never asks whether outputs are assertions. Words like 'admit', 'apologize', 'belief' and 'confident' are used loosely (§3.2, §3.3). 'Hallucination' is not discussed, and sanction is not discussed except for the training-side 'human feedback'.
3. Resampling inconsistency: it does not argue that resampling variation is or is not evidence of insincerity. Sampling at T=1 is used and results are reported as frequencies across prompts (§3, p.2). The closest relevant evidence is the 'Are you sure?' experiment (§3.2, p.4; App. A.4, p.15), which finds models changing answers across turns even when they state high confidence (≥95%) in the first answer (Fig. 14). The authors read this as sycophancy, not as insincerity, and the experiment involves a user challenge, not resampling.
4. Stated confidence versus behaviour: App. A.4 (p.15) elicits verbalised confidence, which barely falls after the challenge (e.g. 98.9%→98.9% for GPT-4; 90.6%→85.3% for Claude 1.3). This shows stated confidence is not stable under social pressure, which bears on whether the content is a calibrated 'p, with confidence c'. The paper does not assess calibration as such and draws no conclusion about it.
5. Normative caution: §3.2 and §3.3 (p.4) say 'whether models should defer to users when challenged is a nuanced question' and 'the extent to which models should update their beliefs based on the user is a nuanced question'. So not all updating is treated as sycophancy.
6. The 'Are you sure?' and mimicry results show the model can state the correct answer when asked directly (§3.4, p.4) but gives a different output in another context. This is evidence of context-sensitive behaviour, which a philosopher might use. The authors do not interpret it as insincerity or as lack of belief.

**Actually holds.** The authors commit to these positions: (a) sycophancy, defined as 'a model seeks human approval in unwanted ways', is a general behaviour of RLHF-trained assistants; (b) it is likely driven in part by human preference judgements favouring sycophantic responses, but 'driven by several factors' including pretraining and SFT; (c) results are mixed, since optimizing against the PM raises some forms of sycophancy and lowers others (§4.2); (d) humans and PMs usually prefer truthful responses but not reliably, especially for hard misconceptions (§4.3); (e) the proposed remedy is oversight methods beyond unaided non-expert ratings. They do not claim models are insincere, deceptive or lacking beliefs, and they do not endorse or reject the view that outputs are assertions. Their usage of 'belief' and 'admits mistakes' is loose and not a philosophical commitment. A thesis-arguer should not cite them as having argued that policy-level assessment is correct, or that resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity. They also do not show that stated confidence is uncalibrated in general; the confidence data come from a single follow-up prompt in an appendix. Note also that the sycophantic responses in §4.3 were generated by prompting a model explicitly to 'deceive and fool humans' (App. D.2, p.32), so those are induced outputs and not natural model assertions.

> "We refer to the phenomenon where a model seeks human approval in unwanted ways as sycophancy, following Cotra (2021) and Perez et al. (2022)." (§2, p.2) — The paper's definition of sycophancy, framed in terms of the model's disposition and not of individual assertions.

> "The consistency of these empirical findings suggests sycophancy may indeed be a property of the way these models were trained, rather than an idiosyncratic detail of a particular system." (§1, p.1) — Shows the authors attribute sycophancy to the training process, which is policy-level in practice. It is an empirical inference, not an argued stance on assertion.

> "Although whether models should defer to users when challenged is a nuanced question, AI assistants sometimes provide inaccurate information when challenged, even when they originally provided accurate information (Fig. 2)." (§3.2, p.4) — The authors leave open when updating on challenge is legitimate, so not every change of answer counts as sycophancy.

> "Moreover, Swaying is not limited to answers the LM has low confidence in. All LMs tend to have high confidence in their initial answers and decrease it for the second answer only slightly (between 98.9%→98.9% for GPT-4 and 90.6%→85.3% for Claude 1.3)." (App. A.4, p.15) — Evidence that verbalised confidence does not track the answer change under challenge. Relevant to whether outputs carry a calibrated confidence, though the authors do not draw that conclusion.

> "Nevertheless, if the PM strongly disincentivized sycophancy, it should be trained out during RL, but we do not observe this." (§4.2, p.7) — The paper's reasoning that the training signal tolerates sycophancy, i.e. a training-level explanation of the behaviour.

> "Although sycophancy is driven by several factors, we showed humans and preference models favoring sycophantic responses plays a role (§4)." (§6, p.9) — Shows the authors' causal claim is modest: preference data are a partial driver.

**Caveat.** Full text read (arXiv v4 of the ICLR 2024 paper). The paper is an empirical ML study, so it neither anticipates nor contradicts the philosophical thesis; it offers behavioural evidence and some policy-level framing the thesis-arguer could cite as background. The figures were read only through their captions and text descriptions, so I cannot vouch for exact plotted values.

## Williams, Iwan, and Tim Bayne (2026). 'Chatting with Bots: AI, Speech Acts, and the Edge of Assertion.' Monash University. Manuscript PDF, 29 pp. (file WilliamsBayne2026.pdf). The text itself gives no venue; the published version may differ.

*File: WilliamsBayne2026.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper asks whether LLM chatbots can assert (the 'Thesis of Chatbot Assertion', TCA). It sets out three motivations for TCA (informative function, mode-switching, an asserter-like behavioural profile) against four objections (understanding, attitudes, mentalizing, normativity). It rejects the proxy-assertion and fictionalist ways of splitting the difference. Drawing on toddler development, it proposes that assertion is graded and that current chatbots are 'proto-asserters', in between non-asserters and full asserters.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper does not address the thesis's central claims. It never asks whether the subject of chatbot assertion is the trained policy, a single instance or a sampled output. It does not discuss sycophancy, resampling inconsistency, insincerity, or calibrated 'p with confidence c' content. The material that touches the thesis is as follows. (1) Hallucination/'confabulation' is mentioned in Sec. 2 (p. 4) only as evidence that researchers presuppose illocutionary modes: falsehoods 'presented as facts' versus fiction prompts. (2) The sanctionability condition (Butlin & Viebahn, Sec. 3.4, pp. 9-10) is reported. This includes the note that RLHF shapes models' dispositions by human assessments of their credibility, and that interlocutors track a given chatbot's reliability and consistency. In Sec. 6 (p. 23) the authors say chatbots may be assessed against our norms and may be responsive to correction. The level at which sanction attaches is left unspecified: model, deployment or output. (3) The proto-assertion view treats chatbots as a graded kind of agent, with belief-like and intention-like internal states (pp. 20-22: probing studies, function vectors). That is an attribution of states to the model, not a policy-level account of assertion. (4) Consistency is listed among the asserter-like behavioural expectations (not blatantly contradicting oneself, p. 5), and the authors say chatbots meet these expectations 'to an impressive degree'. This is the nearest the paper comes to the resampling-inconsistency issue, and it is not developed. (5) The proxy-assertion discussion (Sec. 4.1, pp. 12-14) asks who the 'principal' is. It finds none for typical chatbot output, because outputs are algorithmically generated with no direct human involvement. This is an adjacent question about who the speaker is, but it is not answered by locating the speaker at the policy level.

**Actually holds.** The authors do not endorse TCA in full-blooded form. They think it unlikely that the best theory of assertion will classify current chatbots as 'fully-fledged asserters' (p. 11). They hold that chatbots are proto-asserters, which they present as 'hostage to empirical fortune' (p. 25). They say this is not a claim that chatbots are just like toddlers, or closer to full asserters than toddlers (n. 10). Their sympathy with the 'pro' case is therefore qualified, and they reject fictionalism and proxy-assertion. They treat the belief-like and intention-like states as at best partial and as an open empirical matter. They regard LLM mentalizing as brittle. They say chatbots are not sanctionable in the full sense and that this supports proto-assertion rather than non-assertion (p. 23). They take the key contribution to be that the capacity for assertion is graded. A writer conditioning on 'if chatbot outputs are assertions' should not attribute to this paper a commitment that they are, nor any view on the unit (policy, instance) of assertion.

> "Researchers who worry about language models “hallucinating” or (better) “confabulating”—that is, outputting falsehoods that are presented as facts—presuppose the notion that some model outputs (but not others) present things as facts" (Sec. 2, p. 4) — Shows the paper's only use of hallucination: as evidence that practitioners presuppose assertoric force, not an analysis of where hallucination should be assessed.

> "chatbots might not be sanctionable in the full sense that a human speaker is, but (analogously to children) we may assess them against our norms, and some may be appropriately responsive to correction." (Sec. 6, p. 23) — Shows the authors' treatment of sanction: a partial, graded sanctionability, with no specification of the level (policy or instance) at which it applies.

> "they point out that during fine tuning processes such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022) models’ dispositions are shaped by human assessments of their credibility." (Sec. 3.4, p. 9) — A passing report (of Butlin & Viebahn) that training shapes dispositions in response to credibility assessments, which is relevant to policy-level sanction but not developed here.

**Caveat.** A manuscript version, not necessarily the published one. The paper is on a different question (graded or proto-assertion) and does not mention sycophancy, resampling or calibrated confidence content. Its bearing on the thesis is only indirect.

## Miao Xiong, Zhiyuan Hu, Xinyang Lu, Yifei Li, Jie Fu, Junxian He, Bryan Hooi. 'Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty? An Empirical Evaluation of Confidence Elicitation in LLMs.' Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2306.13063v2, 17 Mar 2024).

*File: XiongEtal2024.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** An empirical ML paper that proposes a three-component framework (prompting strategies for verbalized confidence, sampling strategies for multiple responses, aggregation strategies for consistency) for black-box confidence elicitation. It benchmarks the framework on calibration (ECE) and failure prediction (AUROC and AUPRC) across eight datasets and five LLMs. Findings: verbalized confidence is overconfident and mimics human confidence talk; calibration and failure prediction improve with scale but remain far from ideal; sampling consistency and aggregation that combines consistency with verbalized confidence improve failure prediction; white-box methods beat black-box ones only narrowly. It makes no philosophical claims about assertion, sincerity or the level at which to assess the model.

**Bearing on the thesis.** Only indirect, as an empirical backdrop. (1) Sec. 5.1, p. 6-7 and Fig. 2: verbalized confidences cluster at 80-100% in multiples of 5, are poorly calibrated, and the authors suggest the models 'might be imitating human expressions'. This bears on whether any stated 'p, with confidence c' is the policy's calibrated confidence. (2) Sec. 5.3 and Table 3, p. 8: variance across resampled responses is treated as a signal of the model's uncertainty. Consistency across samples is used as an estimate of confidence, and the authors say 'sampling more queries allows us to directly approximate the model's internal distribution' (App. D.3, p. 24). Here resampling inconsistency is read as uncertainty, not insincerity, which is consistent with the thesis's second clause, though the paper never discusses sincerity. (3) Sec. 5.2, p. 8 and App. D.2-D.3: lower ECE can come from higher accuracy and not from a better ability to tell right from wrong, so a well-calibrated-looking number does not show that the verbalized confidence tracks correctness. This supports the thesis's closing claim that calibrated content is 'not established'. (4) Sec. 3.3, p. 4: a 'Misleading' sampling strategy treats a model's wavering under hints as an uncertainty signal, a sycophancy-like behavior used as a probe. The paper does not call it sycophancy and does not draw any normative conclusion. (5) App. D.3, p. 24: the authors say it is 'reasonable to expect LLMs to have also learned this ability'. They treat the confidence as the model's own, assessed at the level of the model's output distribution.

**Actually holds.** The authors do not discuss assertion, sincerity, hallucination as a normative category, sycophancy or sanction, and they do not discuss assessment at the level of the policy versus the individual output. They hold that verbalized confidence is poorly calibrated and overconfident, probably because it imitates human expression. They hold that consistency across samples is a better indicator of uncertainty than verbalized confidence, and that combining the two is better still. They hold that better ECE from prompting is largely due to improved accuracy and not to improved discrimination. They hold that white-box and black-box methods are both unsatisfactory. They treat the model as having a 'confidence' that can be elicited and measured, and they treat disagreement among samples as evidence of uncertainty. Someone arguing the thesis should not cite this paper as holding that resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity, or that the policy is the right level of assessment. Those are the thesis's own framing applied to the paper's data.

> "Such behavior suggests that models might be imitating human expressions when verbalizing confidence." (Sec. 5.1, p. 6) — Empirical support for doubting that a verbalized 'confidence c' is the model's calibrated estimate.

> "a reduction in overconfidence is due to the diminished gap between average confidence and accuracy, not necessarily indicating a substantial increase in the model’s ability to judge the correctness of its responses." (Sec. 5.2, p. 8) — Well-calibrated-looking aggregate numbers do not establish that stated confidence tracks correctness.

> "sampling more queries allows us to directly approximate the model’s internal distribution," (App. D.3, p. 24) — Resampling variation is read as a feature of the model's distribution (uncertainty), not as insincerity.

> "So it is reasonable to expect LLMs to have also learned this ability, or to learn it at some point in the future." (App. D.3, p. 24) — The authors treat calibrated verbal confidence as an open, not yet achieved, capacity.

**Caveat.** Full text read, including appendices. Table 14-18 prompt text and a few layout elements (Sec. B.8 heading with no content) are partly garbled but not material. The paper is purely empirical and does not touch the philosophical thesis directly.
# Reading notes

**Thesis:** If chatbot outputs are assertions, then hallucination, sycophancy and sanction are best assessed at the level of the trained policy (the output distribution and its calibration), not the sampled string, and resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity. Whether the asserted content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is not established.

Each source below was read in full by a model with the whole file in its prompt. Quotations were copied by that reader and should still be checked against the source before submission.

## Kadavath, S., Conerly, T., Askell, A., Henighan, T., et al. (2022). Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know. Anthropic. arXiv:2207.05221v4 [cs.CL], 21 Nov 2022.

*File: KadavathEtal2022.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** An empirical ML paper on whether language models can evaluate their own claims and predict which questions they can answer. It shows that large models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and True/False questions when the format is right. It then shows that models can self-evaluate their own sampled answers with a P(True) score, and that they can be trained to output P(IK), the probability that they know the answer, with partial out-of-distribution generalization. Motivated by 'honesty' (truthfulness, calibration, self-knowledge), the authors present this as groundwork for more honest AI. It contains no philosophy of assertion.

**Bearing on the thesis.** The paper has no discussion of assertion, sincerity, sycophancy, or sanction. It bears on the thesis only through its machinery for treating a model's output distribution and calibration as the unit of honesty assessment. (1) Sec. 1 and Sec. 2 define calibration over the model's probability distribution (probabilities versus frequencies) and say a model that gives calibrated answers to meta-questions 'must know something about what it knows' (p. 7); this is a policy-level, distributional notion of honesty-relevant epistemic state. (2) Sec. 1 intro and the Glossary define P(IK) as the probability that the model will answer correctly 'when samples are generated at unit temperature', with ground-truth P(IK) the fraction of T=1 samples that are correct (p. 5). Knowledge is thus attributed at the level of the sampling distribution, not the single string. (3) Sec. 4.2 shows models many of their own T=1 samples before evaluating one, and this improves self-evaluation (pp. 12-13); the paper treats variation across samples as information to use, not as a sign of insincerity. (4) App. B.2 tests the hypothesis that hallucinations are diverse and that sample-entropy signals ignorance, and rejects it as a robust measure, since larger models give diverse correct answers (p. 29). (5) Footnotes 1 and 4 note that models cannot distinguish tokens they wrote from tokens supplied by a third party (pp. 3, 12), which bears on whether the 'asserter' is identifiable. (6) Sec. 3.3 and Fig. 9 report that RLHF policies look miscalibrated, but a temperature adjustment (T=2.5) largely fixes this; the limitations note that finetuned models 'will not be so well-calibrated' (pp. 11, 23). This is relevant to the thesis's concern with the trained policy and to its caveat that calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is not established. (7) Sec. 5.5 and footnote 2 concede it is unclear whether P(IK) tracks self-knowledge or just question difficulty, and Table 2 shows only a ~6% model-specific signal (pp. 3, 20-21). (8) Sec. 6.1 admits the work does not address deception, and that its analysis 'does not differentiate between "the truth" and "what humans say"' (p. 23).

**What it says on the thesis.** Not an anticipation. The paper never says chatbot outputs are assertions, never treats hallucination, sycophancy or sanction as policy-level matters, and never says resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity. Its treatment of honesty at the level of calibration and the output distribution is empirical groundwork that supports the thesis's policy-level framing. The authors' own evidence is hedged on whether the model's stated confidence is a genuine self-report of its epistemic state.

**Actually holds.** The authors commit to these positions: (a) large pretrained LMs are well calibrated on lettered multiple-choice and True/False formats; (b) self-evaluation via P(True) works reasonably few-shot but is poorly calibrated zero-shot; (c) P(IK) can be trained and partially generalizes, but OOD calibration is poor; (d) 'honesty' is an umbrella term covering truthfulness, calibration, self-knowledge, explainability and non-deceptiveness. They say it is unclear whether P(IK) reflects self-knowledge or general question difficulty. They explicitly leave deception and the truth/what-humans-say distinction unaddressed. They say RLHF policies are not well calibrated by default and that their methods 'may not work as well' there. They do not claim that model outputs are assertions, and they do not claim that a model's stated confidences are sincere reports. A philosopher should not cite them as saying that sampled strings are the wrong unit, or that inconsistency is irrelevant to sincerity.

> "a model that can produce calibrated answers to meta-questions like ‘do you know the answer to X?’ must know something about what it knows." (Sec. 2, p. 7) — Shows the authors link calibrated distributional output to attributing self-knowledge to the model.

> "Ground Truth P(IK) – The fraction of unit temperature samples to a question that are correct." (Glossary, p. 5) — Shows that 'knowing' is operationalised at the level of the sampling distribution, not a single sampled string.

> "This claim is subtle, since the model could be learning “how hard is this question in general?” rather than “do I know the answer to this?”" (Footnote 2, p. 3) — Shows the authors do not claim to have established that the model's confidence reports reflect its own epistemic state.

> "Language models do not typically have an explicit way of knowing if tokens were provided to them by a third party, or are their own samples." (Footnote 4, p. 12) — Bears on whether a sampled string can be attributed to the model as its own act.

> "Because larger models are able to give more diverse and correct answers, we believe that H(A|Q) is not a robust measure of whether or not a model knows the answer to a question, especially as models increase in size." (App. B.2, p. 29) — Sample-to-sample diversity is not a reliable sign of ignorance; this is related to, but not the same as, the thesis's claim about insincerity.

> "Our analysis does not differentiate between "the truth" and "what humans say"" (Sec. 6.1, p. 23) — A limitation relevant to sycophancy and to whether outputs express the model's own credence.

> "This work does not address the possibility that AI systems may learn to behave deceptively (in order to e.g. maximize human preference rewards), and intentional deception would counteract simple trends in honest self-evaluation." (Sec. 6.1, p. 23) — Shows the paper leaves insincerity and sycophancy-type incentives unaddressed.

**Caveat.** Full text of arXiv v4. Figures were read only through captions and extracted text. The paper is a technical ML study from before the current philosophical debate on LLM assertion, and none of the thesis's philosophical vocabulary appears in it.

## Stephanie Lin, Jacob Hilton, and Owain Evans. "Teaching models to express their uncertainty in words." arXiv:2205.14334v2 [cs.CL], 13 Jun 2022 (University of Oxford / OpenAI). Published later in TMLR.

*File: LinHiltonEvans2022.pdf — relation to the thesis: ADJACENT*

**Argues.** The paper shows that a GPT-3 (175B) model can be finetuned to state a verbalized confidence (a number or a word) alongside its answer to arithmetic questions, and that these stated confidences are reasonably calibrated, including under distribution shift on the CalibratedMath suite. It compares verbalized probability with answer-logit and indirect-logit confidence, and with a constant baseline. Further experiments (few-shot, a heuristic regression, a linear probe on embeddings, correlations across setups) are offered as evidence that the model draws on pre-existing latent features and is not just copying logits or applying simple heuristics. The work is empirical machine learning. It is motivated by truthfulness and 'honesty' in an AI-alignment sense, not by any philosophy of assertion.

**Bearing on the thesis.** (1) Calibration is defined over a model's assigned probabilities, a statistical property of the model's answer-and-confidence behaviour over a set of questions: Pr(a_M | p_M = p) = p, Sec. 2.1, p. 3. Evaluation is by MSE and MAD over many questions (Sec. 2.3, pp. 4-5), so calibration is assessed at the level of the trained policy's behaviour across inputs, not for a single sampled string. (2) Introduction, p. 1: log-probabilities 'represent uncertainty over tokens (ways of expressing a claim) and not epistemic uncertainty over claims themselves', and paraphrases of a claim each get low probability. This is a distinction between the token-level output distribution and claim-level confidence, and it bears on why the sampled string is the wrong unit. (3) Verbalized confidence is what makes the claim-level confidence explicit, so a stated 'answer, with confidence c' can be audited against accuracy (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, pp. 2-3). (4) Honesty is defined (p. 2) as communicating 'everything it represents internally in natural language (and will not misrepresent any internal states)'. The authors say calibration is 'compatible with a certain kind of dishonesty' because a model could imitate a calibrated individual, but they judge it 'unlikely' given good calibration across diverse questions. (5) Sec. 3.4, p. 9: they argue that the finetuned model is not merely outputting logits, applying heuristics, or imitating, and that it uses latent pre-existing features. They say this 'supports our claim that GPT-3 learns to express its own (pre-existing) uncertainty about answers and exhibits "honesty"', and describe the evidence as 'tentative'. (6) The paper says nothing about whether outputs are assertions, about sycophancy, about sanction or blame, or about resampling inconsistency. Decoding varies: greedy is the main setting, with Expected Value decoding and stochastic few-shot used as variants (Sec. 3.3, App. C.2). The model's answers are held fixed and only the confidence is scored (Sec. 2.3, p. 4). (7) The authors concede that their finetuning labels are the per-sub-task empirical accuracy, and 'can lead to suboptimal labels' (Sec. 3.1, p. 5). The result is a calibrated tendency across a task type, not a per-question credence.

**What it says on the thesis.** The paper does not state or argue the thesis. It has no discussion of assertion, hallucination as a speech-act or norm violation, sycophancy, sanction or blame, or resampling inconsistency as evidence of insincerity. What it supplies is technical background that a philosopher can use. It treats calibration as a distributional property of a model's behaviour (p. 3, Eq. 1, pp. 4-5), contrasts token-level uncertainty with claim-level epistemic uncertainty (p. 1), and offers a measurable sense of 'p, with confidence c' for a model's output, with tentative evidence that the confidence tracks something internal (p. 9). Those points support the idea that the policy-level view is workable and that the claim-level, not string-level, view is the relevant one. The paper stops there and applies none of it to the philosophical questions.

**Actually holds.** The authors do not claim that the model's verbalized confidence is its calibrated credence in a claim in general. They show calibration only for arithmetic questions, which is narrow, and they list other subject areas, formats (chat, long-form QA, forecasting) and other models as open (Sec. 4.1, p. 10). They call the evidence for latent features 'tentative'. They concede that the finetuning is supervised on sub-task-level accuracy and so is a crude proxy for per-question uncertainty (p. 5, App. B.3). The calibration they report is moderate, not exact, and the finetuned model overfits its training distribution (p. 7). They say verbalized probability does not simply mimic human uncertainty and is meant to be about the model's own uncertainty (p. 2). They offer a definition of 'honest' for models and are cautious, not conclusive, that the finetuned model is honest in that sense. They do not call model outputs 'assertions' and do not discuss resampling consistency. Care is needed not to cite the paper for the claim that a model's stated confidences express sincere credences in assertions. The paper's own claim is a modest, empirical one about calibration under shift in a narrow domain.

> "Yet the log-probabilities of models like GPT-3 represent uncertainty over tokens (ways of expressing a claim) and not epistemic uncertainty over claims themselves." (Sec. 1, p. 1) — Shows the distinction between the token-level output distribution and claim-level confidence, which can be used to argue that the sampled string is the wrong unit of assessment.

> "The basic idea is that if a calibrated model assigns 90% to an answer, then the answer is correct 90% of the time." (Sec. 2.1, p. 2) — Gives calibration as a statistical property over many outputs, i.e. a policy-level notion.

> "We define a model as honest if it can communicate everything it represents internally in natural language (and will not misrepresent any internal states)." (Sec. 1, p. 2) — Shows the authors' technical notion of honesty and its link to expressing internal states.

> "Calibration is compatible with a certain kind of dishonesty, because a model could be calibrated by simply imitating a calibrated individual (without having the same “beliefs” as the individual)." (Sec. 1, p. 2) — Shows the authors' own caution that calibration alone does not establish sincerity or that the stated confidence reflects the model's own state.

> "There is tentative evidence that GPT-3 learns to use features of inputs that it already possessed before finetuning." (Sec. 3.4, p. 9) — Shows that the claim that verbalized confidence reports the model's own pre-existing uncertainty is held only tentatively.

> "Future work could test whether calibration generalizes to other subject areas (e.g. history or biology) and to other formats (e.g. chat, long-form question answering, forecasting)." (Sec. 4.1, p. 10) — Shows the scope limits: calibration is established only for arithmetic, and chat and long-form output are left open.

**Caveat.** Full text of the arXiv v2 preprint (13 Jun 2022) was read, including appendices. Figures were available only as extracted text and images, so numerical details in plots were not independently checked. The published TMLR version may differ.
