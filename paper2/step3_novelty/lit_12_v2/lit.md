### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Commitment-as-accountability: asserting makes the speaker liable to sanctions, blame or being called to account if the content is false. The thesis's test of 'sanctionability' draws on this tradition. — *Peirce; Alston (2000)*
  > an act of assertion […] renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law (or, at any rate, those of the moral law) in case [the asserted proposition] should not be true
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Commitment as a responsibility to defend, defer or retract when challenged. This supplies the 'disownability' condition, since one escapes the commitment only by withdrawing the assertion. — *Brandom (1994); MacFarlane (2003); Rescorla (2009a)*
  > One can escape this commitment only by withdrawing the assertion.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Mixed accounts that separate liability (being right or wrong on the issue) from fidelity (the duty to defend) and frankness (sincerity). A bearer-of-deviation condition could be read as one of these components. — *Green (1999–2020a); Searle; Peirce*
  > one who asserts that P is liable to be right or wrong on the issue of P’s truth exactly as P turns out to be true or false.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Non-normative cognitive or surface-feature accounts count an utterance as an assertion by default patterns on production and comprehension sides. These are rivals that need no commitment-bearer, so they could count machine output as assertion without relocating commitment to an operator. — *Pagin (2011, 2020); Jary (2010)*
  > it is the cognitive patterns associated with surface features, on the production and comprehension sides, that characterize assertion.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Pagin's argument that commitment is not sufficient for assertion, because a commitment can be incurred without asserting. This bears on whether relocated commitment to an operator would amount to the operator's asserting. — *Pagin (2004); contested by Pegan (2009), MacFarlane (2011), García-Carpintero (2013), Marsili & Green (2021)*
  > incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — The related idea that a speaker can act without an assertion being made in the full sense, for instance an actor representing himself, or irony and indirect assertion where the literal act is not performed. — *entry's discussion of self-representation and indirect assertion (Davidson, Black; Searle, Sadock)*
  > the audience not invited to believe that the speaker, that is, the actor, has that belief.

**Nearest positions.** Commitment as accountability and liability to sanctions — Peirce, Alston; Commitment as challenge-response and retraction — Brandom, MacFarlane, Rescorla; Mixed account of fidelity, liability and frankness — Green, Searle; Surface-feature and cognitive accounts of assertion that need no commitment-bearer — Pagin, Jary

**The entry is silent on.** Machine or LLM assertion, proxy or delegated assertion, assertion attributed to an operator or principal, 'imputed deviation' with a machine-side bearer, and a 'second act' being deflated. The entry treats speakers as ordinary human agents and never discusses a gap between an utterer and the one held accountable. The thesis's claim that proxy views are parasitic on attributing views therefore has no counterpart in the entry.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — ethics-ai

- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Current LLMs lack the features needed for a machine-side bearer of responsibility or commitment. They have no individual agent, generate no norms of their own, and cannot reflect on or change values, so they cannot be held responsible for them. — *Barandiaran and Almendros 2024; the entry itself*
  > There is no individual agent, the agent does not generate its own norms, and the agent is not shaped by its interaction
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Responsibility gap: neither the human nor the machine may be responsible. The entry lists responses: keep humans in or on the loop, or in 'meaningful control'; accept tragic choices; or drop the assumption that every event has someone responsible. It also notes sceptical accounts of the gap. — *Sparrow 2007; Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018; Danaher 2022; Tigard 2021, Königs 2022, Da Silva 2024*
  > neither the human nor the machine may be responsible
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Retribution gap: difficulty of allocating punishment when a machine acts. This resembles the thesis's idea that a deflated 'second act' lacks sanctionability. — *Danaher 2016a*
  > The difficulty of allocating punishment is sometimes called the “retribution gap”
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Distributed agency yields distributed responsibility. Responsibility lands on a human or institutional party because societal systems need a person to blame or credit. — *Taddeo and Floridi 2018; Nyholm 2018a, 2018b*
  > With distributed agency comes distributed responsibility.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — A thin notion of agency applies to current AI, including a merely reactive LLM, while the thick notion that grounds responsibility does not. Thin agency has no responsibility-bearing status. — *Floridi 2023a, 2023b; Moor 2006; Allen, Smit and Wallach 2006; Zafar 2024 criticises Floridi*
  > current AI systems, even a merely reactive LLM, come out as agents, but without intelligence
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — LLM outputs raise a 'testimony gap', and hallucinations are treated as a form of bullshit. These are epistemic and testimonial analogues of the question of who stands behind an output. — *Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Hicks, Humphries and Slater 2024*
  > create a “testimony gap”

**Nearest positions.** Responsibility-gap debate (gap vs. human-in-meaningful-control vs. sceptics) — Sparrow 2007; Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018; Tigard, Königs, Da Silva; Thin vs. thick agency, with LLMs as agents without moral agency — Floridi 2023a; Moor 2006; Barandiaran and Almendros 2024; Testimony gap for LLM outputs — Sparrow and Flenady 2025

**The entry is silent on.** The entry never discusses assertion, commitment, 'attributing' versus 'proxy' accounts of LLM speech acts, imputed deviation, a 'second act', or disownability. It does not argue that one account is parasitic on the other.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — collective-responsibility

- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Deflated or 'as if' bearers of responsibility. Thompson treats states and corporations as responsible because they are 'like' moral agents. The entry replies that likeness is not enough, since 'acting like a moral agent is not the same as being a moral agent.' This is structurally close to the thesis's claim that a deflated bearer lacks what is needed. It is about groups, not machines or proxies. — *Janna Thompson (2006); the entry's own critique*
  > “acting like a moral agent” is not the same thing as being a moral agent. (And if one really is a moral agent then there is no need to go to the lengths of specifying likeness.)
- **PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK** (2/5) — Representative or proxy subjects. Hobbes's Leviathan and Tuomela's representative subject make group agency a matter of members' agency transferred or represented. May requires that some individuals be 'authorized to represent their own actions as the actions of the group.' These are proxy-style views in which the group's agency is derivative of individuals'. The entry does not argue that they collapse into an attributing view. — *Hobbes (1651); Tuomela (1989); May (1987); Copp (1980)*
  > some individuals be authorized to represent their own actions as the actions of the group as a whole (May 1987, p. 55)
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Derivative-agency and individualist positions that locate the real bearer in individuals. Sosa holds that group minds are derivative constructs from members' minds. Narveson holds that only individuals can 'literally' bear full responsibility, so any collective sense is metaphorical. Both support the 'relocation to the operator' horn. — *David Sosa (2007); Jan Narveson (2002); Downie (1969)*
  > “Nothing else,” Narveson writes, “can literally be the bearer of full responsibility”
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Rival accounts that supply a non-individual bearer without a Kantian unified self. Shockley's 'being at fault' with 'coordinating control' and Crawford's 'morally bad organization' deserving collective punishment (apology, amends, change) give the collective its own sanctionable status. Silver and Bjornsson & Hess ground it in reasons-responsiveness and reactive attitudes. These contradict the idea that no non-attributing bearer of imputed deviation is available. — *Kenneth Shockley (2007, 2016); Neta Crawford (2007); Silver (2022); Bjornsson and Hess (2017); Smiley (2010)*
  > deserving of some kind of punishment apart from that meted out to its members for their role in harm

**Nearest positions.** Representational or authorization accounts of group agency, where members' agency is transferred or represented — Hobbes, Tuomela, May, Copp; Derivative or metaphorical group agency versus 'literal' individual bearers — Sosa, Narveson, Thompson ('like moral agents'); Non-Kantian collective blameworthiness grounded in sanctionability, coordinating control, or reasons-responsiveness — Shockley, Crawford, Silver, Bjornsson & Hess

**The entry is silent on.** The entry says nothing about machines, AI, operators, or proxy versus attributing views as such. It also does not discuss 'second acts', sanctionability or disownability as tests for a bearer, 'imputed deviation', or the claim that one family of views is parasitic on another.
