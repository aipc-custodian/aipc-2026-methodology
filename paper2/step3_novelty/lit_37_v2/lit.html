Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: assertion, ethics-ai, testimony-episprob — The theses dispute whether LLM output deployed under a reliability policy counts as assertion, so the assertion entry (commitment, knowledge norm) is central, with ethics of AI and the epistemology of testimony as the neighbouring debates on machine-generated content and the reliability-based norms that govern it.

For unreviewed, open-range LLM output, the deployer's commitment is to the policy's reliability, not to each content p. Proxy accounts owe an explanation of how a standing policy yields content-level commitment without uptake, and what governs the unseen output looks like a reliability norm rather than the knowledge norm.

What is left. No finding I could confirm meets the ANTICIPATED standard. The one CONTRADICTED finding (Butlin & Viebahn on organisational sanctionability for indirectly put-forward content) means the thesis must argue that sanctionability or liability for content differs from assertoric commitment to it. What remains is the claim that, for unreviewed open-range LLM output, the deployer's commitment is to the policy's reliability and not to each content; that existing proxy accounts do not say how a standing policy yields content-level commitment without uptake; and that the governing norm for unseen output looks like a reliability norm, not the knowledge norm. The thesis must be stated against Butlin & Viebahn's own conclusion that AI cannot assert even as a proxy, and must settle whether the Freiman and 2026 Phil & Tech items already cover its negative half.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Commitment accounts treat assertion as accountability or liability for the content's truth, and take it to include a duty to respond to challenges. This gives the thesis a framework in which commitment can be located at a policy or standing level rather than at the content level. — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green, Watson

a speaker accepts responsibility for a content p being true iff the speaker knowingly [takes] on the liability to ([lay] herself open to) blame

ADJACENT (2/5) — Austin's uptake requirement, which the entry says is less plausible for speaker-content verbs such as assert. This bears on the thesis's 'without uptake' demand, since the entry reports that assertion may not require hearer uptake. — Austin; the entry's own gloss

it is less plausible when the structure is speaker-content (“Bill asserted that p”)

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — The Pagin objection argues that commitment is not sufficient for asserting, which pressures any hearer-directed or commitment proxy account. A deployer's commitment to a policy would face the same sufficiency problem. — Pagin (2004); responses by Pegan, MacFarlane, García-Carpintero, Marsili & Green

incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Alternative norms to the knowledge norm: justification, evidence-responsiveness, and rational-belief norms. A reliability norm sits near these as a non-factive or process-level candidate. The entry also reports Rescorla's view that there is no norm on making assertions, only norms governing later reactions. — Lackey, Douven, Kvanvig, Maitra & Weatherson, Smithies; Rescorla (2009a)

Rescorla (2009a), who defends a commitment account of assertion (see section 6.1), argues there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Goldberg's norm-of-assertion hypothesis explains testimonial entitlement via a norm that must be common knowledge between speaker and hearer, plus the speaker's authorizing the hearer to defer challenges back to them. This is relevant to what a deployer-level policy would need to supply. — Goldberg (2015)

it must be common knowledge between speaker and hearer that the speaker’s assertion is subject to this norm.

Nearest positions. Commitment as accountability or liability for the content's truth — Peirce, Alston; Commitment as a responsibility to respond to challenges or retract — Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Knowledge norm versus justification norm for assertion — Williamson versus Lackey, Kneer, Reuter & Brössel; Pagin's insufficiency objection to commitment accounts

The entry is silent on. Non-human, automated or policy-generated utterances, delegated or proxy assertion, speaker-less output, and unreviewed generation. The entry names no reliability norm, no process-level norm, and no standing-policy commitment. It also does not discuss how commitment could attach to a policy rather than to individual contents.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — ethics-ai

ADJACENT (2/5) — Entry flags 'testimony gap' and hallucination-as-bullshit: LLM output lacks the testimonial/assertoric standing of human speech, bearing on whether content-level commitments attach to unseen output. — Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Hicks, Humphries, and Slater 2024

Others have argued that the “hallucinations” that plague LLMs are a form of this “bullshit” (Hicks, Humphries, and Slater 2024) or create a “testimony gap” (Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025).

ADJACENT (2/5) — Right to a 'well-calibrated machine decision' rather than a human justification: an institutional standard of policy-level reliability in place of case-by-case justification. — Huq 2020

some authors have argued that instead we have a right to right to a “well-calibrated machine decision” (Huq 2020) or that we need to avoid a “testimony gap”

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Responsibility for AI outputs is distributed; many societal systems need allocation to a person; responsibility-gap literature and the shift to risk distribution framing give a base for tying deployer accountability to a policy rather than each output. — Taddeo and Floridi 2018; Sparrow 2007; Simpson and Müller 2016; Milano and Nyholm 2024

the real issue may well be the distribution of risk (Simpson and Müller 2016).

ADJACENT (2/5) — Opacity discussion: the output is opaque even to programmers; epistemic conditions affect normative issues; the entry doubts traditional epistemology's fit for AI. — Durán and Jongsma 2021; Alvarado 2023

it remains to be seen whether notions and questions from traditional epistemology (about the conditions for true justified belief) are suitable for the many ways in which human perception and knowledge are impacted by AI technologies.

Nearest positions. Testimony gap / LLM hallucination as bullshit — Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Hicks, Humphries, Slater 2024; Right to a well-calibrated machine decision — Huq 2020; Responsibility gaps and distributed responsibility / risk distribution — Sparrow 2007; Taddeo and Floridi 2018; Simpson and Müller 2016

The entry is silent on. Speech-act theory of LLM output: assertion, commitment, uptake, proxy accounts of deployer assertion, and the knowledge norm vs a reliability norm of assertion are not discussed anywhere in the entry.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — testimony-episprob

ADJACENT (2/5) — Group testimony without member belief. The Population Commission 'testifies' that p although no member believed p or had justification for it. Non-Summativists say a group can testify that p even if none of its members do. This is the nearest case in the entry of content-level testimony with no individual's content-level uptake or commitment. — Tollefsen 2007; Lackey 2014 (non-summativism); Hardwig 1985

At the time the report was released, no single member of the UN Population committee believed p ... Third, and finally, the UN Commission did seem to testify that p

ADJACENT (2/5) — Deflationism about group testimony. A group's testimony reduces to some individual's testimony, whether a member or a mere spokesman. A proxy-style account of whose commitment attaches to the output has a counterpart here, though it is only mentioned. — Lackey 2014 (deflationary epistemology of group testimony); Tollefsen 2007 (inflationist); Lackey 2018 (inflationary account of group assertion)

Deflationists maintain to a group's testimony that p can be reduced to some individual's testimony that p (regardless of whether those individuals are members of the group, or just mere spokesmen)

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Testimonial Reliabilism. Testimonial justification consists in the reliability of the processes producing the belief, including the speaker's production processes. The thesis's 'reliability norm' can build on this, with the policy's reliability as the relevant process. — Graham 2000a, 2000b, 2006a; Goldberg 2010a; Sosa 2010

A hearer's testimonial justification consists in the reliability of the processes involved in the production of the hearer's testimonially-based belief.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Testimony without speaker knowledge or belief. Lackey's Creationist Teacher and Persistent Believer cases, and her hearer-testimony account, treat testimony as a source of knowledge regardless of what the speaker believes or intends. This loosens the link between the speaker's commitment to p and the content's epistemic standing. — Lackey 2008; Graham 2006b; Carter & Nickel 2014

testimony is a source of knowledge and justified belief regardless of what the speaker intended to say.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Rival knowledge-based views. Williamson and others are cited as supporting the Transmission View (TV-N). Testimony is tied to assertion, and the reader is sent to the Assertion entry. A defender of the knowledge norm would resist the claim that the governing norm is only reliability. — Williamson 1996, 2000; Audi; Burge; Owens (TV-N proponents); Fricker 1987, Sosa 1994 (testimony = assertion)

TV-N: For every speaker, A, and hearer, B, B knows that p on the basis of A's testimony only if A knows that p too.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Assurance and telling accounts. Testimony requires an intentional act of telling, with a speaker who intends to assure a hearer of p. A commitment-to-the-policy view must say why this uptake-style requirement is absent for unreviewed output. Hearer testimony (Lackey) is an alternative that does not require speaker intention. — Hinchman 2005, 2014; Moran 2005, 2018; Faulkner 2007, 2011; McMyler 2011; Zagzebski 2012; Lackey 2008

when a speaker tells their audience that p is true, she is doing much more than merely uttering p. Rather, she is inviting her audience to trust her

Nearest positions. Non-summative and inflationary group testimony, where testimony attaches to the content though no member believes it — Tollefsen 2007, Lackey 2014/2018; Deflationary and spokesperson accounts, which reduce group testimony to an individual's testimony — Lackey 2014; Testimonial Reliabilism, with justification a matter of process reliability, including the speaker's production processes — Graham, Goldberg 2010a, Sosa 2010; Knowledge-norm and Transmission views, in which testimony requires speaker knowledge — Williamson, Audi, Burge

The entry is silent on. LLMs and AI-generated text, deployers, standing policies as objects of commitment, proxy accounts of assertion, and whether the governing norm of unreviewed output is knowledge or reliability. The entry mentions the knowledge norm only by citation (Williamson 1996) and defers assertion to a separate entry. It does not discuss commitment to a policy versus commitment to each content, or uptake as a condition on content-level commitment. Its nearest discussion of mere 'spokesmen' is a one-line mention within group testimony.
