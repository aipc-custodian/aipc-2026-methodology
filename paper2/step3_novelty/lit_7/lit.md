**What is left.** Moffatt-type cases, where a deployer is held to a chatbot's statement though no designer or deployer had any objective regarding that content, put pressure on the sufficiency of Green & Michel's objectives condition. They also put pressure on Arora's producer-side responsibility condition. A role-and-uptake account of which party is answerable fits them, in the way the objective theory of contract and apparent authority already do. Whether the tribunal's finding of negligent misrepresentation shows that the statement had assertoric or promissory force, and not just that its deployer bears the risk, is not settled by the case. The functionalist and accountability literature has already put the general view that force is public-role and normative-effect based, so the thesis's contribution is limited to the Moffatt-based divergence argument against the objectives condition. Gubelmann and Skiba should probably be treated as a separate target, since their arguments turn on the LLM's lack of intention and on norms, not on a producer-side objective.

**Not found.** I found no source that uses Moffatt as a divergence test against the objectives condition for proxy speech acts, and none that argues the tribunal's reasoning shows illocution and not just liability. I found no direct engagement with Searle's status functions, Brandom's deontic scorekeeping, or Ludwig's work on authorised spokespersons beyond a passing citation of Ludwig. I did not retrieve Butlin & Viebahn's 'AI Assertion', Rosen & Dale's 'LLMs Don't Do Things with Words', or the full text of any 2026 paper listed above. The 2026 pieces may anticipate more than their abstracts show. The empirical search found only consumer-blame and attribution studies. I found no psychology, linguistics or experimental-pragmatics work on whether ordinary speakers treat chatbot outputs as promises or assertions (force), so that gap is untested, not clean. Most of the philosophy is behind paywalls and I read abstracts and excerpts, so the silence is weak evidence.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — Force conventionalism: force is fixed by a public, conventional procedure with a conventional effect, not by the speaker's psychology. — *Austin; Searle (weaker form); Reimer (illocutionary conventions); Millikan (natural conventions)*
  > There must exist an accepted conventional procedure having a certain conventional effect, that procedure to include the uttering of certain words by certain persons in certain circumstances…
- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — Uptake as a condition on performing a speech act: an act can misfire without audience uptake, and a systematic lack of uptake can silence a speaker. — *Austin; Langton and Hornsby; McGowan*
  > Other attempts at speech acts might misfire because their addressee fails to respond with an appropriate uptake: I cannot bet you $100 on who will win the election unless you accept that bet.
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — Intentionalism: force is fixed by manifest communicative intention (Strawson) and by overt commitment (Green), so producer psychology matters. The entry also argues that mere conventions are not required. — *Strawson 1964; Green*
  > What makes an utterance of a sentence in the indicative mood a prediction rather than a command, for instance, is that it manifests an intention to be so taken
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Uptake is not required for some speech acts, such as refusing, inviting and surrendering. — *Bird 2002; Maitra 2009*
  > Bird (2002) denies that the speech act of refusal requires uptake. Such an illocution is, he contends, like inviting and surrendering, which can occur whether or not their intended audiences grasp or accept the proffered illocutions.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Force is individuated by publicly accessible commitment and norms, such as the 'How do you know?' challenge. The entry stresses that commitments are not undertaken merely by intending them. Public norms of assertion and conjecture can ground divergence tests. — *Green; Williamson 1996; Brandom (bibliography)*
  > speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it, even when it is common knowledge that this is what I am trying to do.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Davidson's autonomy of linguistic meaning and the claim that no device can guarantee force. This would be a referee's challenge to any practice-fixed view of force. — *Davidson 1979; Hare 1989 replies*
  > there could be a society with a convention that utterance of a certain expression constituted performance of a certain illocutionary act, even those utterances that occur on stage or as used by jokers or storytellers
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Characteristic aims (perlocutions) as individuating speech act types. This is the nearest thing in the entry to an 'objectives-based' view. — *Austin; T. Cohen 1973; Searle and Vanderveken*
  > A type of speech act can have a characteristic aim without each speech act of that type being issued with that aim

**Nearest positions.** Force conventionalism — Austin, Searle (weaker form), Reimer, Millikan; Intentionalism / overt commitment — Strawson 1964, Green, Grice; Uptake-dependence of illocution and silencing — Austin, Langton/Hornsby, with Bird and Maitra dissenting

**The entry is silent on.** The entry does not discuss 'Moffatt' or any case of that kind. It has no 'divergence tests' against 'objectives-based proxy' views, and it does not discuss non-human or institutional producers of utterances (e.g. AI outputs) as candidate illocutions. It does not use the phrase 'status-conferring practice of adoption'.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — social-ontology

- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — Conferralism: a social status is held because of other people's attitudes or dispositions in a context, not because of the bearer's or the producer's own states. This is the closest existing version of 'status fixed by uptake'. — *Ásta 2008–2018*
  > A social property is “conferred” on an object, according to Ásta, if the object has the property because of some attitudes of people. A baseball umpire, for instance, confers the property of being a strike on certain pitches.
- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — Searle's status-function theory: status is imposed by collective acceptance or recognition, through Declarations. The status does not depend on the speaker's private psychology alone. — *Searle 1995, 2010*
  > What imposes these statuses is collective attitudes: our collective acceptance or recognition that they have those statuses.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Performatives and the linguistic construction of the social: utterances establish facts merely by being uttered, and speech acts can be constitutive of social identities. — *Austin 1961, 1962; Butler 1988; Winch 1958; Thomasson 2003*
  > Certain utterances are “performative”: merely in uttering them, we establish facts in the world.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Mentalistic and individualist accounts of how social categories are set up place the construction work in the participants' mental states. Bratman derives shared intention from individual attitudes. Searle's institutional facts depend on mental states 'not on practices'. — *Bratman 1993, 2014; Searle 1995, 2010; Hart 1961*
  > he does not require that rules be implemented in behavior: institutional facts depend only on mental states in a society, not on practices.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Heterogeneity of anchors: social categories are not set up by one uniform kind of practice, and practice theories lack the resources to accommodate that diversity. — *Epstein 2014a*
  > Even theories of practice, he argues, do not have the resources to accommodate this heterogeneity.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Practice theories are hard to read ontologically, and it is often unclear whether they make a constitutive claim or merely tell a causal narrative. That is exactly what a referee would press against 'fixed by a practice'. — *Bourdieu, Giddens, Schatzki; Haslanger*
  > it is hard to tease apart different kinds of ontological claims they make, and also hard even to know when these theories are making an ontological claim, as opposed merely to telling a narrative of causal influence.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Whether anchoring is synchronic or diachronic. Uptake that comes after an utterance raises the question of whether history can be constitutive. — *Epstein 2015 and Schaffer 2019 (for diachronic determination); Bernstein 2016 (against); Searle (synchronic)*
  > Diachronic ontological determination is a matter of controversy: in favor see Epstein 2015, Schaffer 2019; opposed see Bernstein 2016
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — Some debates are dismissed as merely verbal. A divergence test between rival accounts of force could be read as terminological. — *McGrath 2008; Chalmers 2011; Barnes 2014; Díaz-León 2022 (against the dismissal)*
  > merely because a debate is verbal does not exclude it from being metaphysically substantive.

**Nearest positions.** Searle's status functions, imposed by collective acceptance and declaration — Searle 1995, 2010; Conferralism, where status depends on others' attitudes or dispositions in context — Ásta; Performativity and speech-act constitution of the social — Austin, Butler, Thomasson; Practice theories as the basis for setting up categories (with the worry that they collapse into mentalism) — Bourdieu, Giddens, Schatzki

**The entry is silent on.** The entry does not discuss illocutionary force as such, Austinian uptake, 'objectives-based proxy' views, or anything called 'Moffatt', nor divergence tests of this kind. It does not discuss the force of legal or institutional texts. Section 5.7 (Law) and the rest of the entry are cut off in the provided text, so any treatment of force in law cannot be checked.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — contract-law

- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — The objective theory of contracts. Legal effect is fixed by the public, objective meaning of the parties' speech acts, not by what they privately intended. This is the nearest existing version of 'not producer psychology'. — *Hotchkiss v. National City Bank (court); proponents of the 'objective theory of contracts'; R2 §§24, 201*
  > A contract has, strictly speaking, nothing to do with the personal, or individual, intent of the parties. … If … it were proved by twenty bishops that either party … intended something else than the usual meaning which the law imposes upon them, he would still be held
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Contract law imposes obligations regardless of the promisor's attempt to disclaim promissory force. This is a divergence case where public rules, not producer attitude, settle force. — *Lucy v. Zehmer; the 'not a legally enforceable agreement' clause cases; Scanlon's 'I'm not promising' disclaimer discussion*
  > Contractual obligations are imposed notwithstanding a promisor's attempt to negate their promissory intent
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — Rosen's Gricean reframing keeps intention central. What matters is the parties' 'legal intentions' to produce legal effects through their speech acts. The legal-intention view is itself a producer-psychology view of force. — *Gideon Rosen (2011), following Grice (1957)*
  > The parties' legal intentions—which seem to matter, ultimately—can come apart from their semantic intentions.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Raz's promising and the standard picture ground obligation in an intention to bind by the very act of communicating. The entry says the standard picture needs an account of prospective intention. — *Raz (1977); Fried; Audi; Bratman*
  > a promise arises when a person communicates … an intention to undertake by the very act of communication an obligation to perform an action
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The standard-form clicking puzzle. The law finds contractual intent in thoughtless acts where intentionality seems absent, which supports force being conferred publicly rather than by psychology. The entry's own fix is a dispositional theory of intention, which is the closest psychologistic rival. — *Rakoff 1983; Radin 2007, 2014; Barnett 2002; the entry's dispositional proposal*
  > the consumer's actions in virtue of which the law finds an intention to be bound appear thoughtless—that is, lacking in intentionality.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — The 'reasonable person' gloss on objective meaning works as an objective proxy for intention. It is the closest thing to an 'objectives-based proxy view' in the entry. — *R2 §201 and the objective theory's proponents*
  > objective meaning concerns what a reasonable person in the position of the addressee would have inferred was meant based on the available evidence.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Offer and acceptance are defined in terms of manifestations and of justifying the addressee's understanding. This is a public-uptake structure, and it also fixes offer and acceptance as legal powers conferred by rules. — *Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§24, 50; Markovits and Benson on the contract relation*
  > so made as to justify another person in understanding that his assent to that bargain is invited and will conclude it.

**Nearest positions.** Objective theory of contracts: public or reasonable-person meaning, not private intent — Hotchkiss, R2 §§24, 201; Rosen's Gricean legal-intention framework: force turns on parties' legal intentions, distinct from semantic intentions — Rosen (2011), following Grice; Intention-based promise and standard-picture views, with the dispositional-intention repair for form contracts — Raz, Fried, the entry's §2.3, Radin, Barnett

**The entry is silent on.** The entry never discusses illocutionary force, Austin or Searle, or 'uptake' as a speech-act concept, nor a status-conferring or institutional-practice account of force. Grice is its only speech-act resource. It never mentions Moffatt, so it neither supports nor undermines Moffatt as a case of illocution. It does not discuss 'objectives-based proxy views' by name, and it offers no divergence-test methodology for separating practice-fixed force from intention-fixed force.
