**What is left.** Austin's own taxonomy places the speaker's thoughts, feelings and intentions in Γ, so a denial that an LLM performs an apology or promise on the ground that it lacks intentions or remorse needs an added premise. That premise is that the act type is intention-constituted and not a conventional procedure whose A/B conditions the output can meet. The thesis has to pitch this as a sharpening of the existing conventionalism/intentionalism dispute, applied to LLM speech-act denials (mainly Gubelmann's explicit intention premise and the intention parts of Magnus et al.). It must not present it as a discovery that the debate overlooked Austin. It should drop the claim that Attah shares the premise, and should not say Magnus et al. rely on intention alone, since they also appeal to relational and moral standing. It must credit Butlin–Viebahn, Williams–Bayne and Löhr for already treating the issue as one of normative status and proxy, and say what an Austin-A/B framing adds to them. The unoccupied part is the explicit Austinian A/B/Γ regrouping of the LLM positions.

**Not found.** I found no peer-reviewed paper that argues, in terms of Austin's A/B/Γ conditions, that intention-based LLM speech-act denials mistake an abuse for a misfire. That is weak evidence, because I saw only abstracts, extracts and secondary reports. I did not retrieve the full texts of Gubelmann, Magnus et al., Butlin–Viebahn or Löhr, so I cannot say whether any of them mention the misfire/abuse distinction. I found no source that checks the thesis's historical claims: that Searle treats a lack of sincerity-grade states as compatible with performing an insincere assertion, and that the Strawsonian/Gricean rewrite of Austin turned intention into a performance condition. I found nothing on Skiba, though the thesis names that work. I did not locate Green & Michel's proxy-account paper directly, only mentions of it. My empirical searches covered the psychology and HCI work on AI apology only. I did not search linguistics on apology as a conventional routine, or sociology or economics of institutional speech. The thesis's key premise, that apology, promise and advice are at least partly procedure-constituted, got no direct test. I saw only the general conventionalist/intentionalist framing.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Force conventionalism: every speech act invokes an extra-semantic convention or procedure, and Austin's felicity conditions are procedure-centred. The thesis's 'procedure-constituted' side is this view. — *Austin; Searle (weaker form)*
  > There must exist an accepted conventional procedure having a certain conventional effect, that procedure to include the uttering of certain words by certain persons in certain circumstances…
- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — Intentionalism: speech acts are explained by discernible communicative intentions, not by conventions. The entry says it is compatible with conventions for special cases such as appointing and excommunicating, which is a split by act type like the thesis's. — *Strawson 1964; Skinner 1970 (conventions matter for historical understanding)*
  > Strawson contends that rather than appealing to a series of extra-semantic conventions to account for the possibility of speech acts, we explain that possibility in terms of our ability to discern one another's communicative intentions.
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — The entry defines speech acts through speaker meaning and overt commitment. Intention is therefore built into the definition of a speech act, not just added as an extra felicity condition. — *Green (entry author), following Grice and Strawson*
  > a speech act is a type of act that can be performed by speaker meaning that one is doing so. This conception still counts resigning, promising, asserting and asking as speech acts, while ruling out convincing, insulting and whispering.
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — Procedure alone does not suffice. Uttering a performative sentence without speaker meaning performs no act, and merely trying or intending is not enough either. — *Green (somniloquist case); Green 2013; Davidson 1979 (jokers and actors)*
  > while talking in my sleep I might say, "I hereby promise to climb the Eiffel Tower," without thereby making any promise.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Insincerity is an abuse, not a misfire: the act is still performed. A promise made with no intention to keep it is a promise that is infelicitous but valid. — *Austin, as presented by the entry*
  > Here I have promised all right, but the act is not felicitous because it is not sincere. My act is, more precisely, an abuse because although it is a speech act, it fails to live up to a standard appropriate for speech acts of its kind.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Misfires arise from lack of authority, social position or audience uptake. These are status or standing conditions, not mental states. — *Austin; Searle and Vanderveken (preparatory conditions); Langton and Hornsby (silencing)*
  > Such conditions often concern the social status of interlocutors. For instance, a person cannot bequeath an object unless she already owns it or has power of attorney
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Standing to perform an act can be lost or denied through community response, independently of the speaker's intentions. — *Green (Section 9); Langton 1993; Hornsby and Langton 1998*
  > the inveterate promise-breaker will, in time, lead others in his community to be unwilling to accept any promises he tries to make. He can perform countless locutionary acts but will be unable to perform the illocutionary act of promising, at least in this community.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Some illocutionary conventions, or performative effects, can be secured without the speaker intending them to be performed. Alternatively, speech acts can be performed without intending that act, via intentionally performing others. — *Reimer 1995 (illocutionary conventions); Searle and Vanderveken (strong illocutionary commitment); Hare 1989; Millikan 1998 (natural conventions)*
  > it is difficult to see how one can perform S n while having no intention of performing a speech act at all.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Searle holds that manifesting an intention to perform an act suffices for performing it. Green objects that this lacks overtness and that intentions must be efficacious. — *Searle 1989; Green 2013*
  > Searle also takes it that manifesting an intention to perform a speech act is sufficient for the performance of that act.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Speech acts as overt commitment: liabilities and norms of challenge and retraction, not inner states, mark the act. This could ground a 'standing' account that does not turn on intention. — *Green (Section 5.3); Brandom and Williamson in the bibliography and in the 'How do you know?' norm*
  > speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment

**Nearest positions.** Force conventionalism: speech acts are constituted by invoking conventions or constitutive rules (Austin 1962; Searle 1969, weaker form); Intentionalism via speaker meaning: acts are constituted by overtly manifested communicative intentions (Strawson 1964; Grice-based; Green 2019); Illocutionary conventions and natural conventions as a mixed route (Reimer 1995; Millikan 1998; Bach and Harnish's standardization)

**The entry is silent on.** Whether artificial systems such as LLMs can perform speech acts such as apologizing or promising, whether they act in their own name or through proxies, and whether standing might track 'mind-involving capacities'. 'Artificial intelligence' appears only in a list of disciplines and Shoham 1993 only in the bibliography. The entry has no discussion of agency by proxy or delegation, and it does not use Austin's A/B/Γ labels, only the misfire/abuse distinction. It does not frame the issue as intention-constituted versus procedure-constituted act types for particular acts.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — austin-jl

- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The entry splits infelicity into misfires (the act is not achieved: wrong formula, wrong person, wrong circumstances) and abuses (the act is performed, but insincerely). Failures of sincerity-type states therefore do not void the act. The thesis can build its A/B versus Γ reading on this. — *Austin (1962b), as reported in the entry*
  > Second, there are abuses: in these cases, the act is performed, but insincerely, perhaps for example in instituting a marriage of convenience.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The entry's examples of misfire are procedural or circumstantial: incorrect formula, already married, purser rather than captain. None is a missing intention. This fits the claim that the conditions that make an act fail to occur are procedure-constituted. — *Austin (1962b: 15–16)*
  > if we…utter the formula incorrectly, or if…we are not in a position to do the act because we are…married already, or it is the purser and not the captain who is conducting the ceremony, then the act in question, …marrying, is not successfully performed at all
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — In the action-and-excuses material, Austin holds that responsibility needs nothing more than that the act be a normal instance of something the agent does. Voluntariness or purpose is not an extra requirement ('No modification without aberration'). A referee could use this as a parallel from Austin's other work: intention is not a default condition on agency. — *Austin (1957, 1966)*
  > nothing more need be true than that the action is a normal or standard instance of something they do. For example, it need not be true of the actor, in addition, that they did what they did voluntarily or on purpose
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — The entry describes the illocutionary act as classified by force. It says the various modes of assessment apply most fundamentally to that act. It does not say what constitutes an illocutionary act (conventional procedure, intention or uptake). The reception literature in the bibliography names Searle, Bach and Harnish, Strawson, Schiffer and de Gaynesford ('Uptake in Action'). The entry gives none of their positions beyond titles and name-lists, but a referee would raise them as the intention-based or uptake-based rivals. — *Austin; the debate is cited only by name (Bach and Harnish 1979; Strawson 1964a; Schiffer 1972; Searle 1968, 1969; de Gaynesford 2018)*
  > an act classifiable not only by its content—as with the locutionary act—but also by its force (stating, warning, promising, etc.)
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — On the entry's account, Austin treats judgment and speaking as exercises of language-involving 'judgmental capacities' that are limited and fallible. This is a neighbour for the thesis's closing suggestion that standing tracks mind-involving capacities. The entry applies it to knowledge and perception, not to speech-act standing. — *Austin (1946, 1962a), as reported in the entry*
  > Knowledge arises through the successful exercise of judgmental capacities in propitious circumstances—that is, through a combination of acumen and opportunity

**Nearest positions.** Austin's own felicity framework (misfire versus abuse, from How to Do Things with Words) — Austin, as reported in the entry; Intention- and communication-based accounts of illocution — Searle, Bach and Harnish, Strawson, Schiffer; named in the entry's bibliographic lists but not described; Uptake-based readings of illocution — de Gaynesford 2018, 'Uptake in Action'; title only in the bibliography; Hornsby's work on illocution and performatives — cited but not described

**The entry is silent on.** The entry never uses the A/B/Γ labels or lists the felicity conditions in full. It does not say whether Austin's conditions require intention. It does not discuss whether act types like apology or promise are intention-constituted or procedure-constituted. It says nothing about proxy-occupied roles, status or standing, or artificial or LLM speakers. It does not discuss denials of speech-act performance on the basis of lacking intention.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — On Austin's own taxonomy, insincerity (the lack of the appropriate intention or belief) is an infelicity of the abuse kind. It does not stop the act from being performed. Failures of procedure or presupposition are a separate class (flaw-type misexecutions, misinvocations). — *Austin 1962, as reported in the entry*
  > an assertion is insincere in case of lying as a promise is insincere when the appropriate intention is lacking (Austin 1962 [1975: 40]). This is an infelicity of the abuse kind.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Austin treats void assertions (failed reference) as procedure failures of the same kind as using the wrong legal formula or failing the requirements of a naming procedure. This is a procedure-constituted picture of the act. — *Austin, as reported in the entry*
  > an assertion is, according to Austin, void in case of a failed referential presupposition... This is then an infelicity of the same kind—flaw-type misexecutions—as the use of the wrong formula in a legal procedure
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Intention-based accounts of illocutionary acts. Grice-style, Searle's reflexive-intention analysis and Bach & Harnish's expressed-belief-plus-intention analysis all make hearer-directed intentions constitutive of asserting. These are the 'intention-based denials' the thesis targets. — *Grice 1957; Searle 1969; Bach & Harnish 1979*
  > S asserts that p iff S expresses i. the belief that p, and ii. the intention that H believe that p.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Strawson's mixed verdict. Communicative intentions are largely irrelevant for highly conventionalized acts, while convention does little work for ordinary illocutionary types. This is a near-neighbour of the intention-versus-procedure dichotomy the thesis draws. — *Strawson 1964*
  > when it comes to highly conventionalized utterances, communicative intentions are largely irrelevant, but... convention does not play much role for ordinary illocutionary types.
- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — Convention or procedure is neither necessary nor sufficient for assertion. Stainton and Strawson show assertions without conventional force-indicators. Davidson and Bach & Harnish argue that any conventional sign can be used insincerely or in acting, so convention is not sufficient. Williamson argues that constitutive-rule acts cannot be conventional. — *Strawson 1964; Stainton 1996, 2006; Davidson 1979, 1984; Bach & Harnish 1979; Williamson 1996*
  > If Strawson and Stainton are right, convention isn't necessary for making assertions... If Davidson, and Bach and Harnish are right, then conventions are also not sufficient
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Constitutive-rule and normative accounts that do not need an intention condition. Searle's constitutive rule (counts as an undertaking), Brandom/Peirce commitment, and Rescorla's view that there is no norm of making an assertion, only norms governing later reactions. These are status-style accounts that could host the thesis's 'standing' idea. — *Searle 1969; Brandom 1994; Peirce; Alston 2000; Rescorla 2009a*
  > Making an assertion has normative consequences... some authors focus on the distinctive responsibilities ('commitments') that a communicator takes on
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — The entry flags intention requirements on asserting as an open question. It also says insincere assertions are still assertions, and that Pagin's speaker-side belief profile is only a default. — *Entry; Pagin 2011, 2020*
  > there are more general questions about what intentions are required of a speaker in order for his utterance to qualify as an assertion. For instance, must he intend it to be an assertion? Must it be made voluntarily?
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Austin's uptake requirement, and the entry's point that it is less plausible for speaker–content verbs such as 'assert'. Bill can assert even if no hearer is present. — *Austin 1962; the entry's own critique*
  > Bill failed to tell Lisa that the station was closed... but Bill still asserted that it was closed, since Bill believed she was still there.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Mind-involving capacity conditions on asserting. Jary's metarepresentation safeguards, Sperber & Wilson's 'humanly graspable' objection to reflexive intentions, and the self-representation accounts all tie assertion to cognitive capacities. — *Jary 2010; Sperber & Wilson 1986; Davidson; Green 2007*
  > cognitive safeguards consist in the ability of the hearer to not simply accept what is said but meta-represent the speaker as expressing certain beliefs and intentions

**Nearest positions.** Austin's felicity-conditions framework: abuse-type insincerity versus procedural flaws and misinvocations — Austin 1962; Communicative-intention analyses of asserting (Gr-A, Searle's reflexive intentions, Bach & Harnish's expressed belief plus intention) — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish; criticized by Strawson and Sperber & Wilson; Conventionalist versus constitutive-rule and commitment/status accounts of assertion — Dummett, Searle, Kölbel versus Strawson, Stainton, Davidson, Williamson; Brandom and Green on commitment

**The entry is silent on.** The entry says nothing about artificial agents or LLMs as asserters, proxies or delegated roles, or institutional 'status' or 'standing' as a basis for performing an act. It does not use Austin's A/B/Γ labels or discuss whether apology and promise are intention-constituted or procedure-constituted. It never takes up whether an intention-based denial of speech-act performance imports a non-Austinian condition.
