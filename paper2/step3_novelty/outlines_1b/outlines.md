## Outline 0 — The Unit of Assertion Failure: Assessing Chatbot Hallucination, Sycophancy and Sanction at the Level of the Policy

*Grant the antecedent that chatbot outputs are assertions and ask which object bears assertion's defects; use a same-string/different-policy test to show the policy does (about a third of the words go to this load-bearing step), extend it to sanction and then to sincerity, and finish by showing these verdicts survive every content reading the evidence leaves open, including the unsettled 'p, with confidence c' reading.*

- **Surprise.** A specialist would expect the policy-level move to be argued from the metaphysics of the speaker (against Chalmers's thread or instance view). Here it is argued from a same-string/different-policy test that makes the choice of speaker nearly irrelevant, and the ML results (Kalai on grading, Sharma on preference training) are used as premises about what sanction is, so that string-level grading is shown to cause hallucination. A second surprise is that sycophancy, not resampling, is the evidence that would show insincerity.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the string not being the unit of assessment even granting that every output is an assertion. It commits to sanction being a policy-level matter that deployer responsibility cannot replace, and to resampling disagreement having a likelihood ratio of about one for insincerity. It also commits to the open content question being a robustness test of these verdicts, not a hedge on them, so a cautious version that conditioned everything on calibrated 'p, with confidence c' would be the weaker paper.
- **Gap.** §3, abductive: §3 moves from a causal fact (sanction works only by reshaping the output distribution) to a normative claim (the policy is the party sanctioned, and the sanction's adequacy is assessable there). This goes through only if the antecedent 'outputs are assertions' is read with a sanction concept on which credibility-tracking feedback onto the asserter's dispositions suffices, or on which the training objective gives the policy something at stake. Butlin & Viebahn's third element, that losing credibility be bad for the asserter, must then be met at policy level or be dispensable. If it requires interests that no policy has, the antecedent plus their condition is unsatisfiable and §3's claim is vacuous. A further requirement is that deployer responsibility is not an alternative locus for the same assessment, but a separate question about deployment decisions.
- **Arrives at.** §5
- **Load-bearing.** Whether an output is a hallucination or a sycophantic assertion is fixed by the output distribution that produced it, including its calibration and how it varies with the prompt, not by the sampled string and its truth value.

## Outline 1 — Assessing the Distribution, Not the Draw: Hallucination, Sycophancy and Sanction in Chatbot Assertion

*Suppose chatbot outputs are assertions. Separate the level of assessment from the identity of the speaker (§1). Show that hallucination and sycophancy are distributional defects measured against calibration as a standard (§2). Show that sanction acts on the policy, the same channel that produces those defects (§3). Show that the asserted content's being the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is unsettled (§4). Use that unsettledness as a premise for the claim that resampling inconsistency does not discriminate sincerity from insincerity (§5).*

- **Surprise.** A specialist would expect the thesis's last sentence ('not established') to be a hedge at the end. Here it is a premise placed before the sincerity claim, and that claim is argued as robust across the live readings of content. A specialist would also expect Chalmers's and Shanahan's anti-model arguments to be rebutted on who the speaker is. Here they are made irrelevant by separating the level of assessment from the identity of the speaker. Hallucination, sycophancy and sanction are also unified as effects of one training and evaluation regime, and the learning-theory (Kalai et al.) and sycophancy (Sharma et al.) papers are used as premises in a philosophical argument.
- **Commitment.** It commits to the claim that a sampled string cannot be hallucinatory or sycophantic as an asserter's defect, only as a symptom of the distribution. It commits to the claim that deployer-only accounts of sanction (Butlin & Viebahn, Gómez) are incomplete under the supposition. It commits to classifying minority samples on a bare-categorical reading as overclaim and not insincerity, which is where the paper can fail. A cautious version would call policy-level assessment 'a useful additional perspective', would not use the content question as a premise, and would not take a position on how to classify overclaim.
- **Gap.** §6, abductive: The paper moves from 'spread across resamples is what a stable policy with a non-degenerate distribution predicts' to 'spread is not evidence of insincerity'. That requires (a) sincerity to be a relation between what an utterance conveys and the policy's state, so that a draw from the distribution is not by itself a mismatch with it; (b) that on the bare-categorical reading, where a sample asserts p flatly although the distribution puts little mass on p, this be classified as overclaim, a calibration and warrant failure assessed per §3, and not as asserting what one does not believe. If overclaim counts as insincerity, the clause is false on that reading. (c) That decoding randomness (temperature, which is a deployer setting) be a performance slip and not an attitude. The greatest distance is that the clause is argued across content readings §5 has left open, and no reading is established.
- **Arrives at.** §6
- **Load-bearing.** If chatbot outputs are assertions, hallucination and sycophancy are defects of the trained policy's output distribution, assessed against calibration as a standard, and are not properties of sampled strings.

## Outline 2 — The String Is Not the Unit: Assessing Chatbot Assertion at the Level of the Policy

*Grant the antecedent that chatbot outputs are assertions, fix a criterion for the right level of assessment, show that the speaker question does not block applying it at the policy, apply it to hallucination, then to sycophancy and sanction, then to resampling, and finish by showing that the evidence for calibrated 'p, with confidence c' content falls short.*

- **Surprise.** A specialist would expect an argument for policy-level assessment to defend the policy as the speaker. This one concedes the speaker to Chalmers (thread or virtual instance), gives up the model-as-speaker rival, and wins the thesis by separating the speaker from the level of assessment. It also takes the engineers' inference from sample variation to unreliability (Manakul et al.; Xiong et al.) as correct while denying the philosophers' inference from the same variation to insincerity.
- **Commitment.** The paper commits to a false string being neither necessary nor sufficient for hallucination, to sycophancy being unattributable to any single output, to sanction being a training channel and not exclusively a deployer relation, and to resampling variation carrying no weight on sincerity. A cautious version would call policy-level assessment a useful supplement. The paper also commits to its closing negative, that the available evidence leaves the 'p, with confidence c' reading unsettled, and so can be overturned by future calibration evidence or by a flat-assertion argument.
- **Gap.** §4, abductive: §4 measures hallucination against the policy's behavioural calibration, a threshold-style property in Kalai et al.'s sense, and then treats a failure of that standard as an assertoric fault. Yet §7 says it is not established that what is asserted is 'p, with confidence c'. The bridge holds only if flat assertion of bare p already carries a graded threshold norm, so that a policy whose rate of answering outruns its accuracy violates the norm of assertion dispositionally even though the content is just p. If the norm for flat assertion is instead Williamson's knowledge norm with no threshold reading, then calibration is only a reliability measure of an instrument, and §4's claim that hallucination is an assertoric failure at the policy level has to be rebuilt on safety rather than calibration.
- **Arrives at.** §7
- **Load-bearing.** A failure of assertion is best assessed at the level at which its identity conditions are fixed and at which correction or sanction can take hold on it.

It also keeps the unresolved calibration-content question in the middle of the paper and builds the sincerity argument to hold on both readings.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the claim that string-level assessment of hallucination and sycophancy is the wrong target and not merely an incomplete one, since a perfectly calibrated policy produces false flat outputs without any defect. ## Outline 4 — Judge the Policy, Not the String: Assertion, Hallucination, Sycophancy and Resampling in Chatbots

*Supposing chatbot outputs are assertions, show that the three failures named in the thesis are constituted at the level of the output distribution; that this holds whoever the speaker is; that what the policy asserts is left open; that resampling divergence then fails to indicate insincerity; and that the policy beats the string and the deployer as the unit of assessment.*

- **Surprise.** The structure separates the locus of assessment from the identity of the speaker. - **Commitment.** The paper commits to inconsistency being no evidence of insincerity, not weak evidence, and to the string being the wrong unit for these three assessments even while conceding that per-assertion norm violation is string-level. It refuses to rest on the credence reading of content, leaving the content claim open at the thesis's own strength. It also stakes the sanction claim against Gómez and Butlin & Viebahn on the ground that deployer assessment is downstream of the policy.

- **Load-bearing.** Hallucination as a failing to be assessed and remedied, sycophancy, sanction-sensitivity and calibration are each constituted by how a system's outputs vary across prompts and samples, so no single sampled string settles whether they are present.

## Outline 5 — The Policy Is Where the Assertion Goes Wrong: Hallucination, Sycophancy, Sanction and the Resampling Fallacy

*Suppose chatbot outputs are assertions. Individuate the faults of an asserter (hallucination, sycophancy) by what they are, which shows they are distributional. Fix the level (trained policy, not string, thread, role or deployer) by showing that each rival level either inherits the fault from the policy or cannot register it. Then show that the content-and-calibration question stays open, and that this openness is what strips resampling inconsistency of its force as evidence of insincerity. Finally show that the level verdicts survive every live answer to the content question.*

- **Surprise.** A specialist would expect the content question ('p, with confidence c') to appear as a closing concession. Here it is argued early as a premise (§5) and does the work of disarming the resampling-as-insincerity inference. A specialist would also expect Chalmers's incoherence argument against the model-as-speaker to be a threat to a policy-level view. Here cross-context contradiction becomes the very data of sycophancy assessment, and the consistent systematic sycophant is used to show that resampling consistency clears no one.
- **Commitment.** The outline commits to the claim that sycophancy cannot be assessed at the level of the string or the thread at all, not merely that the policy level is also available. It commits to the claim that deployer sanctionability is no substitute for sanction of the asserter, and that the level verdicts hold on every live answer to the content question. A cautious version would say policy-level assessment is 'a useful complement' and would not stake the verdict on the content hypothesis.
- **Gap.** §7, abductive: From 'the faults are distributional' (§2), 'thread and role inherit them from the policy' (§3) and 'sanction reaches the system through training' (§4), the thesis claims the trained policy is the BEST level of assessment. That is a comparative claim, and it also silently equates the assessed distribution with the trained policy, not the deployed system with its decoding temperature, system prompt and scaffolding. It goes through only if (i) the trained policy's conditional distribution is the dominant and stable determinant of fault rates under those deployment variations, so that assessing the policy predicts the deployed system (Manakul et al. note that sampled distributions are partly artefacts of the sampling regime), and (ii) no rival unit (string, thread, deployer) offers an assessment that is both actionable and non-redundant with the policy-level one. Part (ii) is where Williamson's per-act norm (§7) and the deployer-locus views of Butlin & Viebahn and Gómez (§4) must be shown to supply nothing the policy-level verdict does not already supply.

- **Load-bearing.** Hallucination and sycophancy, taken as faults of an asserter rather than as mere false or agreeable strings, are properties of the output distribution across samples and prompts (its calibration and its dependence on the user's stated views), so no single sampled string by itself bears either fault.

## Divergence

**Forced** — every outline does this, so the thesis requires it:

- Grant the antecedent that chatbot outputs are assertions and argue conditionally from it
- Argue that hallucination and sycophancy are properties of the output distribution (calibration, variation across prompts and samples), not of a single sampled string
- Argue that sanction works only by reshaping the trained policy through training, so it is assessed at the policy level
- Argue that resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity, because it reflects the spread of the output distribution
- Treat the 'p, with confidence c' content question as unsettled, with calibration evidence not fixing what a given output asserts
- Close with a section restating the full thesis as the arrival point, framed by an intro and a final non-argumentative section
