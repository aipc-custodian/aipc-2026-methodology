Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: assertion, testimony-episprob, artificial-intelligence — The theses concern whether and by whom assertion occurs in chatbot output, which is the debate in 'assertion'; the user's adoption of content they did not author is a testimony-transmission issue (testimony-episprob), and the AI entry gives the chatbot context.

Where a user publicly puts forward chatbot output as their own, the user is the speaker. Designers who do not put forward particular outputs are not their principals. Creator-centred proxy accounts therefore fail for such cases.

What is left. Williams & Bayne have already argued that proxy accounts (Green & Michel, Arora) cover only deployments where a person or organisation has adopted blanket endorsement of the outputs, so the claim that designer-centred proxy accounts do not reach ordinary chatbot use is not new. What survives, as far as I could check, is the positive claim: when a user publicly puts forward chatbot output as their own, the user is the speaker, because adoption plus answerability suffices for assertion without a prior content-intention. On that claim the intention requirement for assertion falls on the one who puts the content forward, not on the producer of the content. It follows that the model-level question 'does the chatbot assert?' (Gubelmann's target) and the question whether assertion occurs in chatbot-mediated discourse come apart. The thesis also has to show that denial arguments really infer the second from the first, since Gubelmann's stated conclusion is about whether LLMs are speakers.

Not found. I found no source arguing that adoption suffices for assertion without prior content-intention for chatbot output. I found none naming the user as the principal or speaker in the proxy debate, and none using the notebook-retrieval, passed-on testimony or ghostwriting analogies for this question. These absences come from abstracts and snippets only. I did not read the full text of Butlin & Viebahn (section on proxy assertion), Williams & Bayne (any user case), the 2026 Synthese paper or the 2026 Phil & Tech accountability paper, and any of them could contain the user-side move. I did not search the general philosophy of assertion on whether assertion requires a content-intention, including selfless assertion, transmission of testimony, ghostwritten or reported speech and Kirk Ludwig's 'Proxy Assertion' (2020), which appeared only as a title. From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Commitment/accountability accounts locate assertion in the speaker's taking on responsibility. A user who publicly puts forward output as their own incurs that liability, whatever the output's source. — Peirce; Alston; Brandom; Green; MacFarlane

an act of assertion […] renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law (or, at any rate, those of the moral law) in case [the asserted proposition] should not be true

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Assertion is characterised by the speaker's own relation to the content (representing oneself as believing or knowing, expressing belief), and speakers can be responsible for content they did not originate. This fits the user adopting the output. — Black; Davidson; Unger; Slote; Green 2007

in asserting that p the speaker represents herself as believing that p

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Deference or testimony cases: a speaker may defer responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony they rely. This is a model for the user taking over chatbot output while still answering for it. — MacFarlane 2003; Goldberg 2015

or (when appropriate) to defer this responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony one is relying

ADJACENT (1/5) — Austin's uptake and the speaker-content structure. The entry says assertion, unlike telling, may not depend on a hearer. It also separates the speaker's act from the audience's reaction. — Austin; the entry's own discussion

it is less plausible when the structure is speaker-content (“Bill asserted that p”)

ADJACENT (2/5) — Intention-based accounts require the speaker to have communicative intentions and a belief. A user who relays output without checking it may lack the required belief or intention, so sincerity and 'belief-expression' conditions pose a problem. — Grice; Bach & Harnish; Searle

S asserts that p iff S expresses i. the belief that p, and ii. the intention that H believe that p.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Norm-of-assertion debate. If the user is the speaker, she is subject to the knowledge, justification or belief norm. The selfless-assertion case (a teacher asserting what she does not believe) is a close analogue. — Williamson; Lackey; Kneer

it would be appropriate for Stella, as a teacher, to assert (31)

ADJACENT (1/5) — Cappelen holds that assertion is a defective theoretical category, and Greenberg that there is no sui generis norm. Either could undercut the thesis's use of 'speaker' and 'principal'. — Cappelen 2011, 2020; Greenberg 2020

Cappelen, Herman, 2020, “Assertion: A Defective Theoretical Category”

Nearest positions. Commitment/accountability accounts of assertion — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Self-representation as believing or knowing — Black, Davidson, Unger, Slote; Communicative-intention accounts — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish; Norm-of-assertion views and the selfless-assertion case — Williamson, Lackey, Goldberg

The entry is silent on. Chatbots, AI-generated or machine-generated text, and non-human or non-agentive sources of content. Also silent on 'principals', designers or creators as asserters, proxy or agency-based accounts of assertion, and cases where a speaker relays content from an artefact. The creator-centred proxy view the thesis rejects is not described anywhere.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — testimony-episprob

ADJACENT (3/5) — Deflationism about group testimony. A group's testimony reduces to the testimony of some individual, whether a member or a 'mere spokesman'. This is the entry's closest model for 'the person who puts the content forward is the testifier, not the body behind it'. The thesis extends the same move to a user relaying chatbot output. — Lackey (2014) deflationary account; opposed by Inflationists such as Tollefsen (2007)

Deflationists maintain to a group's testimony that p can be reduced to some individual's testimony that p (regardless of whether those individuals are members of the group, or just mere spokesmen)

ADJACENT (2/5) — Summativism vs Non-Summativism, and Inflationism. A speaker-like entity can testify even if no individual does. A creator-centred proxy account would resemble the view that a collective or institutional source is itself the testifier. — Tollefsen (2007), Lackey (2014), Hardwig (1985), Faulkner (2018)

Non-Summativists maintain that it is possible for a group to testify that p even if none of its members do.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Lackey's disjunctive account includes 'hearer testimony'. An act counts as testimony if the hearer reasonably takes it as conveying information, whatever the speaker intended. On this view chatbot output can be testimony without anyone being the 'speaker'. It also undercuts any assumption that a speaker must be identified. — Lackey (2008: 30–32)

testimony is a source of knowledge and justified belief regardless of what the speaker intended to say.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Accounts of testimony tied to the speaker's offering and intention can ground 'putting forward as one's own' as what makes someone the testifier. Coady and Graham require the statement to be 'offered as evidence'. The Assurance View's Telling requires speaker intentions. The user's public adoption supplies exactly those. — Coady (1992: 42); Graham (1997: 227); Hinchman (2005)

S's stating that p is evidence that p and is offered as evidence that p.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Testimony as assertion, and whether assertion is necessary or sufficient for testifying. If testimony is assertion, a user who asserts chatbot output as their own is the asserter and so the testifier. Goldberg denies that assertion is even necessary. — E. Fricker 1987, Sosa 1994; Goldberg 2010b

one testifies that p if, and only if, one asserts that p.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Transmission and Generation. A relaying speaker may lack knowledge or justification, as with the Creationist Teacher and Persistent Believer cases. If the user is the speaker, their epistemic standing is relevant to what hearers acquire, and the chatbot's reliability sits upstream of the user's own epistemic state. — Lackey (2008); Graham 2006b; Goldberg 2005; Wright 2015, 2019

Can a hearer acquire testimonial knowledge that p from a speaker who does not know that p themselves?

ADJACENT (2/5) — Testimonial Reliabilism and Anti-Individualism count the processes producing the speaker's testimony as relevant. If designers are not the speaker, the chatbot's reliability may be an upstream process of the user-speaker, not a separate testifier's. — Graham (2000a, 2000b, 2006a); Goldberg (2010a); Lackey (2008)

the processes involved in the production of the speaker's testimony

Nearest positions. Deflationism about group testimony: testimony reduces to that of an individual, including a spokesman — Lackey (2014); Inflationism / Non-Summativism: the collective itself can be the testifier — Tollefsen (2007), Faulkner (2018); Lackey's disjunctive speaker/hearer testimony, on which testimony does not require that the speaker intended it — Lackey (2008); Offering and intention accounts of testimony and telling — Coady (1992), Graham (1997), Hinchman (2005)

The entry is silent on. The entry says nothing about artificial or non-human sources such as chatbots or AI. It does not treat delegated or proxy speech, principals and agents, responsibility for relayed content, or designers or creators as candidate testifiers or proxies. 'Creator-centred proxy accounts' have no counterpart in it. Its only analogues are the group-testimony debates over spokesmen and members versus the group.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Descartes' test: a machine may emit words, but does not 'act from knowledge'. On this view the machine is not the one speaking, which leaves open who is. — Descartes (1637), quoted in the entry's history section

we can easily understand a machine's being constituted so that it can utter words ... they did not act from knowledge, but only for the disposition of their organs.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Moral AI and machine ethics treat machines as autonomous deciders with 'responsibility'. The entry's example is a lying machine that persuades people of falsehoods. A referee would ask who the liar is. — Wallach & Allen (2010); Clark (2010); Bello & Bringsjord (2013)

Clark (2010) uses a computational theory of the mind ... to build a lying machine that successfully persuades people into believing falsehoods.

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry says Moral AI is not concerned with where a machine's moral code comes from, whether designers, users, or inference. This is a weak parallel to the question of whose communicative intentions an output carries. — Moral AI field, as summarised in the entry

The source could be humans, and the machine could receive the code directly (via explicit encoding) or indirectly (reading).

ADJACENT (1/5) — Humans hold robots to different ethical standards than humans in like conditions. This bears on attributing responsibility for machine acts and may complicate a purely user-centred attribution. — Malle et al. (2015), cited in the entry

humans hold robots to different ethical standards than they expect from humans under similar conditions

ADJACENT (1/5) — The Turing Test makes linguistic indistinguishability the criterion, and the entry notes disputes over whether it has been 'passed'. This concerns whether the machine counts as a linguistic agent, not who is speaking when a user publishes its text. — Turing (1950); Nilsson (1998); Moor (2003)

Passing in this sense operationalizes linguistic indistinguishability.

Nearest positions. Machine speech is mere organ-disposition, not speech from knowledge, so the machine is not a speaker — Descartes (1637), as quoted in the entry; Machines as autonomous moral agents bearing 'responsibility', including deceptive output — Wallach & Allen (2010); Clark (2010); Moor (2006) on a spectrum of moral agents; Linguistic indistinguishability as the criterion of machine intelligence — Turing (1950), Nilsson (1998)
