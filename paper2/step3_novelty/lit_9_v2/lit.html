Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: teleology-biology, content-teleological, artificial-intelligence — The theses turn on an etiological (selection-based) account of purpose that separates organisms from artefacts, which is the debate in teleology in biology; they also engage teleological/selectional grounding of content and proxy speech acts, and LLM goals within AI.

Gubelmann's 'externally fixed goals' premise needs an etiological reading to sort organisms from watches. On that reading, unintended output-time dispositions such as sycophancy and reward hacking strain objective-based proxy accounts. LLMs' placement relative to organisms is left open.

What is left. Granting that Gubelmann's Kantian contrast is already one of autonomy/self-maintenance and that he already places LLMs in an intermediate, moving position, the thesis can still claim a more specific point. If 'externally fixed goals' is read etiologically (derived from a designer's intention-content versus fixed by selection), the unintended output-time dispositions of trained LLMs (sycophancy, reward hacking) do not sit cleanly on the artefact side, and objective-based proxy accounts are strained where no designer objective was intended. That claim must answer two rivals: the reading on which such dispositions are malfunctions relative to a still-available intended objective, and the organisational account on which etiology is the wrong axis.

Credit (precedents the thesis develops). Gubelmann 2024 — already questions the mechanism/organism dichotomy for LLMs and says present models are only 'for now' on the mechanism side; Green & Michel 2022 (via secondhand reports) — grounds proxy speech in the principal's communicative objectives, which is what the thesis targets; Arora 2024 — ties proxy assertion to designer accountability and flags unforeseeable outputs; Williams & Bayne 2024 — notes that Green & Michel's proxy grounding needs an identifiable principal; Etiological function theory (the SEL/INT contrast, Neander-style unification) — supplies the intention-versus-selection distinction; The author of PhilArchive GAROOB-3 — already notes that natural selection and artifact fabrication can both count as externally imposed; ML-safety literature on sycophancy and reward hacking — supplies the evidence for unintended dispositions

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — teleology-biology

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry reports that Cummins-style causal-role analysis is not specific to biology and applies equally to artifacts. A bare 'contribution to a system's capacity' reading therefore cannot sort organisms from watches. This supports the thesis's claim that an etiological reading is needed to do the sorting. — Cummins 1975; Lewens 2004 (as cited by the entry)

there is nothing specifically biological about the pattern of explanation offered by functional analysis; it applies equally well, for example to the contributions made by the components of artifacts

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Etiological and selected-effects accounts ground function in why the trait is there. They are said to be specific to biology and to separate genuine function from accidental utility. This is the machinery the thesis's 'etiological reading' would draw on. — Wright 1973, 1976; Wimsatt 1972; Millikan 1984; Neander 1991a

the function of X is Z means (a) X is there because it does Z, and (b) Z is a consequence (or result) of X's being there

PRECEDENT · ENTERTAINED (2/5) — On the Kant–Ginsborg line, organisms differ from complex artifacts through a natural purposiveness tied to regeneration and reproduction. The entry reports this as a way of sorting organisms from artifacts without appeal to externally fixed goals. This is a rival route to the thesis's sorting problem, not an anticipation of its etiological answer. — Kant 1790; Ginsborg 2004; Aristotle's immanent teleology (as reported)

the regenerative and reproductive aspects of organisms lead us to attribute a kind of natural purposiveness that is absent in artifacts

ADJACENT (2/5) — Organizational accounts sort organisms by self-maintenance, with no appeal to selection history. A referee would raise this as an alternative to the etiological reading. The same literature also covers derived functions in learning systems, where differential persistence or retention within a lifetime plays the role of selection. This is the closest the entry comes to training-like processes producing functions. — Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009; Moreno & Mossio 2015; Millikan 1984 (derived proper function); Bouchard 2013; Garson 2017

using 'differential persistence' and 'differential retention' within an organism's lifespan to play the role that differential reproduction plays in direct natural selection accounts

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry reports a debate over whether function accounts can handle malfunction, along with the proper-function / derived-function machinery. This bears on whether unintended dispositions count as functions or malfunctions on an etiological reading. It does not treat unintended or misaligned outputs of designed systems. — Davies 2001; Wouters 2005; Sullivan-Bissett 2017; Millikan 1984

Davies (2001) argues that the natural selection accounts are unable to provide an account of malfunction insofar as they individuate traits functionally

Nearest positions. Etiological / selected-effects accounts of function, grounded in why a trait is there — Wright; Millikan; Neander; Wimsatt; Cummins-style causal-role functional analysis, which applies equally to artifacts and so does not sort organisms from watches — Cummins 1975; Lewens 2004; Organizational / self-maintenance accounts and Kant–Ginsborg natural purposiveness as non-historical ways of demarcating organisms — Mossio, Saborido & Moreno; Moreno & Mossio; Ginsborg 2004

The entry is silent on. Gubelmann or any 'externally fixed goals' premise, LLMs or AI systems, sycophancy, reward hacking, and objective-based proxy accounts of goal-directedness. The entry does not discuss artifact teleology as grounded in designer-set objectives. It also does not discuss unintended output-time dispositions of trained systems or the placement of LLMs relative to organisms.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — content-teleological

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry separates artifact functions, which rest on design or intention, from biological functions, which rest on selection history. It also requires that functions grounding original intentionality not depend on other intentional phenomena. This gives the thesis its organism/watch sorting criterion. — Wright 1973 (artifact functions as purposive); Neander 1991 and Millikan 1989a (etiological theory); entry's own framing

the functions to which it appeals, as grounding original intentionality, cannot in turn depend on other intentional phenomena.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — On etiological theories a function is what an item was selected for, not what it is currently disposed to do, so a trait can have a function it fails to perform. Selection is not limited to natural selection: learning, conditioning, reward and neural selection also count. This gives the thesis a way to read training as a selection process and to separate trained-for function from output-time disposition. — Millikan 1984; Papineau 1984/1993; Dretske 1986; Garson 2011, 2019a

Papineau (1984) speaks of learning and Dretske (1986) invokes functions that depend on recruitment by conditioning.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Cases where the selected-for effect diverges from the intuitively represented content or behaviour. In the kimu case, Millikan's theory assigns content from the selected effect (snorf-free space), against the creature's apparent behavioural profile. This is a structural analogue to a proxy objective diverging from output-time dispositions. — Pietroski 1992; Millikan 2000; Neander 2006, 2013

On Millikan's theory, Bs mean snorf-free-space and there is no representation of red in a kimu's brain.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Rival views hold that design functions or non-etiological functions can ground content, so an etiological reading is not required to sort organisms from artifacts. Shea allows design functions in place of stabilized functions. Nanay, Piccinini, Schroeder and Bauer combine teleosemantics with non-etiological accounts of function. — Shea 2018; Nanay 2014; Piccinini 2020a; Schroeder 2004; Bauer 2017

Shea allows that representational systems may have design functions instead of stabilized functions, so stabilization is not strictly necessary for representation

ADJACENT (2/5) — Objections to teleosemantics concerning what counts as a representational system. Swampman is the case with no selection history and so no function. Burge's liberalism objection says teleosemantics over-attributes representation to simple systems. Both bear on where an LLM would sit relative to organisms. — Davidson 1987; Millikan 1984; Burge 2010; Artiga 2016; Shea 2018

Swampman's component parts have no functions according to an etiological theory of function and so his "brain" states have no contents

Nearest positions. Etiological/selected-effects teleosemantics, extended to learning and conditioning as selection — Millikan, Papineau, Dretske, Neander, Garson; Non-etiological or design-function teleosemantics, on which an artifact's designed or modal function can ground content — Shea (design functions), Nanay, Piccinini; Liberalism and over-attribution worries, plus the Swampman/no-history problem — Burge, Artiga, Shea, Millikan

The entry is silent on. Gubelmann, large language models, AI systems and machine learning training objectives; sycophancy, reward hacking and proxy-objective accounts; and any application of etiological functions to trained artifacts as opposed to organisms. The entry does not discuss whether training counts as selection that fixes goals, or whether artifacts with non-intentional selection histories qualify for content.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

ADJACENT (2/5) — AI's agents are defined by goals fixed externally by designers: intelligence as rationality, i.e. maximizing expected utility under a designer-specified performance measure U. This is the objective-based, externally fixed-goal picture the thesis targets. The entry offers no etiological reading of those goals. — Russell (1997); Russell & Norvig (AIMA)

A performance measure \(U\) evaluates this sequence; ... a perfectly rational agent can be taken to be the function \(f_{opt}\) which produces the maximum expected utility in the environment under consideration.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry's reinforcement-learning discussion supplies a basis for the thesis: the machine learns from occasional rewards and punishments, so the dispositions it ends up with are shaped by a training history, not read off the stated objective. The entry does not mention reward hacking or sycophancy. — Entry's own exposition of reinforcement learning; Sutton & Barto (1998)

only occasionally receives feedback on its behavior in the form of rewards or punishments. The machine has to learn to behave rationally from this feedback.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Adversarial inputs show a gap between benchmark performance (the optimized objective) and real-world behavior, which is a mild precedent for unintended learned dispositions. — Szegedy et al. 2014, as reported by the entry

has raised doubts about how well performance on benchmarks can translate into performance in real-world systems with sensory noise

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry says machine ethics must worry about unintended consequences of codes and their representations, and the artifact-versus-animal contrast is its own framing. Neither is an etiological account of goals. — Moral AI (Bringsjord et al.); entry's framing that AI aims to build 'artificial animals'

making sure that the moral code and its representation do not lead to unintended consequences

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry treats the question of whether artifacts reach animal- or person-level status as a goal and as a contested philosophical matter (strong vs. weak AI, Chinese Room). That is a different axis from the thesis's organism/watch sorting. — Charniak & McDermott 1985; Haugeland 1985; entry's Section 8

The ultimate goal of AI (which we are very far from achieving) is to build a person, or, more humbly, an animal.

Nearest positions. Intelligence as rationality / bounded optimality: agents maximize expected utility under a designer-specified performance measure — Russell; Russell & Norvig; Reinforcement learning: behavior shaped by occasional reward/punishment feedback, not an explicit specified function — Sutton & Barto, as reported; Strong vs. weak AI and the question whether artifacts can reach animal/person level — Haugeland, Charniak & McDermott; Searle (Chinese Room)

The entry is silent on. Gubelmann or any 'externally fixed goals' premise; etiological or teleosemantic accounts of goals or function (selection history vs. design history) that would sort organisms from watches; sycophancy, reward hacking, specification gaming; large language models and their standing relative to organisms. The entry predates the LLM era and has no discussion of these topics.
