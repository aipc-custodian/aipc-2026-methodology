Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, assertion, intention — The theses are about how illocutionary force is fixed, whether by a prior communicative intention or by later uptake and ownership, so the speech-acts entry is the core debate, assertion covers commitment and retraction, and intention covers the intention-based view being challenged.

Human illocutionary force is often settled incrementally through uptake and later ownership, so a force-fixing intention present at output time cannot by itself be what separates human speakers from LLMs. Whether LLMs show reason-responsive taking-up of and commitment to their earlier outputs remains open, and it is a condition on how the system is organised.

What is left. That human illocutionary force is sometimes settled after output through negotiation and the speaker's later ownership is already argued (McDonald, Kukla, and conversation analysis). What survives is a narrower claim: when the human/LLM contrast is drawn by comparing an output-time intention (as in Gubelmann's malfunction test and Attah's Levelt-style template), it passes over those cases of incremental or retrospective fixing. For those cases, whether an LLM thread supports speech acts depends on whether the system takes up, owns and is held to its earlier outputs, which can be checked at the level of interaction structure. How often retrospective fixing happens in ordinary talk, and what LLM threads actually show, are both left unestablished by the sources I found.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

CONTRADICTED (4/5) — Force is fixed at the time of performance. Later acts can retract a speech act but cannot change what was performed. This cuts against the claim that force is settled incrementally by later ownership. — Sbisà 2007, as reported by Green

nothing I can do on Wednesday can change the fact that I made a promise or assertion on Monday. However, on Wednesday I may be able to retract a claim I made on Monday.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Intentionalism: force is fixed by a manifest communicative intention at the time of utterance. This is the view the thesis says cannot separate humans from LLMs. — Strawson 1964; Searle 1989 (manifesting an intention suffices); Green's overtness account

What makes an utterance of a sentence in the indicative mood a prediction rather than a command, for instance, is that it manifests an intention to be so taken

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Speech acts are undertakings of commitment, and the force-defining norms are reason-responsive: an asserter must answer 'How do you know?' and retract if she cannot. — Green (the entry's own view; cf. Williamson 1996 for the challenge)

she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Intention alone, even when it is common knowledge, does not suffice for a speech act. What matters is publicly accessible commitment, which the speaker must actually undertake. — Green 2013, against Searle's 1969 claim

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Some speech acts depend on audience uptake. Uptake failure produces a misfire, and systematic denial of uptake can silence a speaker. — Austin 1962; Langton 1993; Hornsby and Langton 1998. Bird 2002 and Maitra 2009 dissent.

I cannot bet you $100 on who will win the election unless you accept that bet.

ADJACENT (3/5) — A speaker can perform a speech act without intending to perform that act, by being committed to it through other acts she did intend. Intention is therefore not strictly necessary for each act. — Green, via Searle and Vanderveken's strong illocutionary commitment

it is difficult to see how one can perform S n while having no intention of performing a speech act at all.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speaker meaning without cognitive-effect intentions, with examples such as photocopiers, infants and photos. This is relevant to whether non-standard 'speakers' can mean anything. — Davis 1992; Green 2019 (overt manifestation of commitment)

Davis (1992) provides a range of cases such as speaking to pre-linguistic infants, uncooperative photocopy machines, and photos of deceased loved ones.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speech acts are best situated in conversation, where scorekeeping and Questions Under Discussion track commitments across turns. This is a natural home for the thesis's 'later ownership'. — Roberts 2004, 2012; Lewis 1979; Stalnaker; MacFarlane 2011; Asher and Lascarides 2003

a speech act's "ecological niche" may nevertheless be the conversation.

Nearest positions. Intentionalism about force (force fixed by manifest communicative intention at the time of utterance) — Strawson 1964, Searle 1989, Green's overtness account; Normative-commitment account of force (force is a mode of commitment, with retraction and challenge obligations) — Green 2019/2020, drawing on Williamson 1996 and Brandom (bibliography only); Force conventionalism / uptake-dependence (some force depends on conventions and audience uptake) — Austin 1962, Searle 1969, Langton and Hornsby; Bird 2002 dissenting

The entry is silent on. The entry does not discuss LLMs or machine-generated text as speakers, and it does not discuss force being settled incrementally or retroactively by later uptake and ownership. It treats later acts only as retraction or withdrawal. It also says nothing about organisational or architectural conditions on a system's commitment-responsiveness. 'Artificial intelligence' appears only in the opening list of fields influenced, and Shoham 1993 appears only in the bibliography.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Commitment accounts locate assertion's force in what the speaker is held to or takes on after the utterance: responding to challenges, defending, or retracting. This is a downstream, reason-responsive relation to one's earlier output, not an intention at output time. — Brandom (1994); MacFarlane; Rescorla (2009a); Peirce; Alston

To assert a sentence S (at a context U) is (inter alia) to commit oneself to providing adequate grounds for the truth of S ... in response to any appropriate challenge ... One can escape this commitment only by withdrawing the assertion.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Some deny that any norm governs the making of assertions. Only later reactions are norm-governed: the speaker must defend or retract when challenged. — Rescorla (2009a)

there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Austin's uptake requirement makes illocution depend on the hearer's understanding. The entry reports it as less plausible for speaker-content verbs such as 'assert'. — Austin (1962); the entry's own critique

performance of an illocutionary act depends on the "securing of uptake"

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Intention-based accounts make the speaker's output-time intentions constitutive of assertion. The thesis says these cannot be what separates humans from LLMs, but this tradition holds that they fix force, though the entry also records objections to it. — Grice (1957); Searle (1969); Bach & Harnish (1979)

S asserts that p iff S expresses i. the belief that p, and ii. the intention that H believe that p.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Commitment is not sufficient for asserting, and assertions are not fully reducible to their social effects. This pressures any account that grounds assertion in downstream commitment. — Pagin (2004); Marsili & Green (2021)

incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Descriptive account on which assertion is a prima facie informative utterance, defined by default speaker and hearer patterns tied to surface features. It is a possible way of treating LLM output as assertoric without any commitment. — Pagin (2011, 2020)

an assertion is an utterance that is prima facie informative

ADJACENT (2/5) — Assertion involves cognitive and social safeguards: hearers meta-represent the speaker, and sanctions apply against misleading assertions. — Jary (2010)

it must also be subject to the cognitive and social safeguards that distinguish assertion.

Nearest positions. Commitment as accountability and as expected conduct, including defending or retracting when challenged — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; No norm for making assertions, only norms on later reactions — Rescorla (2009a); Communicative-intention accounts fixing force by speaker intention at utterance — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish

The entry is silent on. The entry never discusses LLMs, artificial or non-human speakers, or whether a system's internal organisation could satisfy commitment or uptake conditions. It also does not treat force as settled incrementally over a conversation, and it has no account of what organisation would make a system capable of taking up and owning its earlier outputs.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — intention

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Intention need not be formed prior to or at the start of an act. Intention in action is distinguished from prospective intention, so a force-fixing intention fixed in advance is not required for intentional doing. — Searle (1983), as the entry credits the refinement; Bratman on spontaneous action

although we sometimes form an intention prior to acting, this is not essential. I can wave my arm intentionally without planning in advance.

ANTICIPATED (3/5) — Intending is not a static state at a time but a form of imperfectivity, being in progress toward completing an act. The progress can be vestigial, and an intention's content can be settled by how the doing unfolds. — Michael Thompson (2008); also Moran and Stone, Ferrero 2017, Russell 2018

intending to do A is not a mental state because it is not static; instead it is a form of imperfectivity or being in progress towards the intentional completion of an act

SUPPORTED (2/5) — What separates human agents from mere purposive behaviour is reason-responsive self-relation. The agent gives application to the question 'Why?' and takes ownership of what she is doing. This is a condition on how the agent is organised, not just on a moment's mental content. — Anscombe (1963); Hampshire (1959); Velleman, Setiya, Rödl

That is why, for Anscombe, the question 'Why?' is 'given application' by the agent. Intentional action turns on knowing the answer to that question.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Intention is individuated by its functional role in commitment, resistance to reconsideration, and cross-temporal and inter-personal coordination. This backs the thesis's 'condition on how the system is organised' and its stress on commitment to earlier outputs. — Bratman (1987, 2009b, 2018)

intentions are 'conduct-controlling pro-attitudes, ones which we are disposed to retain without reconsideration, and which play a significant role as inputs to [means-end] reasoning'

ADJACENT (2/5) — On Davidson's early view, the intention with which one acts is not a state but a redescription of the action in terms of a primary reason. The action's character is fixed by its relation to reasons rather than by an inner act at output time. — Davidson (1963)

the phrase does not refer to an event or state of the agent, but is a way of redescribing what she is doing in terms of a 'primary reason'

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — On the entry's main accounts, intentional action and acting for a reason require an intention, or knowledge of what one is doing, in the action itself. The Simple View and the practical-knowledge tradition put the distinguishing mark in the agent at the time of acting, not in subsequent uptake or ownership. — The Simple View (Bratman's label); Anscombe/Velleman/Setiya on practical knowledge

if we hope to unify intention with intentional action, we cannot accept this. Intention must figure in the correct account of acting for a reason, and thus intentionally.

ADJACENT (3/5) — The entry notes that a rival view lets an action's status as intentional hold without a corresponding intention, and Anscombe's warning against letting what I think I'm doing settle what I do. Both bear on whether later self-ascription or ownership could settle the character of an act. — Bratman 2000 (intentional action without intention); Anscombe 1963, p. 52

Anscombe warns against the 'false avenue of escape' on which 'I really "do" in the intentional sense whatever I think I am doing'

Nearest positions. Intending as being in progress / imperfective, not a mental state — Thompson (2008), Moran and Stone, Ferrero, Russell; Intention as a commitment-involving planning state defined by its functional role and diachronic norms — Bratman, with Holton, Broome, Tenenbaum, Paul; Practical knowledge and the agent's own application of 'Why?' as what distinguishes acting for reasons from mere purposive behaviour — Anscombe, Hampshire, Velleman, Setiya

The entry is silent on. Illocutionary force, speech acts, and linguistic communication; uptake by an interlocutor as something that fixes what an act is; retrospective or post hoc ownership of earlier acts as constitutive of their character; LLMs and artificial systems as candidate intenders; the human/machine contrast. The only contrast case is 'other animals'. Inter-personal coordination appears only as a function of plans (Bratman), not as a source of force or content.
