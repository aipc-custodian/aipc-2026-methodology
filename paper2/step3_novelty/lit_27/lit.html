Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, artificial-intelligence, assertion — The thesis argues that LLM outputs can count as assertives and directives because an act type's point is fixed by constitutive norms, so speech-acts is the core debate, artificial-intelligence is the neighbouring debate about whether LLMs have goals or agency, and assertion covers the aim-of-truth norm.

Barandiaran and Almendros's autonomy and normativity conditions are not necessary for performing assertives and directives. An act type's point can be fixed by externally imposed, selection-shaped organisation without the performer generating its own goals. The thesis does not show these conditions are sufficient, and the 'run together' charge applies to Gubelmann's use of the argument rather than to B&A's text.

What is left. B&A's autonomy and normativity conditions are conditions on autonomous agency, and B&A themselves allow that an LLM can engage performatively in purpose-structured tasks. Gubelmann, who requires autonomous goal-setting for intentions and hence for illocution, wrote independently of them. Attah (design-derived purposes) and recent function-based accounts of assertion already argue that externally fixed purposes can suffice for something speech-act-like. What survives is narrower. It is an act-level claim covering both assertives and directives: an output can be an assertive or a directive when it is produced by a system whose training-shaped organisation is directed at that act-type's point, without the system's goals being self-generated. This is offered as a challenge to Gubelmann's premise that illocution requires intentions grounded in autonomous goal-setting. It also holds that 'library-that-talks' is an unstable description once such a system is granted, though that point is not established by anything found here, and the thesis does not claim these conditions are sufficient.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (1/5) — An act type's point is a type-level, function-like feature. It does not have to be a goal of each performer, and the entry likens it to a biological trait's function. — Green (entry author), drawing on Searle & Vanderveken's 'illocutionary point' and Austin's perlocutions as characteristic aims (Cohen 1973)

A type of speech act can have a characteristic aim without each speech act of that type being issued with that aim: Speakers sometimes make assertions without aiming to produce belief in anyone... the view that a speech act-type has a characteristic aim is akin to the view that a biological trait has a function.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Illocutionary point is one component of force, defined in the speech-act framework as a feature of the act type. The first of the septuple's seven components is point, with direction of fit separately distinguishing word-to-world acts like assertions from world-to-word acts like commands. — Searle & Vanderveken 1985; Anscombe/Searle on direction of fit

Illocutionary point: This is the characteristic aim of each type of speech act. For instance, the characteristic aim of an assertion is to describe how things are...

ANTICIPATED (3/5) — Biosemantic / selection-based account of what fixes the norms of language-use. Natural conventions are reproduced patterns, perpetuated by precedent, with the hearer's response a learned, involuntary process outside the hearer's voluntary control. — Millikan 1998 (the entry's critic is Green)

Millikan describes the hearer's response as a hidden, inner act that is not under B's voluntary control.

ANTICIPATED (2/5) — Force conventionalism: an externally instituted convention or constitutive rule fixes what act is performed. The performer need not originate the act's point, only invoke the convention. — Austin; Searle (weaker version, allowing speech acts without constitutive rules such as a dog requesting to be let out)

Searle espouses a weaker form of force conventionalism than does Austin in leaving open the possibility that some speech acts can be performed without constitutive rules; Searle considers the case of a dog requesting to be let outside (1969, p. 39).

CONTRADICTED (4/5) — Intentionalism: performing a speech act requires intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment. A performer's intention is necessary, and indeed efficacious intention, not just externally fixed organisation. — Strawson 1964; Green 2013, 2019; entry author's own view

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Section 8 says it is hard to see how one could perform a speech act with no intention of performing any speech act at all. Section 2 makes speaker meaning a necessary condition for non-conventional speech acts. — Green (entry author); Bertolet 2017

it is difficult to see how one can perform S n while having no intention of performing a speech act at all.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Simulation versus genuine performance. Nothing guarantees assertoric force, because jokers and actors can mimic any force-marking device. Performance depends on the speaker's intention, not on form or convention. — Davidson 1979; Hare 1989 (reply); Green 1997

no convention could make it the case that I intend to put forth a certain sentence as an assertion.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Speaker meaning does not require intending cognitive effects on an addressee. Instead it can be overtly manifesting one's commitments or state of mind, which lowers the cognitive demands on performers. — Davis 1992; Vlach 1981; Green 2019

some authors have advocated a construal of speaker meaning as overtly manifesting an aspect of one's commitments or state of mind (Green 2019).

ADJACENT (2/5) — Normativity of speech acts as commitment and liability to error. Acts differ in their norms, for example assertion answers to 'How do you know?', which conjecture does not, and these norms are tied to what the speaker is committed to. — Williamson 1996; Green 2017, 2019; Brandom (bibliography)

One who asserts P lays herself open to the challenge, "How do you know that?", and she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately.

Nearest positions. Illocutionary point as a type-level characteristic aim, comparable to a biological function — Searle & Vanderveken 1985; Green (entry's gloss, 3.4); Biosemantic natural conventions fix norms by selection and precedent — Millikan 1998 (criticised by Green); Intentionalism: speech acts require overt, efficacious intention and commitment — Strawson 1964; Green 2013/2019

The entry is silent on. Barandiaran and Almendros, Gubelmann, and any 'autonomy' or 'normativity' conditions on agency in the enactivist sense. Also artificial or non-organic performers (LLMs); the only nonhuman cases are Searle's dog and Davis's photocopiers, which are addressees. The entry also gives no necessity or sufficiency analysis of agent-generated goals. It never names Searle's assertive/directive categories (it gives only Austin's five and the direction-of-fit contrast). The 'run together' charge against Gubelmann's use of an argument has no counterpart in the entry.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

SUPPORTED (1/5) — In Russell's account of intelligence, an agent's goals come from outside it. A designer-supplied performance measure U fixes what counts as success, and the agent does not generate U itself. This is the nearest thing in the entry to 'externally imposed organisation without self-generated goals'. — Russell (1997); Russell & Norvig (AIMA)

A performance measure \(U\) evaluates this sequence; notice the box labeled “Performance Measure” in the above figure.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — AI measures artifacts against externally specified tests of performance, not against whether they process information as humans do. This fits the thesis's decoupling of act performance from autonomy-style conditions. — The entry's own characterization of AI; Newell (1973) on psychometric tests; Turing

AI is first and foremost concerned with engineering computational artifacts that measure up to some test (where, yes, sometimes that test is from the human sphere), not with whether these artifacts process information in ways that match those present in the human case.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Descartes's second test denies that a mechanism's organisation, as opposed to knowledge or reason, can ground genuine, context-sensitive linguistic action. Machines act only from the disposition of their organs. A Cartesian would resist the claim that organisation alone fixes an act's point. — Descartes (1637), quoted in the entry

they did not act from knowledge, but only for the disposition of their organs.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Linguistic behaviour as the criterion for a machine counting as a language user. The Turing Test operationalizes 'linguistic indistinguishability', and Nilsson defines AI by passing it. A referee would ask whether performing assertives and directives is more than passing such a test. — Turing (1950); Nilsson (1998); Descartes's first test

Passing in this sense operationalizes linguistic indistinguishability.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The strong-AI debate over whether artifacts have genuine understanding or mentality, as against merely simulating it. The entry lists the Chinese Room and Gödelian arguments, but the supplied text cuts off before developing them. — Haugeland (1985) for the strong aim; Searle's Chinese Room, by section heading only

“The exciting new effort to make computers think … machines with minds, in the full and literal sense.”

ADJACENT (1/5) — Moral AI treats a machine's norms as externally supplied. The code can come from humans by explicit encoding or by reading, and the machine need not generate it. This parallels the thesis's externally imposed normativity. — Bringsjord et al.; Govindarajulu & Bringsjord; Wallach & Allen

The field of Moral AI, for now, is not concerned with the source or provenance of such codes.

Nearest positions. Cartesian denial that mechanical 'organs' suffice for genuine language use and action from knowledge — Descartes (1637), quoted in the entry; Behavioural or operational criteria for linguistic competence — Turing (1950), Nilsson (1998); Rational-agent view in which goals and success are fixed by an external performance measure — Russell (1997), Russell & Norvig (AIMA)

The entry is silent on. Everything specific to the thesis. The entry has no discussion of speech-act theory or of assertives and directives. It does not mention Barandiaran and Almendros, enactivist autonomy or normativity conditions, selection-shaped or teleofunctional organisation, large language models, or Gubelmann. It gives no account of what conditions are necessary or sufficient for performing an act type. This version of the entry predates LLMs, and its treatment of language is limited to the Turing Test, QA and NLP.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

ANTICIPATED (3/5) — Constitutive-rule accounts already fix an act type by rules or norms that apply to the performer, not by the performer's own goal-generation. On Searle's view the constitutive rule defines the act, and on Williamson's view being subject to the norm characterizes assertion whether or not the speaker conforms to it. — Searle (1969); Williamson (1996, 2000); Goldberg, Montminy (Individuation)

It should be emphasized here that it is being subject to the norm that characterizes assertion, not conforming to the norm.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Communicative-intention accounts make hearer-directed intentions, which presuppose the performer's own goals, central to assertion. This supports a necessity claim like B&A's and counts against the thesis. — Grice (1957); Searle (1969); Bach & Harnish (1979)

S asserts that p iff S expresses i. the belief that p, and ii. the intention that H believe that p.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — The entry's descriptive accounts tie assertion to default patterns on the production and comprehension sides, keyed to surface features, with no requirement that the speaker actually be sincere or have the typical properties. — Pagin (2011, 2020)

the typical properties on the speaker and hearer side are only default properties associated with surface features of the utterance

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Hearer-uptake and the speaker's psychology are not always required. Austin's uptake condition is questioned for speaker-content acts like asserting. Stainton argues that convention is not necessary. Pagin argues that commitment is not sufficient. — Austin; Stainton; Pagin (2004)

incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry leaves open whether intention or voluntariness is required of the asserter, which is the question B&A's autonomy condition answers. — the entry (unattributed open question)

For instance, must he intend it to be an assertion? Must it be made voluntarily?

ADJACENT (2/5) — Normative accounts hold that assertion carries commitment or accountability, which are sanctions imposed by a social practice. Some argue that there is no assertion-specific norm at all. — Peirce; Alston; Brandom; Greenberg (2020); Rescorla (2009a)

an act of assertion […] renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law

Nearest positions. Constitutive-rule/norm accounts: the act type is fixed by externally applicable rules or norms — Searle (1969), Williamson (2000); Communicative-intention accounts: assertion requires hearer-directed intentions of the speaker — Grice (1957), Bach & Harnish (1979); Commitment accounts: assertion is a socially imposed accountability — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, Green; Pagin's default-pattern account: assertion is characterized by cognitive patterns tied to surface features

The entry is silent on. B&A's autonomy and normativity conditions, Gubelmann, and any treatment of artificial systems or LLMs as performers of speech acts. It also does not discuss selection-shaped or evolutionarily and training-imposed organisation as a source of an act type's point, or the question of whether an agent must generate its own goals. The handicap principle appears only in bibliography titles (Green 2009; Graham 2020).
