**What is left.** Granting Butlin and Viebahn's point that a principal can be answerable for content she has not seen, and Williams and Bayne's point that proxy accounts need a standing, blanket stance by the principal, the surviving claim is narrower. For open-range, unreviewed LLM output, the usual proxy grounds of authorisation or foreseeable range do not explain content-level commitment. The deployer's standing commitment is therefore best modelled as a commitment to the output policy's reliability, governed by a reliability norm rather than the knowledge norm. This is a claim about what grounds the commitment and which norm governs it. It is not a claim that principals cannot be answerable for unseen content. The 'publishing a reference work' or 'certifying an instrument' classification, and the 'fourth category' label, are what remains to argue for.

**Not found.** (1) I found no source arguing that a reliability, statistical or policy-level norm, rather than the knowledge norm, governs a deployer's unseen LLM output. I did not run a search on the norms-of-assertion literature (knowledge, reasonable-belief, reliability or selfless-assertion views), so that silence is weak. (2) I did not retrieve a reference-work entry or survey on proxy assertion or AI assertion. Williams & Bayne and the 2026 Synthese paper are the closest syntheses, and I saw only snippets of the latter. (3) I did not read the full texts of Green & Michel, Arora, or Ludwig's "Proxy Assertion" (the source of the proxy notion they draw on), so I cannot say whether they treat unforeseeable outputs. I confirmed Butlin & Viebahn's view only from the abstract and the proxy-section snippets. (4) I looked for adjacent empirical work (psychology, HCI, moral psychology) on how people attribute commitment, lies or blame to deployers versus models versus policies. I found only general chatbot-trust and credit-and-blame studies that did not bear on the claim, plus a mention that Kneer (2021) found subjects will ascribe lies to machines, which I did not follow up. So I cannot report a clean empirical result, only that I did not find on-point work. (5) I did not search the philosophy of testimony or instrument literature (for example Freiman & Miller, or Goldberg) for the certifying-an-instrument or reference-work analogy beyond the Episteme paper. (6) Much of this literature is paywalled or was visible to me only through snippets, so absence of a hit says little.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Commitment accounts: asserting generates accountability or a commitment to defend content when challenged. Thesis can redescribe the deployer's commitment as policy-level rather than content-level. — *Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green, Watson*
  > a speaker accepts responsibility for a content p being true iff the speaker knowingly [takes] on the liability to ([lay] herself open to) blame
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — The entry treats assertion as dependent on uptake only weakly. Austin requires uptake, but for speaker-content verbs like 'assert' the entry says uptake is not needed. This undercuts the thesis's demand that proxy accounts explain content-level commitment 'without uptake'. — *Austin, entry's own discussion*
  > Bill failed to tell Lisa that the station was closed... but Bill still asserted that it was closed
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Commitment is modulated in strength and defeasible: it applies only to appropriate challenges and can be escaped by retraction. Commitments can be graded, and a speaker can defer responsibility to another asserter. — *MacFarlane, Green, Brandom*
  > or (when appropriate) to defer this responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony one is relying
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Goldberg: the norm must be robustly epistemic and common knowledge between speaker and hearer, and the speaker authorizes the hearer to defer back challenges. This is a reliability-flavoured testimonial rationale a referee would raise. — *Goldberg 2015*
  > it must be common knowledge between speaker and hearer that the speaker's assertion is subject to this norm
- **ANTICIPATED** (2/5) — Rescorla: no norm governs the making of assertions, only norms on later reactions (defend or retract). This partially anticipates the idea that what governs unseen output is not a content-level knowledge norm. — *Rescorla 2009a*
  > there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Knowledge-norm defenders distinguish primary and secondary propriety (following a rule vs. reasonably believing one follows it). Reliability of the process could be placed as secondary propriety. Also Smithies' view that one needs justification to believe one is in a position to know. — *DeRose; Smithies 2012*
  > Secondary propriety is dictated by whether you have reasons to think you are following the rule.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Content-level norms are said to be fixed by the individual speaker's epistemic relation to p (knowledge, justification, belief). The unseen-output case challenges the assumption that the speaker has any such relation to p. — *Williamson; Lackey; Kvanvig*
  > the norms concern the epistemic relation between the speaker and the content of their assertion

**Nearest positions.** Commitment as accountability/responsibility to defend — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Green; Knowledge norm with primary/secondary propriety defence — Williamson, DeRose; Justification/non-factive norms (Lackey, Kneer, Reuter & Brössel), plus Rescorla's view that there is no assertion-making norm, only post-hoc retract-or-defend norms

**The entry is silent on.** Any non-human or automated producer of assertions, proxy or delegated assertion (deployer asserting through a tool), policy-level or reliability-based commitment, and unreviewed output with no speaker aware of the content. The entry treats the speaker as an individual with an epistemic state about each content p. Nothing is said on reliabilism as a candidate norm either (the candidates are knowledge, truth, justification, belief, certainty).


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — ethics-ai

- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The entry treats opacity and the lack of a reasons-giving agent as a problem of a 'testimony gap'. Sparrow and Flenady's testimony-gap worry is the closest hook for a claim about commitment to LLM output without uptake. — *Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025*
  > we need to avoid a “testimony gap” (Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025)
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Rights to a 'well-calibrated machine decision' rather than a human decision or justification amount to a reliability-style norm governing machine output. — *Huq 2020*
  > we have a right to right to a “well-calibrated machine decision” (Huq 2020)
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — The entry distinguishes causes that produce a decision from the reasons a rational agent would give. It says the latter is what counts for responsibility. A referee would ask how a deployer's policy-level commitment relates to this. — *entry (Sullivan 2022; Zerilli 2022; Baron 2025; Robertson 2025)*
  > the typical distinction is between the causes that lead to a decision and the reasons that a rational agent would provide, where the latter is what counts for responsibility
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Responsibility for AI output is distributed across designers, developers and users. Responsibility gaps are contested. Risk-distribution framings compete with allocating responsibility per event. — *Taddeo and Floridi 2018; Simpson and Müller 2016; Hansson 2013; Tigard 2021; Königs 2022; Da Silva 2024*
  > we should not assume that for every event there is someone responsible for that event, so the real issue may well be the distribution of risk
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — LLM hallucinations are analysed as 'bullshit' (indifference to truth). This is a neighbouring account of the norm governing LLM output. — *Hicks, Humphries and Slater 2024; Frankfurt 2005*
  > the “hallucinations” that plague LLMs are a form of this “bullshit” (Hicks, Humphries, and Slater 2024)
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — The entry flags that the fit between traditional epistemology and AI-mediated knowledge is unsettled, and it discusses AI as epistemic technology. — *Alvarado 2023*
  > it remains to be seen whether notions and questions from traditional epistemology (about the conditions for true justified belief) are suitable
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Meaningful-control views address accountability for autonomous systems through human oversight. They differ from the thesis's unreviewed-output case. — *Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven 2018*
  > keep humans “in the loop” or “on the loop” or in “meaningful control”

**Nearest positions.** Testimony gap for LLM output — Robert Sparrow and Flenady 2025; Right to a well-calibrated machine decision (reliability standard) — Huq 2020; LLM hallucination as bullshit — Hicks, Humphries and Slater 2024 (after Frankfurt 2005); Distributed responsibility / risk-distribution over responsibility gaps — Taddeo and Floridi 2018; Simpson and Müller 2016

**The entry is silent on.** Assertion, commitment, proxy or delegated assertion, and the knowledge norm of assertion. It says nothing on whether deployers commit to LLM output content, on policy-level versus content-level commitment, or on uptake. Open-range unreviewed output as a distinct case is not discussed. The entry treats these issues only through responsibility, opacity and testimony-gap framings.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — testimony-episprob

- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Deflationism about group testimony: a group's testimony reduces to some individual's testimony, whether a member or a mere spokesman. This is the nearest structural analogue to a 'proxy' account, where a party speaks for another. The entry never extends it to machines or deployers. — *Lackey (2014) is the deflationist on the epistemology of group testimony. Tollefsen (2007) is the inflationist. Lackey (2018) gives an inflationary account of group assertion.*
  > Deflationists maintain to a group's testimony that p can be reduced to some individual's testimony that p (regardless of whether those individuals are members of the group, or just mere spokesmen), whereas Inflationists maintain that a group itself can be a testifier.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — The Population Commission case: a group testifies that p although no member believed p or had justification for p. This shows testimony without content-level commitment by any individual, which the thesis can build on. — *Tollefsen (2007) supplies the case. Non-Summativists such as Tollefsen and Lackey (2014) draw the lesson.*
  > At the time the report was released, no single member of the UN Population committee believed p.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Reliability-based accounts of testimonial justification, including a reliabilist account of group testimony in which the group's statement is justified by its reliability or truth-conduciveness. They give a ready vocabulary for a policy-level reliability norm. — *Testimonial Reliabilists: Graham (2000a, 2000b, 2006a), Goldberg (2010a), Sosa (2010). Lackey (2014) for groups.*
  > Lackey (2014) defends a reliabilist account according to which your belief is justified by the reliability (or truth conduciveness) of the group's statement that p
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Anti-individualist and process-reliabilist views make the reliability of the speaker's production process, not the speaker's knowledge of p, matter. The Creationist Teacher case lets a hearer know p from a speaker who lacks knowledge of p. Together these loosen a knowledge norm on the source. — *Goldberg (2010a) and Lackey (2008); Graham (2000b) and Lackey (2008) on the reliable testifier requirement.*
  > the speaker in question needs to actually be a reliable reporter.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Hearer Testimony: testimony counts when the hearer reasonably takes an act as conveying information, regardless of what the speaker intended. This separates testimonial status from the speaker's content-level intention, as the thesis needs. — *Lackey (2008: 30–32)*
  > H, S's hearer, reasonably takes a as conveying the information that p (in part) in virtue of a's communicable content.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — The knowledge norm of assertion and the testimony-assertion link. The entry cites Williamson on knowledge and assertion only as a Transmission View source and refers readers to the Assertion entry. It lists the testimony-assertion link among questions it does not cover. — *Williamson (1996, 2000), cited under TV-S and TV-N. Pagin's Assertion entry. Goldberg (2010b) denies that assertion is necessary for testimony.*
  > as well as important questions about the relationship between testimony and assertion (e.g., Pagin 2007 [2016])
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — The Assurance (Interpersonal) View: testimonial justification depends on the speaker telling, with intentions directed at the hearer. This is the view on which 'uptake' matters and a standing policy would not suffice for telling. — *A. Ross (1986), Hinchman (2005, 2014), Moran (2005, 2018), Faulkner (2007, 2011), Zagzebski (2012), McMyler (2011)*
  > S tells A that p iff A recognizes that S, in asserting that p, intends: (i) that A gain access to an epistemic reason to believe that p
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Coady's account of testimony requires the statement to be offered as evidence, by a speaker with relevant competence, directed at those in need of evidence. It is criticised for requiring too much. A referee will use it to ask whether open-range output meets T1–T3. — *Coady (1992: 42); critics Fricker (1995) and Lackey (2008)*
  > S's stating that p is evidence that p and is offered as evidence that p.

**Nearest positions.** Deflationism vs inflationism about group testimony, where the deflationist reduces it to individual or spokesman testimony — Lackey (2014, 2018) and Tollefsen (2007); Testimonial Reliabilism, where justification consists in the reliability of production and consumption processes — Graham, Goldberg (2010a), Sosa (2010); The Assurance View, where telling requires interpersonal, intention-based assurance — Hinchman, Moran, Faulkner, McMyler

**The entry is silent on.** Anything about LLMs, AI or automated sources, deployers or operators, proxy speakers, unreviewed or unseen output, or commitment as opposed to justification. The entry also does not discuss the content of assertion norms (knowledge versus reliability), leaving that to the Assertion entry. It never asks what a standing policy commits a speaker to, or whether commitment can exist without uptake. Its nearest analogues, spokesmen and group reports, are all human or institutional speakers.
