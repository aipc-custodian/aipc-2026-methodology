Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: speech-acts, artificial-intelligence, assertion — The thesis is about whether an LLM can perform assertives and directives, which turns on speech-act theory's separation of an act type's internal point from the performer's own aims; the entries on AI and assertion cover the agency debate it addresses and the norm of assertion.

Barandiaran and Almendros's autonomy and normativity conditions are not necessary for performing assertives and directives. An act type's point can be fixed by externally imposed, selection-shaped organisation without the performer generating its own goals. The thesis does not show these conditions are sufficient, and the 'run together' charge applies to Gubelmann's use of the argument rather than to B&A's text.

What is left. Granting that B&A's individuality and normativity conditions are conditions on autonomous agency, the thesis holds that this autonomy requirement does not carry over to performing assertives and directives, because an act type's point can be fixed by externally imposed, selection-shaped organisation. Against Gubelmann's premise that illocuting requires intentions grounded in autonomy, it frames the question as one of act-level, not agent-level, purpose. I found no source that argues this against both B&A and Gubelmann. The closest is a 2026 'assertion without asserter' paper, seen only in abstract, which reaches a related conclusion by a different route. Only the portion of the thesis aimed at B&A's own text is weak, since B&A's own abstract already allows performative engagement.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (2/5) — The entry treats an act type's 'characteristic aim' as a function-like feature of the type. A type can have the aim even when individual performances lack it, so the point is fixed at type level and not by each performer's own goals. — Entry's own gloss, building on Searle and Vanderveken's 'illocutionary point' and Austin and T. Cohen on perlocutions

A type of speech act can have a characteristic aim without each speech act of that type being issued with that aim... the view that a speech act-type has a characteristic aim is akin to the view that a biological trait has a function.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Millikan's biosemantic 'natural conventions' reproduced by weight of precedent are a selection-shaped, non-voluntary account of how force-bearing linguistic patterns arise. The entry criticises it as obscure and as failing to show the hearer response is conventional. — Millikan 1998; criticised by the entry's author (Green)

Millikan describes the hearer's response as a hidden, inner act that is not under B's voluntary control.

CONTRADICTED (4/5) — The entry's favoured account makes performing an assertive or directive depend on speaker meaning and on the intentional, overt undertaking of a commitment. It says it is hard to see how one could perform a speech act with no intention to perform one. This is a rival requirement that the thesis must show can be met by a system lacking its own goals. — Entry's author (Green), following Strawson 1964 and Grice 1957

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry reports challenges to the Gricean requirement of audience-directed intentions, which loosen the conditions for speaker meaning and so for speech acts. These include Davis's cases of speaking to pre-linguistic infants, photocopy machines and photos, and Green's overt-manifestation-of-commitment view. Searle's dog requesting to be let out is also mentioned as a speech act without constitutive rules. — Davis 1992; Green 2019; Searle 1969 (dog case)

Instead of intentions to produce psychological effects in an addressee, some authors have advocated a construal of speaker meaning as overtly manifesting an aspect of one's commitments or state of mind

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry reports the claim that performing an act can be settled by external, social uptake, not only by the speaker's own states. Examples are misfire through lack of uptake and illocutionary silencing. It also reports that speech acts can be performed without the performer's intention, via illocutionary entailment from other acts. — Austin; Langton, Hornsby; Bird 2002; Maitra 2009; the entry's illocutionary-logic discussion

it is difficult to see how one can perform S n while having no intention of performing a speech act at all.

Nearest positions. Intentionalist/Gricean account: speech acts require speaker meaning and overt commitment — Strawson 1964, Grice 1957, Green (entry's author); Biosemantic force conventionalism — Millikan 1998 (selection-based, criticised in the entry); Characteristic-aim/function analogy for illocutionary point — Searle and Vanderveken 1985, T. Cohen 1973, as presented in the entry

The entry is silent on. Barandiaran and Almendros, autonomy and normativity as conditions on agency, Gubelmann, and artificial or language-model speakers. It also never discusses whether goal-generation by the performer is required for speech acts, or the sufficiency of selection-shaped organisation. It mentions artificial intelligence only as a field influenced by speech act theory.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

ADJACENT (1/5) — AI is measured against the powers of animals and persons. The entry notes that AI textbooks leave out phenomenal consciousness, creativity and reading, and it asks whether the agent progression suffices for a person or only for an animal. This is the nearest the entry comes to asking what an artificial agent must have in order to count as a genuine agent. — Entry authors (Bringsjord & Govindarajulu), citing Charniak & McDermott and Russell & Norvig

Are we given enough, in general, to build an artificial person, or is there enough only to build a mere animal?

ADJACENT (1/5) — Intelligence is defined as rationality, with agents as functions from percepts to actions and goals fixed externally by a designer-supplied performance measure. This is a framework in which an agent need not generate its own goals. — Russell (1997); Russell & Norvig

a performance measure \(U\) evaluates this sequence

ADJACENT (2/5) — The Turing Test and Descartes's language test treat the capacity to use speech appropriately as the criterion of human-like intelligence. The entry mentions Descartes's claim that machines act from the disposition of their organs rather than from knowledge. — Turing; Descartes; Nilsson

they did not act from knowledge, but only for the disposition of their organs

Nearest positions. Descartes's speech test: machines act from the disposition of their organs, not from knowledge, so their language use falls short — Descartes, as quoted in the entry; Russell's rational agent: goals set by an external performance measure, with no self-generated goals required — Russell & Norvig; Turing Test as an operational criterion of linguistic indistinguishability — Turing; Nilsson

The entry is silent on. Speech act theory (assertives, directives), Barandiaran and Almendros, enactivist or autonomy-based accounts of agency, normativity as a condition on performing speech acts, selection-shaped or teleosemantic fixing of an act's point, and Gubelmann. The entry does not discuss any of these.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Pagin's descriptive account makes assertion a matter of default speaker-side and hearer-side patterns tied to surface features. The speaker-side pattern is etiological: the utterance is made 'in part because it is true'. Nothing in it requires the speaker to generate its own goals. This is something the thesis can build on, since the point of the act type is fixed by a reason-for-production pattern. — Pagin (2011, 2020)

an assertion is an utterance that is prima facie informative … it is the cognitive patterns associated with surface features, on the production and comprehension sides, that characterize assertion.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The norms that characterise assertion are social or constitutive norms that apply to speakers from outside, in the form of rules, commitments and liabilities. They are not goals the performer generates for itself. This supports the idea that an act type's point can be externally imposed. — Searle (constitutive rules); Peirce, Alston, Brandom (commitment/accountability); Williamson (norm of assertion)

Constitutive rules, according to Searle, are like definitions: they define what it is to engage in a certain activity

PRECEDENT · PASSING (1/5) — The entry raises, without answering, the question of what intentions or voluntariness a speaker needs for an utterance to count as an assertion. This is the closest it comes to the necessity question the thesis addresses. — The entry itself, in section 4.2

Apart from the communicative intentions accounts of assertion, there are more general questions about what intentions are required of a speaker in order for his utterance to qualify as an assertion. For instance, must he intend it to be an assertion? Must it be made voluntarily?

ADJACENT (3/5) — Communicative-intention accounts make asserting depend on rich hearer-directed intentions: Grice's three clauses, Searle's reflexive intention, and Bach and Harnish's R-intention. A referee would raise these as showing that agential capacities of some sort are necessary for assertion. The thesis would have to separate this from the autonomy condition. — Grice (1957); Searle (1969); Bach & Harnish (1979)

For S to express an attitude is for S to R-intend the hearer to take S's utterance as reason to think S has that attitude.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Several views make belief, self-representation or sincerity central to asserting, e.g. that the asserter represents herself as believing or knowing p. A referee would ask whether a performer without its own goals or attitudes can meet these. The entry also notes that the norm literature takes the speaker to be subject to the norm. — Black (1952); Davidson (1984); Unger; Slote; Williams (2002); Green (2007)

in asserting that p the speaker represents herself as believing that p.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Directives and the other speech-act classes are mentioned only in the taxonomy (Austin, Searle). The entry itself is about assertion. Commitment accounts say assertion generates responsibilities for the speaker, which a referee might press as requiring an accountable agent. — Peirce; Alston (2000); Brandom (1994)

an act of assertion […] renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law

Nearest positions. Pagin's cognitive account: assertion as prima facie informative, with the utterance produced partly because it is true; Searle's constitutive-rules account and the social commitment accounts (Peirce, Alston, Brandom, Green), where norms are social and externally imposed; Communicative-intention accounts (Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish), on which asserting requires speaker intentions

The entry is silent on. Barandiaran and Almendros, Gubelmann, and the autonomy and normativity conditions on agency. Also LLMs and artificial performers, selection-shaped or externally imposed organisation as fixing an act type's point, directives in any depth, and the 'run together' charge. The entry does not discuss whether non-agents can perform speech acts.
