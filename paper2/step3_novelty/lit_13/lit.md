**What is left.** Gubelmann's organism/mechanism criterion, applied to a maintained human–LLM pipeline, does not by itself exclude the pipeline's firm or newsroom, because his own stopping point is artifactuality and not biology. If such a whole counts as self-maintaining and self-goal-setting, an LLM's outputs are speech acts of the whole and not of the LLM as a part. This differs from proxy accounts, which already allow corporate principals but treat the LLM as a stand-in, because here the LLM is an organ of the whole and no one's intention is relayed. Group-agent theory, Luhmannian autopoiesis and collective-authorship work have already made the general moves that a whole can be the author and that attribution is a separate step. What remains for the thesis is the specific Kantian argument that Gubelmann's three autonomies can be met at the level of an assemblage, together with the claim that this reduces 'LLMs cannot speak' to 'parts as such do not speak'. It also has to show why a firm is not just another extrinsic end, in Kant's sense of a thing designed by a rational agent for an external purpose.

**Not found.** I found no source that applies Kant's natural-purpose criterion to firms or human–LLM pipelines to argue that a whole speaks through its LLM organs. I found no published reply to Gubelmann that argues the organism criterion should stop at the assemblage, and no text that uses the proxy view as a foil in exactly this way. I did not find Kant's own remarks on whether non-organic wholes such as states can be natural purposes; I did not search for that, and a referee will likely raise it. I also did not retrieve Gubelmann's explicit disclaimer of biological chauvinism or the exact wording of his cognitive-autonomy concession for LLMs, so those two premises are unchecked against his text. Most of the philosophy sources were seen as abstracts or excerpts. Psychology and cognitive-science work on how people attribute speakerhood or authorship to human–AI wholes (as opposed to collective-performance studies) was not searched. Paywalled work, German-language Luhmann-and-AI literature, and the legal and organisational-theory literature on corporate speech were largely invisible to me, so the absence of hits is weak evidence.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — collective-intentionality

- **ANTICIPATED** (4/5) — A whole made of members can be the subject of intentional states once it has an internal structure with roles and decision mechanisms. Its members then function as parts or positions within it. — *Tuomela (we-groups as organized institutional entities); French; Hindriks's status account; Pettit and List (group agents)*
  > a we-mode group […] is a collective artefact and indeed an organized institutional entity […]. Group members are viewed as functioning in group positions
- **ANTICIPATED** (3/5) — Subjecthood is conferred or fixed by a separate ascription or attribution criterion. This is interpretationism, plus conditions on rational unity that justify the ascription. — *Tollefsen (intentional stance applied to groups; rational unity); Rovane; Hindriks (status account)*
  > making groups' status as intentional subjects dependent on whether their behavior can be interpreted as intentional
- **ANTICIPATED** (2/5) — Intentions are layered. Individual intentions can be derivative from a higher-level joint intention, for example moving a pawn derives from playing chess. — *Sellars (we-derivative vs primary I-referential intentions)*
  > My intention to move a pawn on the chessboard is derivative from our intention to play a game of chess.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Group attitudes may be discontinuous with member attitudes, and the group can be an agent at its own level. This allows authorship to sit at the group level while members stay non-authors of the group's output. — *Pettit; List and Pettit*
  > such groups fulfill them as collective units of agency whose attitudes may be discontinuous with the attitudes of their members
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — The subject need not be a rational unity of agents. Two or more individuals can jointly form the subject, which is a lighter, non-organizational whole. — *Smith 2011; Rovane; Tollefsen; Pettit*
  > two or more individuals jointly form the subject of their collective intentional attitudes
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — The Individual Ownership Thesis, with its naturalist and internalist versions, holds that intentionality belongs to individual minds or brains and cannot be fused into a super-subject. This resists 'the whole may be a speaker'. — *Most participants in the debate; Searle's internalism*
  > Collective intentionality is had by the participating individuals, and all the intentionality an individual has is their own
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Attributions of intentionality to wholes are metaphorical shorthand. Even Tuomela says groups are agents only in a metaphorical sense. — *Quinton 1975; Tuomela 2007; Collingwood ('society is nothing over and above its members')*
  > groups can but need not be taken as (singular) entities, and they are agents and persons only in a metaphorical sense
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — The naturalist version of the Individual Ownership Thesis ties intentionality to brains, and the entry rejects a 'Borg-like' integration of brains. Whether the skin is a principled boundary is an adjacent issue, though the entry does not call it biological chauvinism. — *Naturalist version of the Individual Ownership Thesis*
  > any (primary) intentional state is a higher order feature of a brain. It is not the case that in collective intentionality, individual brains are integrated in a Borg-like manner
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Collective responsibility and agency require agency, intentionality or autonomy on the part of the bearer. Accounts of the whole's responsibility should not be discontinuous with accounts of its agency, which is relevant to who is the author or responsible party. — *Hindriks; Pettit 2007b; List and Pettit*
  > there is no responsibility without agency, intentionality, freedom, or autonomy on the part of the entity to whom responsibility is ascribed

**Nearest positions.** Group-agent subject accounts requiring an organizational structure with roles and decision procedures — Pettit and List; Tuomela (we-groups as collective artefacts, members functioning in positions); French; Interpretationist or status-based attribution of intentionality to structured groups — Tollefsen; Rovane; Hindriks; Deflationary or individualist view that group agency is metaphorical and the Individual Ownership Thesis holds — Quinton; Tuomela's reductionist social ontology; Collingwood; Searle's internalism

**The entry is silent on.** Gubelmann or any Kantian criterion of agency, LLMs or AI systems of any kind, human–machine pipelines, and speech or authorship as the target (as opposed to intention, belief or commitment). It does not discuss non-biological parts as organs of a goal-setting whole, nor 'biological chauvinism' as a charge against the skin boundary. It also has no discussion of whether layered authorship needs a third author-kind, so that conclusion gets no support or opposition from it.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — teleology-biology

- **CONTRADICTED** (4/5) — On Ginsborg's reading of Kant, self-regeneration and reproduction give organisms a natural purposiveness that artifacts lack. This is a principled boundary for the Kantian criterion, and it is not the skin or biological chauvinism. A maintained human–LLM pipeline would be an artifact-type whole and would not meet it. — *Hannah Ginsborg (2004), reading Kant and paralleling Aristotle*
  > Kant thought that the regenerative and reproductive aspects of organisms lead us to attribute a kind of natural purposiveness that is absent in artifacts
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Organizational accounts draw the whole's boundary by self-maintenance. A trait's function is its contribution to the organization that maintains the trait itself. This gives a non-skin, non-chauvinist criterion for what counts as a whole with parts. — *Schlosser 1998; McLaughlin 2001; Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009; Moreno & Mossio 2015*
  > a trait token T has a function F when the performance of F by T contributes to the maintenance of the complex organization of the system, which in turn results in T's continued existence
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Cummins-style functional analysis defines a part's function by its contribution to a larger system's capacity. It is explicitly not specific to biology and applies equally to artifacts. This is the move that makes the LLM an organ of a larger whole. — *Cummins 1975; Lewens 2004; extended to mechanisms by Craver 2007 and Maley & Piccinini 2017*
  > there is nothing specifically biological about the pattern of explanation offered by functional analysis; it applies equally well, for example to the contributions made by the components of artifacts
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — The field-theoretic externalist account locates goal-directedness in an upper-level field that directs, without determining, the embedded parts. It claims full generality across living and non-living systems. It is criticized as too permissive to make sense of biological teleology. — *McShea 2012, 2016; Babcock & McShea 2021, 2024; critique by García-Valdecasas 2025*
  > the goal-directed behavior of teleological systems arises because they are embedded in "upper-level fields" that direct but do not determine their activities
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — For Kant, we inevitably understand organisms as teleological, but the teleology is a product of our cognitive faculties. This supports the thesis's idea that attribution is fixed by a separate rule, and it treats a Kantian purposiveness criterion as applying to whole systems taken as means and ends. — *Kant 1790; Zammito 2006; Lenoir 1982 on the teleo-mechanist strategy*
  > the teleology we see in the natural world is only apparent; it is the product of our limited cognitive faculties
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Derived and lifetime functions for traits that arise within a single organism's life, such as new concepts or antibodies, are grounded in differential persistence or retention. This gives a layered, non-evolutionary account of function that a maintained-pipeline view might draw on. — *Millikan 1984; Bouchard 2013; Garson 2017*
  > using "differential persistence" and "differential retention" within an organism's lifespan to play the role that differential reproduction plays

**Nearest positions.** Kantian organism as both means and end, with natural purposiveness distinguishing organisms from artifacts — Kant, via Ginsborg (2004), Zammito, Lenoir; Organizational or self-maintenance accounts of function and of what counts as a whole — Moreno & Mossio, Schlosser, McLaughlin; Cummins-style causal-role functional analysis, which is domain-general, plus the McShea field account and its permissiveness critique — Cummins, Lewens, McShea, García-Valdecasas

**The entry is silent on.** Everything about LLMs, speakers, authorship and attribution rules. The entry never mentions Gubelmann or any Kantian criterion applied to artificial language systems. It never addresses 'biological chauvinism' as an objection to a stopping point at the skin, or the idea of layered authorship across levels of a whole. It treats teleology and function only for biological and, in passing, artifact systems, not for linguistic agency.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — The entry defines speech acts through speaker meaning, so whether something counts as a speaker depends on intentional, overt commitment-undertaking. It does not depend on any species-based test. The thesis can build on this to ask which entity, part or whole, satisfies the condition. — *Green (the entry's author), drawing on Grice and Strawson*
  > a speech act is a type of act that can be performed by speaker meaning that one is doing so
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment. This is a condition on an agent, not on a sentence or on any single component that produces words. — *Green 2013/2019, against Searle's 'recognition suffices'*
  > speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Uttering words, even a performative formula, is not sufficient for a speech act. The entry's somniloquist case treats an utterance-producing system without speaker meaning as a non-speaker. This parallels 'parts as such do not speak'. — *Green (the entry)*
  > while talking in my sleep I might say, "I hereby promise to climb the Eiffel Tower," without thereby making any promise
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Non-human or non-standard speakers are not excluded in principle. Searle allows a dog requesting to be let out, and Davis's cases include speaking to photocopiers and infants. This sits against biological chauvinism, though neither point concerns speakers. — *Searle 1969; Davis 1992*
  > Searle considers the case of a dog requesting to be let outside (1969, p. 39)
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Millikan's biosemantic conventionalism grounds linguistic conventions in reproduced patterns and functions. A referee could use it to ask whether the stopping point lies in function or history. The entry also compares a speech act's characteristic aim to a biological trait's function. — *Millikan 1998 (the entry criticizes it)*
  > the view that a speech act-type has a characteristic aim is akin to the view that a biological trait has a function
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Layered performance of acts: one speech act can be performed by performing another, and a further act can be performed through a set of intentionally performed acts. This is the entry's nearest analogue to 'authorship layered across levels'. — *Searle 1979, Bach & Harnish on indirect acts; Searle & Vanderveken on illocutionary commitment*
  > she performs S n only by virtue of intentionally performing some other set of speech acts
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Attribution fixed by an institutional rule. Austin and Searle's force conventionalism and the preparatory conditions of authority and uptake make who performs an act depend on social rules. This is close to a 'separate attribution rule'. — *Austin 1962; Searle 1969; Searle & Vanderveken 1985; Langton on uptake*
  > Only an appropriate authority, speaking at the appropriate time and place, can: christen a ship, pronounce a couple married
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — Davidson's point that nothing in the utterance guarantees force, since actors and jokers can simulate it. A referee could use it to say that surface speech-act output from an LLM cannot settle authorship. — *Davidson 1979*
  > every joker, storyteller, and actor will immediately take advantage of the strengthened mood to simulate assertion

**Nearest positions.** Intentionalism about force (speech acts constituted by overt communicative intention) — Strawson 1964, Grice 1957, Green's overtness/commitment account; Force conventionalism (institutional rules fix who performs which act) — Austin 1962, Searle 1969; Biosemantic/natural-convention account of speech acts — Millikan 1998 (the entry criticizes it)

**The entry is silent on.** The entry never mentions LLMs or any machine as a candidate speaker or author. It does not mention Gubelmann or a Kantian criterion for agency or speakerhood. It says nothing on composite or collective speakers, on whole/part or organ relations, on layered authorship across levels, on a separate attribution rule for authorship, or on 'a third kind of author'. AI appears only in the opening list of fields influenced by speech act theory, plus Shoham 1993 in the bibliography with no discussion. All of the thesis's central apparatus is outside the entry.
