What is left. In human cases where output-time facts underdetermine the act (blurts, confabulated explanations), the speaker's subsequent owning, defending or retracting is part of what makes the earlier remark an assertion, and not merely evidence of an earlier intention. Conversation analysis and discourse analysis already say later turns retroactively fix or display act-type, and the standard speech-act view treats retraction as a new act that leaves the original act's identity unchanged. So the surviving contribution is the constitutive, commitment-based reading for assertion in underdetermined cases. Extending that to LLMs also needs an argument that a model's defending or retracting is genuine ownership by a persisting commitment-bearer, which a recent 'Diachronic Argument' paper denies. The universal claims that every position is synchronic and that determinate cases are fixed at utterance should be dropped or narrowed.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

ADJACENT (3/5) — Assertion requires explicitness/determinacy of content and force; if the hearer cannot tell what is intended, the utterance fails to be assertoric. This is a determinacy-at-utterance view, which the thesis qualifies but does not directly deny. — Fricker (2012); criticized by Hawthorne (2012), Peet (2015, 2021), García-Carpintero (2016, 2019b)

as soon as it is not fully determinate to the hearer what the intended content of an utterance is, or what force it is made with, the utterance fails to be assertoric.

SUPPORTED (3/5) — Commitment accounts tie assertion to what the speaker does after the utterance: defending or retracting when challenged. This gives the thesis ownership/disowning machinery to build on. — Brandom (1994); MacFarlane; Rescorla (2009a); Peirce; Alston (2000)

To assert a sentence S ... is ( inter alia ) to commit oneself to providing adequate grounds for the truth of S ... One can escape this commitment only by withdrawing the assertion.

ANTICIPATED (3/5) — There is no norm for making assertions at all, only norms on later reactions: defend or retract when challenged. This makes post-utterance conduct central, though to normative status rather than act-type identity. — Rescorla (2009a)

there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions.

CONTRADICTED (2/5) — Illocutionary acts do not depend on hearer reaction, only on uptake. Assertion's speaker-content structure makes it less dependent even on uptake. This supports the view that the act is fixed at utterance by speaker-side facts. — Austin (1962); the entry's own discussion

The illocutionary act does not depend on the hearer's reaction to the utterance.

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Defeasible surface features of declarative sentences, with default speaker and hearer patterns, characterize assertion. Departures such as lying or a distrustful hearer are allowed. — Pagin (2011, 2020)

it is the cognitive patterns associated with surface features, on the production and comprehension sides, that characterize assertion.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Sneaky or underdetermined assertions and indirect assertion raise the question of whether content not exactly expressed still counts as assertion proper. — García-Carpintero (2019b, 2016); Levinson; Sadock

One question is whether an utterance is an assertion proper that p if that content is not exactly what is expressed

ADJACENT (2/5) — Intention-based accounts make the act depend on speaker intentions. Questions are raised about whether the speaker must intend it to be an assertion or make it voluntarily. This bears on blurts. — Grice; Searle; Bach & Harnish

must he intend it to be an assertion? Must it be made voluntarily?

Nearest positions. Determinacy requirement: underdetermined force or content means no assertion — Fricker (2012), opposed by Hawthorne, Peet, García-Carpintero; Commitment/accountability accounts: defend or retract on challenge — Brandom, MacFarlane, Peirce, Alston, Rescorla; Pagin's prima facie informativeness account, with defeasible default patterns tied to surface features

The entry is silent on. Chatbot or machine outputs, blurts, and confabulations are not discussed. The entry also never says that a speaker's retrospective owning or disowning determines which speech act was performed. It treats retraction and defence as commitment-discharging, not act-individuating.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Retraction/withdrawal is a post-hoc act available for speech acts. The entry treats later retraction as something one can do to a speech act already performed, with commitments dissolving. This is a resource for a thesis about later owning/disowning, though the entry says retraction does not change which act was performed. — Sbisà 2007 (as reported by Green)

on Wednesday I may be able to retract a claim I made on Monday... In both these cases of assertion and promise, I am now no longer beholden to the commitments that the speech acts engender in spite of the fact that the past is fixed.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Whether an act was performed is fixed by the speaker's intentions and overt commitment at the time of utterance. Force is an aspect of speaker meaning, so later attitudes are not constitutive of the act. — Strawson 1964; Green's overtness/commitment account

We perform a speech act, then, when we overtly commit ourselves in a certain way to a content

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Force is underdetermined by content, mood and the observable utterance itself, so the question of which act was performed can remain open. The entry also has the speaker's intention as decisive and discernible only through contextual cues. — Austin; Green

the communicative significance of an act may be underdetermined by what has been said or observably done.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Intention is required for speech acts, yet efficacy requires more than intending. Speech acts need an intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment, which bears on blurts and non-intentional outputs (no intention, no act). — Green 2013 on Searle

speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it

ADJACENT (2/5) — The somniloquist and joker/actor cases show that an utterance's form does not suffice for a speech act. Uptake can also be required for some acts, so audience response, rather than speaker avowal, can co-determine whether an act succeeded. — Green; Austin; Davidson 1979

while talking in my sleep I might say, “I hereby promise to climb the Eiffel Tower,” without thereby making any promise.

ADJACENT (3/5) — Uptake and addressee responses can determine whether an act is performed at all (misfire), so post-utterance facts sometimes matter. But these are audience facts, not speaker owning. — Austin; Langton; Hornsby and Langton

If you do not accept that bet, then I have tried to bet but have not succeeded in betting.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Assertion carries commitment to defend the content, and one is obliged to retract if unable to meet the challenge. This links assertoric status to how the speaker later responds to challenges. — Green; Williamson 1996

she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately.

Nearest positions. Force as overtly manifested commitment at utterance (speaker meaning) — Green; Strawson 1964 intentionalism; Retraction/withdrawal as post-hoc act that leaves the past act intact — Sbisà 2007; Uptake as a condition of success for some speech acts — Austin; Langton; Hornsby and Langton (contra Bird 2002)

The entry is silent on. The entry says nothing about blurts, confabulation, or chatbot/LLM outputs, nor about whether later owning or disowning by a speaker retroactively fixes which speech act was performed. It does not discuss the idea of a determinate/indeterminate split.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

ADJACENT (2/5) — The Turing Test operationalizes linguistic indistinguishability as the criterion for machine linguistic competence, which is about outputs and behavior rather than which speech act was performed. The entry never asks whether a machine's output counts as an assertion. — Turing (1950); Nilsson (1998) defines AI by it

Passing in this sense operationalizes linguistic indistinguishability.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Descartes' test says machines could utter words and emit canned responses but could never arrange speech to reply appropriately to everything said. This is a different criterion from the act-type question, but it bears on whether machine utterances are genuine speech acts. — Descartes (1637)

it never happens that it arranges its speech in various ways, in order to reply appropriately to everything that may be said in its presence
