**Credit (precedents the thesis develops).** Rescorla 2009 — assertoric norms govern reactions to challenge (defend or retract), not the making of the assertion; Moran 2005 — blurting is still an act of the speaker, and the speaker's later regret or amends marks the difference from mere mechanical production; Williams & Bayne 2024/2026 — graded proto-assertion, and the point that chatbots' handling of commitment and updating is relevant to assertion; Butlin & Viebahn 2025 — sanctionability by interlocutors as a requirement on assertion; Gubelmann 2024 — the intention-based view of speech acts that the thesis opposes; MacFarlane and the commitment-account literature (SEP; Shapiro) — retraction and defence as the core of assertoric commitment

### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Commitment accounts: asserting commits the speaker to respond to challenges or retract, with later conduct (defending or retracting) as the consequence of asserting. The entry treats this as following from the act, not as fixing which act was performed. — *Brandom, MacFarlane, Peirce, Alston, Green, Rescorla*
  > making an assertion commits you to respond to people's questions and challenges in certain ways, as the conversation evolves.
- **CONTRADICTED** (2/5) — Austin's uptake condition makes the illocutionary act depend on the hearer's understanding. The entry says this is implausible for speaker-content verbs like 'assert', which suggests the act is settled at utterance by speaker-side facts. — *Austin; entry's critique*
  > Bill still asserted that it was closed, since Bill believed she was still there.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Rescorla holds there is no norm on making assertions, only norms governing later reactions: defend or retract when challenged. This puts weight on post-utterance conduct, though for norms, not for act-identity. — *Rescorla (2009a)*
  > argues there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions.
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Indeterminacy of force or content: some hold that an utterance fails to be assertoric when intended content or force is not determinate to the hearer. Others object. This is the entry's one discussion of underdetermined cases. — *Fricker (2012); objections by Hawthorne, Peet, García-Carpintero*
  > as soon as it is not fully determinate to the hearer what the intended content of an utterance is, or what force it is made with, the utterance fails to be assertoric.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Sneaky and indirect assertions, and the dispute over whether an utterance is an assertion proper, show that act-identity is contested where the literal content differs from what is asserted. — *García-Carpintero (2016, 2019b); Levinson; Searle; Sadock*
  > One question is whether an utterance is an assertion proper that p if that content is not exactly what is expressed, or whether it is an act of a related kind
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Mere self-commitment is not sufficient for asserting: Pagin's counterexample (37) puts pressure on hearer-directed and commitment views. A thesis that makes later owning help settle act-identity must answer it. — *Pagin (2004); Pegan, MacFarlane, García-Carpintero, Marsili & Green*
  > incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.

**Nearest positions.** Commitment accounts: the assertor is accountable and must defend or retract in response to challenge — Brandom, MacFarlane, Peirce, Alston, Green; Rescorla: no norm on making assertions, only on later reactions (defend or retract); Austin's uptake and the speaker-side view of 'assert' — the act is fixed by speaker-side facts at utterance; Fricker vs Hawthorne, Peet, García-Carpintero: indeterminate force or content and whether it blocks assertion

**The entry is silent on.** The entry does not discuss blurts, confabulations, or chatbot outputs. It also does not discuss whether a speaker's retrospective owning or disowning helps determine which speech act an underdetermined utterance was. It says nothing on a split between determinate cases fixed at utterance and indeterminate cases settled afterwards.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — A speech act, once performed, can later be retracted or withdrawn. The entry stresses that retraction does not change the fact that the act was performed. This is the closest the entry comes to later speaker conduct bearing on an act, but it treats the later conduct as discharging commitments from an already-fixed act, not as settling which act it was. — *Sbisà 2007, as reported by Green (the entry's author)*
  > nothing I can do on Wednesday can change the fact that I made a promise or assertion on Monday. However, on Wednesday I may be able to retract a claim I made on Monday.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Whether some acts are performed at all can depend on later audience uptake. A bet is not made unless the addressee accepts it. This is an event after the utterance that settles whether a speech act occurred, but it concerns the addressee's response, not the speaker's later owning or disowning. — *Austin 1962, as reported by Green*
  > I cannot bet you $100 on who will win the election unless you accept that bet. If you do not accept that bet, then I have tried to bet but have not succeeded in betting.
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — On the intentionalist/overtness view, whether an act is an assertion is fixed by what the speaker overtly manifested at utterance, namely commitment to defend the content against 'How do you know?' challenges. Later conduct does not enter. Nothing in the entry makes the act's identity depend on later ratification. — *Strawson 1964; Green 2013, 2019 (the entry's author's own view)*
  > speech acts involve intentional undertaking of a publicly accessible commitment; further, that commitment is not undertaken simply by virtue of my intending to undertake it
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — The entry gives the thesis its premise that output-time facts can underdetermine force. Content and mood underdetermine force, and a true utterance may not be an assertion at all (the microphone test and the somniloquist). Where a speaker's intention is not made manifest, the entry says no indirect act is performed. It also allows that clues and inference to the best explanation can settle an act's identity. — *Green (the entry's author); Bertolet 1994 on indirect speech acts; Davidson 1979 (autonomy)*
  > Testing a microphone in a windowless room, I utter, "It's raining," and it happens to be raining outside. Here I have said something true but have made no assertion.
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — Strong illocutionary commitment shows that one can perform a speech act without intending it, by intentionally performing other acts that jointly satisfy its conditions. This is another route to act identity not fixed by the speaker's intention at the time. It is still determined by what was performed at that time. — *Searle and Vanderveken 1985, as discussed by Green*
  > it is difficult to see how one can perform S n while having no intention of performing a speech act at all.

**Nearest positions.** Intentionalism/overtness: force is fixed by what the speaker overtly manifests at utterance — Strawson 1964; Green 2013, 2019; Retraction as withdrawal of a fixed past act — Sbisà 2007 (as reported by Green); Uptake-dependence of some acts (Austin's misfires; bets need acceptance) and illocutionary silencing — Austin 1962; Langton 1993; Hornsby and Langton 1998; Bird 2002; Maitra 2009; Force conventionalism: acts are fixed by conventions or constitutive rules in force at utterance — Austin 1962; Searle 1969

**The entry is silent on.** The entry says nothing about blurts, confabulation, or chatbot or LLM outputs. It does not discuss later owning or disowning by the speaker as settling which act was performed, and it does not discuss speaker-side retrospective determination of act identity. Retraction is treated only as discharging commitments from an already-determinate act.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — The entry treats AI language ability through the Turing Test, i.e. behavioral/linguistic indistinguishability judged from outputs. It offers no account of which speech act an output performs, and it ties no such status to later owning or disowning. — *Turing (1950); Nilsson (1998); Descartes (1637) on speech as a test*
  > Passing in this sense operationalizes linguistic indistinguishability.
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — The entry says that open-domain question-answering has no agreed formal characterization, and that systems like Watson do not converse or reason on the fly. This bears on whether chatbot outputs could count as assertions at all. — *The entry's own discussion of Watson, Levesque (2013)*
  > Watson can't converse on the fly.

**Nearest positions.** Turing Test: linguistic indistinguishability as the criterion for machine thought — Turing 1950, Nilsson 1998; Descartes's speech test: machines cannot arrange speech to reply appropriately to everything said — Descartes 1637; Strong vs. weak AI and the Chinese Room: whether machine outputs involve understanding — Searle, as the entry's section 8 outlines (the text is truncated before it)

**The entry is silent on.** Speech acts, assertion, blurts, confabulation, and the retroactive role of a speaker's owning or disowning in fixing an act's type. The entry also does not discuss whether chatbot outputs are assertions. The thesis lies wholly outside its scope, and the text breaks off before the philosophy-of-AI sections.
