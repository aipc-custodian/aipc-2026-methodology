# Works to be read in full

**Thesis:** If chatbot outputs are assertions, hallucination, sycophancy and sanction are best assessed at the level of the trained policy, and resampling inconsistency is not evidence of insincerity. Whether the asserted content is the policy's calibrated 'p, with confidence c' is not established.

Fetch these and put the files in one folder. Add nothing the list does not ask for.

- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Patrick Butlin & Emanuel Viebahn, "AI Assertion", Ergo: An Open Access Journal of Philosophy 12 (2025): 968–988 (earlier listed as 2023; doi:10.3998/ergo.7960).  
  This is the named target: the paper must read their descriptive-function and sanctionability conditions, and their credibility-tracking condition, in full. It needs to check whether they assess chatbots output by output or already at system or training level, and whether they treat retraining or fine-tuning as a form of sanction.  
  https://philpapers.org/rec/BUTAAJ  (open access)
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Iwan Williams & Tim Bayne, "Chatting with Bots: AI, Speech Acts, and the Edge of Assertion", Inquiry 69(6) (2026): 3059–3082 (arXiv:2410.16645, 2024; doi:10.1080/0020174X.2024.2434874).  
  This is the other named target: the paper needs their full treatment of the objection from commitment and consistency, and their proto-assertion verdict, to see whether they ever consider a policy-level bearer or the resampling point. The searches had only secondhand reports of their consistency claims.  
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.16645  (open access)
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — David J. Chalmers, "What We Talk to When We Talk to Language Models", PhilArchive preprint, 2nd version (April 2026).  
  This is the strongest rival individuation of the speaker (conversation thread rather than model or policy), and it develops 'quasi-assertion'. The paper must answer it, and the added section on characters and simulacra bears on which level counts as 'the policy'.  
  https://philarchive.org/archive/CHAWWT-8  (open access)
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell & Laria Reynolds, "Role play with large language models", Nature 623 (2023): 493–498. doi:10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8.  
  This is the closest framework to treating the output distribution (a superposition of simulacra) as the object of description. It reaches a role-play verdict rather than assertion, and the paper must distinguish a distribution over characters from a distribution over one speaker's credence.  
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06647-8
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Benjamin A. Levinstein & Daniel A. Herrmann, "Still no lie detector for language models: probing empirical and conceptual roadblocks", Philosophical Studies 182(7) (2025): 1539–1565. doi:10.1007/s11098-023-02094-3.  
  This is the main philosophical treatment of whether LLMs have beliefs or credences and how to measure them, which the 'p, with confidence c' hypothesis and any lying or insincerity claim depend on.  
  https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/still-no-lie-detector-for-language-models-probing-empirical-and-c/  (open access)
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Potsawee Manakul, Adian Liusie & Mark J. F. Gales, "SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models", Proceedings of EMNLP 2023, pp. 9004–9017. doi:10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.557.  
  This is the primary empirical source for treating disagreement across resampled outputs as a signal about hallucination rather than insincerity, so it supports the claim that hallucination is assessed on the distribution.  
  https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.557/  (open access)
- [ ] **ESSENTIAL** — Mrinank Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2310.13548).  
  This is the canonical empirical account of sycophancy as a general, training-driven (RLHF and preference-model) property of models, and so the evidence base for assessing it at the level of the trained policy.  
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13548  (open access)
- [ ] **USEFUL** — Miao Xiong, Zhiyuan Hu, Xinyang Lu, Yifei Li, Jie Fu, Junxian He & Bryan Hooi, "Can LLMs Express their Uncertainty? An Empirical Evaluation of Confidence Elicitation in LLMs", ICLR 2024 (arXiv:2306.13063).  
  It compares verbalized confidence with sample-consistency measures and finds models overconfident, so it bears directly on whether a policy's output is a calibrated 'p, with confidence c'.  
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13063  (open access)
- [ ] **USEFUL** — Adam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh S. Vempala & Edwin Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", arXiv:2509.04664 (2025).  
  It explains hallucination as a product of training and evaluation incentives that reward guessing over admitting uncertainty, which is a policy-level diagnosis the paper can use or contrast with.  
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04664  (open access)
- [ ] **USEFUL** — Jakub Mácha, "Language Without Propositions: Why Large Language Models Hallucinate", Philosophies 11(2) (2026), article 42. doi:10.3390/philosophies11020042.  
  This is a rival diagnosis on which models lack any representation of truth. If it is right, calling hallucination 'miscalibration' presupposes credence-like content the paper must defend.  
  https://doi.org/10.3390/philosophies11020042  (open access)
- [ ] **USEFUL** — Germán Massaguer Gómez, "Assertion, Accountability, and Large Language Models", Philosophy & Technology (2026). doi:10.1007/s13347-026-01136-y.  
  It is a rival account of where sanction and accountability land (on deployers and institutions, not the machine), which the paper must distinguish from sanction as retraining of the policy.  
  https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-026-01136-y
- [ ] **USEFUL** — Fintan Mallory, "Fictionalism about Chatbots", Ergo 10 (2023), article 38, pp. 1082–1100. doi:10.3998/ergo.4668.  
  This is the fictionalist rival that Williams & Bayne reject, and a referee will expect the paper to know it as one of the standard responses to the chatbot-assertion dilemma.  
  https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/ergo/article/id/4668/print/  (open access)
