**What is left.** Granting that proto-assertion, quasi-testimony, fictionalism and proxy accounts were built for assertion, and that commissives are already flagged as awkward for memoryless conversational agents, what survives is the more specific claim that the speaker-unit for promising and similar commissives must be fixed by the act's satisfaction horizon. A persisting scaffolded system, or the principal behind it, can serve as that unit, whereas assertion needs no such unit. On this claim, a graded 'quasi-' or 'proto-' status that may be defensible for assertion is not available for promising, so the choice among the four existing positions is relative to act type and not system-wide.

**Not found.** I found no source that does all of the following together: individuate the speaker-unit separately for each act type by its satisfaction horizon; argue that scaffolding can supply a commissive-capable unit; and argue that proto-assertion is available for assertion but not for promising. Nothing I found covers the 'quasi-promise is incoherent' step. The nearest items are the commissives passage in the 2209.00731 snippet and Magnus et al.'s contrast with Mallory. I read only snippets of both. The search did leave philosophy, but only into computer science and HCI (agent-memory and persona-individuation papers). I did not search psychology, linguistics, economics or sociology on promising, commitment or reputation, for example the developmental psychology of promising, commitment in economics, or user studies of AI promises. That empirical check is therefore incomplete and a clean result there should not be read as absence. I also did not retrieve: the full texts of Freiman & Miller, Williams & Bayne (whether they discuss commissives), Skiba (how he treats promising) and Green & Michel; Butlin & Viebahn, Arora, Löhr, Mallory and Nickel directly; any SEP or handbook entry on promising or on AI speech acts; or the authorship of the 2209.00731 paper, which the snippet did not show. Much of this literature is paywalled.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — speech-acts

- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Content conditions are one of Searle and Vanderveken's seven components of force. Promising requires a future content under the speaker's control, so the entry already treats commissives and assertion as differing in content conditions. — *Searle and Vanderveken 1985*
  > I can only promise what is in the future and under my control; or, at least, I cannot promise to do anything that it is obvious to myself and my promissee that I cannot do.
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Commissives are speech acts through which speakers commit themselves to future courses of action. Promising's sincerity condition is an intention, and the promissory commitment persists until retracted or released. — *Austin's taxonomy; Searle; Sbisà 2007*
  > commissives (in which speakers commit themselves to causes or courses of action, e.g. promising and betting)
- **SUPPORTED** (2/5) — Retraction and release: a promise creates a commitment that lasts until the promisee releases the speaker, which presupposes a persisting committed party. — *Sbisà 2007*
  > you may allow me on Wednesday to retract the promise I made to you on Monday. In both these cases of assertion and promise, I am now no longer beholden to the commitments that the speech acts engender
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Assertion also generates lasting commitments (to defend, to retract if challenged) and illocutionary logic carries these commitments forward across speech acts. A referee could say assertion is not requirement-free with respect to persistence. — *Green; Searle and Vanderveken 1985 on illocutionary commitment*
  > she is obliged to retract P if she is unable to respond to that challenge adequately
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Preparatory conditions and felicity: a speech act can misfire or be an abuse (insincere promise) with respect to the speaker's standing or intention. A system that cannot persist might promise but be insincere, or fail to promise at all. — *Austin; Searle and Vanderveken*
  > I promise to meet you for lunch tomorrow, but haven't the least intention of making good. Here I have promised all right, but the act is not felicitous because it is not sincere.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Whether speech acts require the intention and speaker meaning of the performer. This bears on whether artificial speakers perform speech acts at all, and on speaker meaning without cognitive effect on addressees. — *Grice; Davis 1992; Green 2019; Strawson 1964*
  > a speech act is a type of act that can be performed by speaker meaning that one is doing so.
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Social uptake and standing: a pattern of broken promises or denied uptake can deprive a speaker of the ability to promise at all. — *Pratt 1986; Langton; Hornsby and Langton 1998*
  > the inveterate promise-breaker will, in time, lead others in his community to be unwilling to accept any promises he tries to make.

**Nearest positions.** Searle and Vanderveken's seven components of force, including content conditions and preparatory conditions, under which promising's future-conduct content condition is a feature of the act type; Green's normative-commitment account of force (assertion, conjecture and promise as overt commitments with differing liabilities) together with Sbisà on retraction, which makes commitment persistence central; Austin's felicity conditions (misfire versus abuse), including uptake and the sincerity/intention condition for commissives

**The entry is silent on.** Artificial or non-human speakers, personal identity or persistence of the speaker across time, and scaffolding or memory architecture as a precondition for commissives. The entry never asks what kind of unit must survive until a promise's satisfaction time. It mentions only a dog requesting to be let out (Searle) and pre-linguistic infants, photocopiers and photographs (Davis 1992), and does not compare speech acts by temporal-persistence demands.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — promises

- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Promising treated as a speech act (illocutionary, conventional) in Austin and Searle; the entry frames promising as a form of utterance under conditions, and links it to assertion only via van Roojen and Watson citations. This is the nearest speech-act framing the thesis must be situated against, though nothing about artificial speakers. — *Austin (1962), Searle (1963, 1965, 1979), Alston, Pratt, Harris & McKinney survey*
  > Austin takes promising to be an illocutionary act, that is, he takes it that promising is merely a matter of a certain form of utterance, under certain conditions.
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Promising and assertion are linked in the literature; the entry cites papers on this pairing without describing their content. A referee would raise this against the thesis's promise/assertion contrast. — *Watson (2004) 'Asserting and Promising'; van Roojen (2020) 'Promising and Assertion'*
  > Watson (2004) ... van Roojen 2020
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Promissory obligations bind a promisor over time and must be kept later. Hobbes's covenants of trust and Hodgson's point about act utilitarians show that the gap between promise and performance is what generates the problem. This supports a 'satisfaction horizon' that the promisor must last through. — *Hobbes; Hodgson (1967)*
  > when the time came to keep the promise, the promisor would simply apply the utilitarian calculus, without regard to the fact that he had previously “promised”
- **SUPPORTED** (1/5) — Self-promises and resolutions are explained as solidifying the will over time, and self-binding as protecting choices from deterioration. This ties promising to a persisting agent across time. — *Dannenberg (2015); Liberman (2019) on resolutions and vows*
  > self promises are a way of solidifying our wills, protecting our valued choices from deterioration in the face of time and trials
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Problematic promisees (the dead, absent, or unspecified) show that promissory theories test conditions on the promisee's side. The thesis tests the promisor's side, so a referee would ask whether uptake conditions already cover its concern. — *Leonard; Liberto (2018); J.S. Taylor (2021); Albrecht (2018)*
  > promisees who fail to have the standard cognitive capacity to rely on promises would certainly fail the uptake condition
- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Joint commitment and expectation views impose demanding mental-state requirements. Gilbert requires 'common knowledge' between parties and Scanlon's Principle F requires iterated knowledge and intention. An artificial speaker would have to meet these, which is a nearby route to the thesis's concern. — *Gilbert (2011); Scanlon (1998); Leonard*
  > joint commitment views are similarly cognitively demanding, requiring as they do complex and sophisticated mental states like mutual intentions

**Nearest positions.** Conventionalist speech-act view of promising (promising as an illocutionary act performed by a conventional form of utterance) — Austin, Searle; Normative power views grounding promising in the promisor's capacity to bind a future self — Hart, Raz, Owens, Shiffrin; Self-promise/resolution accounts tying promissory obligation to a will that persists over time — Rosati, Dannenberg, Liberman, with Hobbes's release problem

**The entry is silent on.** Artificial speakers, AI systems, or scaffolding of any kind; the notion of a 'unit capable of lasting through the satisfaction horizon' (a persistence or identity condition on the speaker); any comparison of commissives to assertion in terms of future-conduct content conditions; the possibility of an agent that can assert but not promise. The entry does not discuss personal identity or diachronic agency as a precondition of promising.


### From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

- **ADJACENT** (2/5) — Promising and asserting generate different kinds of commitment. Promising's primary commitment is to act so that φ happens. Asserting's primary commitment is to facts independent of one's actions, namely the defensibility of p. The entry treats them as different families, which partly backs the thesis's claim that the two acts can come apart. The entry never ties this to artificial speakers or to a persisting agent. — *Searle (1969, 1979); Watson (2004)*
  > When you promise to φ , your primary commitment is to (intend to) act so as to make φ happen. By contrast, when you assert that p , your primary commitment is to facts that are independent of your actions
- **CONTRADICTED** (3/5) — Asserting and promising are closely linked. Marsili says promising that you will φ illocutionarily entails asserting that you will φ, so a promise includes an assertion about future conduct. Hawley analyses assertion as a promise to speak truthfully. If Marsili is right, an assertion with future-conduct content has the same persistence demand as a promise, so assertion is not free of it. — *Marsili (2016); Hawley (2019)*
  > promising that you will do something entails asserting that you will do it.
- **SUPPORTED** (3/5) — Assertoric commitment is partly a matter of what the speaker is expected to do afterwards: defend the claim if challenged, avoid contradicting it, or retract it. This is a diachronic demand on the asserter. The entry treats it as a commitment that follows from the primary one (Watson), so it is secondary and conditional. That fits the thesis's contrast between a constitutive and a derivative persistence demand. — *Brandom (1994); MacFarlane; Watson (2004); Hamblin (1970b); Rescorla (2009a)*
  > The speaker's commitment to defend the assertion if challenged is on this view a secondary commitment, that follows from the primary one
- **ADJACENT** (1/5) — The thesis contrasts commissives with assertion. In Austin's taxonomy, commissives (promising, undertaking) are a separate class from expositives, where assertion is placed. This gives a classificatory basis for treating the acts differently. — *Austin (1962); Searle (1975b)*
  > a commissive by promising, undertaking or declaring that you will do something
- **ADJACENT** (3/5) — Accounts of assertion that appeal to sanctions, liability and trust (accountability, the Peircean liability account) presuppose a speaker who can be held to account over time. A referee would raise this against the claim that assertion needs no enduring unit. — *Peirce; Alston (2000); Green (fidelity, liability, frankness)*
  > renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law (or, at any rate, those of the moral law) in case [the asserted proposition] should not be true

**Nearest positions.** Commitment as accountability and as expected future conduct (defend, retract, stay consistent) — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, MacFarlane, Rescorla; Asserting and promising as distinct commitments, with promising future-action-directed and asserting fact-directed — Searle, Watson (2004); Promising entails or is built on asserting (Marsili 2016) or assertion is built on promising (Hawley 2019)

**The entry is silent on.** The entry does not discuss artificial speakers, AI systems, scaffolding, or any 'unit capable of lasting through the satisfaction horizon'. It does not treat the persistence or identity conditions of the speaker as a requirement for performing speech acts, and it does not compare acts by their temporal horizon.
