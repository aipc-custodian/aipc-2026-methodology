Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: assertion, testimony-episprob, artificial-intelligence — The theses concern what makes an utterance an assertion (adoption, answerability, intention requirements) as applied to chatbot output, with testimony-passing as the analogy, and the AI entry covers the chatbot context.

Where a user publicly puts forward chatbot output as their own, the user is the speaker. Designers who do not put forward particular outputs are not their principals. Creator-centred proxy accounts therefore fail for such cases.

What is left. That designers are not principals for particular outputs of general-purpose chatbots, and so creator-centred proxy accounts do not cover them, is already argued by Williams & Bayne and, in a form hostile to user-answerability, by Löhr. What the thesis can still claim is a positive account of one class of cases. Where a user publicly puts forward a model's output as their own, adopting content they did not author and becoming answerable for it, the user is the speaker, and the intention requirement for assertion is not a requirement on the producer of the content. The thesis must also credit, and distinguish itself from, the 'appropriative adoption' idea in 'Talking with Language Models' and the 'assertion without asserter' view. That claim is provisional until the full text of 'Talking with Language Models' is read.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Assertion is tied to a speaker taking on commitment or responsibility for content: accountability (liability to blame if not-p) and answerability to challenges. A user who publicly puts forward chatbot output would take on this commitment, which the thesis can build on to identify the user as the speaker. — Peirce; Alston (2000); Brandom (1994); MacFarlane; Green; Searle

a speaker accepts responsibility for a content p being true iff the speaker knowingly [takes] on the liability to ([lay] herself open to) blame (censure, reproach, being taken to task, being called to account), in case of not-p.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Selfless assertions: a speaker (teacher) can appropriately assert content she does not herself believe or know. This shows the entry recognizes assertion of content that is not the speaker's own first-hand epistemic output, relevant to a user relaying chatbot content. — Lackey (2007)

Intuitively, it would be appropriate for Stella, as a teacher, to assert (31).

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Goldberg: the speaker may authorize the hearer to defer challenges back to the speaker, and MacFarlane's commitment lets one defer responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony one relies. This is relevant to a user relaying chatbot output, since responsibility stays with the user or is deferred. — Goldberg (2015); MacFarlane (2003)

to commit oneself to providing adequate grounds for the truth of S ... or (when appropriate) to defer this responsibility to another asserter on whose testimony one is relying.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Frege's distinction between entertaining content and asserting it with force. A user who puts chatbot output forward supplies the force, while the chatbot output is only content. — Frege; Peirce; Austin

Frege emphasized the distinction between judging a thought content ... to be true, and merely thinking/entertaining that content.

ADJACENT (1/5) — Austin's uptake and the speaker-content structure: assertion may be performed without hearer uptake. Also the entry notes that the speaker's belief and intentions matter, which raises the question of whether a user who does not understand the output asserts it. — Austin; entry's own remark

Bill still asserted that it was closed, since Bill believed she was still there.

Nearest positions. Commitment-as-accountability accounts of assertion — Peirce, Alston, Brandom, Green, MacFarlane; Communicative-intention and self-representation accounts (speaker intends hearer to take it as evidence of belief) — Grice, Searle, Bach & Harnish, Davidson; Norm-of-assertion accounts (knowledge/justification norms, with selfless assertion) — Williamson, Lackey, Goldberg

The entry is silent on. Artificial or non-human speakers, chatbots/LLMs, delegated or proxy speech, speaker/principal relations, designer responsibility, and any 'creator-centred proxy' account of who asserts. The entry never discusses who is the speaker when content is generated by a tool or another agent without being put forward.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — testimony-episprob

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry's account of what testimony is and who counts as the testifier. It cites Coady's conditions and Lackey's disjunctive speaker/hearer testimony, in which testimony can occur 'regardless of what the speaker intended to say'. The Assurance View's Telling requires an intentional speaker who invites trust. These bear on whether a user relaying chatbot output is the speaker, but the entry never applies them to artificial sources. — Coady 1992; Lackey 2008; Hinchman 2005; Graham 1997

S s-testifies that p by performing an act of communication a if and only if, in performing a, S reasonably intends to convey the information that p (in part) in virtue of a's communicable content.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Anti-individualist and Assurance-style accounts make the speaker's own act (telling, assuring, reliable production) the locus of testimonial justification. This supports treating the person who puts forward an output as the accountable speaker. — Hinchman 2005, Moran 2005, Faulkner 2007, McMyler 2011; Lackey 2008, Goldberg 2010a

when a speaker tells their audience that p is true, she is doing much more than merely uttering p. Rather, she is inviting her audience to trust her that p is true

PRECEDENT · FRAMEWORK (2/5) — Group testimony debate: a Deflationist reduces a group's testimony to some individual's testimony, whether that individual is a member or 'just a mere spokesman'. Inflationists treat the group itself as a testifier. This is the nearest structural analogue to deciding whether the speaker is the one who puts the output forward or the originator behind it. — Lackey 2014 (deflationary); Tollefsen 2007 (inflationary); Faulkner 2018; Fricker M. 2012

Deflationists maintain to a group's testimony that p can be reduced to some individual's testimony that p (regardless of whether those individuals are members of the group, or just mere spokesmen)

ADJACENT (1/5) — Hearer-side cases of unusual or non-human sources of text: the alien notebook and the anonymous blogger. They are used against Non-Reductionism and concern gullibility toward a source with no track record, not who the speaker is. — Lackey 2008; E. Fricker 2002

you see a group of aliens from another planet drop a notebook written in what appears to be English

Nearest positions. Speaker-centred accounts of testimony as telling or assuring — Hinchman, Moran, Faulkner, McMyler; Lackey's disjunctive speaker/hearer testimony, on which testimony need not track speaker intention; Deflationism vs inflationism about group testimony, a precedent for identifying the testifier — Lackey 2014, Tollefsen 2007

The entry is silent on. Artificial or AI-generated testimony, chatbots, and users relaying machine output. The entry also says nothing about designers or creators as principals, or about 'creator-centred proxy accounts' of testimony. It treats the speaker/hearer relation only for human speakers and groups, so the thesis has no direct anticipation here.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — artificial-intelligence

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry's Moral AI section discusses who bears moral responsibility for autonomous machine decisions, and how robots might receive moral codes (from humans, directly or by reading). It does not address speakerhood or the proxy relation between users, designers and chatbot output. — Wallach & Allen 2010; Arkin 2009; Moor 2006; Bringsjord et al. 2006

The source could be humans, and the machine could receive the code directly (via explicit encoding) or indirectly (reading).

ADJACENT (1/5) — Descartes' test treats a machine's speech as not 'placing thoughts on record', so the machine is not a speaker and does not act from knowledge. This is relevant background for who counts as the speaker of machine output, but it is not an argument about users and designers. — Descartes 1637 (as quoted in the entry)

they could never use speech or other signs as we do when placing our thoughts on record for the benefit of others

ADJACENT (1/5) — The entry notes that robots may be held to different ethical standards than humans in comparable conditions, which is empirical background on responsibility attribution. It does not discuss designers as principals or proxies. — Malle et al. 2015

humans hold robots to different ethical standards than they expect from humans under similar conditions

Nearest positions. Machine ethics and moral AI: engineer machines to follow a moral code, with responsibility tied to autonomy — Wallach & Allen, Arkin, Bringsjord et al., Govindarajulu & Bringsjord; Cartesian view that machine speech is not genuine communication of thought — Descartes 1637, as quoted in the entry; Turing Test / Human-Act view on which linguistic indistinguishability operationalizes AI — Turing 1950, Nilsson 1998

The entry is silent on. Speakerhood and assertion for chatbot output; the user who publicly adopts the output as their own; designers as principals or proxies; and creator-centred proxy accounts of machine speech. The entry is also silent on any literature on proxy accounts.
