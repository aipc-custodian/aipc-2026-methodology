Stanford Encyclopedia entries read in full: assertion, identity-personal, moral-responsibility — The theses are about the sanction condition on assertion (the assertion entry, which covers norm-based accounts), turn on whether performer and sanction-bearer are the same persisting continuant (personal identity), and treat answerability to sanctions as a responsibility-style relation (moral responsibility).

The sanction condition on LLM assertion is best explained by a bearer/performer grain gap, not by an agency deficit. Policy-level sanctions are durable but fall on a non-performer, and instance-level sanctions fall on the performer but are not durable. A language agent whose identity persists through act-indexed costs and cannot be shed or forked away would plausibly satisfy the condition. This is a necessary condition only.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — assertion

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Commitment-as-accountability: asserting makes the speaker liable to sanctions/blame if the content is false. This gives the thesis its 'sanction condition' to build on. — Peirce; Alston (2000)

an act of assertion […] renders [the speaker] liable to the penalties of the social law (or, at any rate, those of the moral law) in case [the asserted proposition] should not be true, unless he has a definite and sufficient excuse.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry frames assertoric responsibility as the speaker's own liability to blame and as accountability for later conduct (defend or retract). This assumes a persisting performer who bears the cost, which the thesis's persistence-through-act-indexed-costs requirement builds on. — Alston; Brandom; MacFarlane

knowingly [takes] on the liability to ([lay] herself open to) blame (censure, reproach, being taken to task, being called to account), in case of not-p.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The accountability account is held as one component of mixed accounts. The thesis's 'necessary condition only' stance resembles the entry's report that commitment is not sufficient for asserting. This is the objection a referee would raise on sufficiency. — Pagin (2004); Pegan (2009); Marsili & Green (2021)

incurring a commitment is not sufficient for asserting.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Rescorla denies any norm governing the making of assertions, with only norms on later reactions (defend or retract). Greenberg denies a sui generis norm. Cappelen calls assertion a defective theoretical category. These are rivals to treating a sanction condition as constitutive. — Rescorla (2009a); Greenberg (2020); Cappelen (2020)

argues there is no norm at all for proper making of assertions. There are only norms that govern later reactions.

ADJACENT (2/5) — Non-normative and descriptive accounts (self-representation, Pagin's prima facie informativeness) could count LLM outputs as assertions without any sanction condition. This is a rival to the claim that the sanction condition is the live one. — Pagin (2011, 2020); D. Black; Davidson

it appears that any linguistic phenomenon that can be explained by appeal to a knowledge norm can be equally well explained by appeal to the view that asserters represent themselves as knowing what they say, although there is no norm

Nearest positions. Commitment as accountability / liability to sanction — Peirce, Alston (2000), Green (fidelity/liability); Commitment as expected conduct and answerability to challenge (defend or retract) — Brandom, MacFarlane, Rescorla; Commitment is not sufficient for asserting — Pagin (2004), against Pegan, Marsili & Green

The entry is silent on. Artificial or LLM speakers; the bearer vs performer distinction; policy-level versus instance-level sanctions; durability or forkability of the sanctioned identity; whether sanctions must attach to a persisting agent. The entry never considers a speaker on whom sanctions cannot fall or who can be duplicated.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — identity-personal

SUPPORTED (1/5) — The entry separates the persistence question (what it takes for a being to continue existing) from the characterization and personhood questions, and says persistence conditions are what fix which past or future being is you. This gives the thesis a framework for asking which grain (policy or instance) bears a sanction over time. — Olson (entry author)

What does it take for a person to persist from one time to another—to continue existing rather than cease to exist?

SUPPORTED (2/5) — Fission discussion: if a being divides into two, a non-branching view says neither successor is the original. Analogously, forking a language agent would break the identity that carries sanctions. The entry also reports that fission undercuts the practical importance of identity (responsibility, special concern). — Parfit, Shoemaker, Unger (non-branching view); Lewis, Perry (multiple occupancy)

Likewise, someone else could be responsible for my actions, and not for his own. Identity itself has no practical importance.

CONTRADICTED (3/5) — Parfit and Martin argue that numerical identity has no practical importance. Psychological connectedness or continuity suffices for self-concern and for responsibility. A thesis requiring a persisting identical bearer for sanctions would have to answer this. — Parfit (1971, 1984, 1995), Martin (1998); opposed by Sosa (1990), Merricks (2022)

Sosa (1990) and Merricks (2022) argue for the importance of identity; Parfit (1971, 1984: 215, 1995) and Martin (1998) argue against.

ADJACENT (2/5) — The what-matters-in-survival question includes who is responsible for actions and why one should care about a future being. This is the nearest the entry comes to the link between a persisting bearer and being answerable for acts. — Entry's survey of the 'What matters in survival' question

Will the resulting person—who will think he is you—be responsible for your actions or for mine?

ADJACENT (2/5) — The entry's cases for counting bearers (population question, too-many-thinkers, temporal-parts candidates) show that 'which thing is the bearer' can be indeterminate. A policy-versus-instance grain gap resembles the many-candidates problem. — Hudson, Sider, Lewis (temporal parts); Olson (too-many-thinkers)

it would be indeterminate when we came into being and what it takes for us to persist

ADJACENT (2/5) — Anticriterialism holds that there may be no informative necessary and sufficient persistence conditions. This would challenge any specification of when a language agent's identity 'persists through act-indexed costs'. — Merricks (1998); Swinburne, Lowe, Langford

Psychological and physical continuity are evidence for persistence, it says, but do not always guarantee it and may not be required.

Nearest positions. Psychological-continuity views, with the non-branching restriction and fission as death — Parfit, Shoemaker, Unger, Lewis; Identity is not what matters in survival (connectedness/continuity suffices) — Parfit, Martin; against: Sosa, Merricks; Animalism / brute-physical views, on which persistence tracks the organism and not mental continuity — Olson, van Inwagen, Snowdon

The entry is silent on. Assertion, the sanction condition on assertion, LLMs and language agents, and the distinction between a policy-level bearer and an instance-level performer. The entry also does not discuss agency deficits, durable sanctions, or whether a being's responsibility depends on its persisting through costs. It treats responsibility only as one listed question under 'what matters in survival'.

From the Stanford Encyclopedia — moral-responsibility

ADJACENT (2/5) — Holding responsible involves sanction-liability: blameworthiness is often taken to involve liability to sanction, which is why conditions on blameworthiness are pressing. This is the nearest the entry comes to a 'sanction condition', but it is not applied to assertion or to LLMs. — The entry generally; Wallace 1994, Watson [1987]2004 (fairness conditions tied to resentment's sanctioning nature)

blameworthiness, unlike praiseworthiness, is often taken to involve liability to sanction.

SUPPORTED (1/5) — Fairness of sanction as a ground for capacity conditions: it is unfair to sanction an agent lacking relevant capacities. A thesis about who the sanction falls on (bearer vs. performer) can build on this fairness-of-sanction framing. — Rosen 2015; Wallace 1994; Watson [1987]2004

it would be, Rosen claims, unfair to sanction such an agent

ADJACENT (2/5) — Forward-looking accounts: responsibility is a matter of whom sanctions can be applied to so as to shape motives. Sanctions are aimed at the 'correct point of application' of the motive, and may reach those other than the wrongdoer. This resembles the policy-level/instance-level distinction and the question of where sanctions land. — Schlick; J.J.C. Smart; Wallace's criticism; Vargas's agency cultivation; Pereboom

It is a matter only of knowing who is to be punished or rewarded, in order that punishment and reward function as such

ADJACENT (2/5) — Wallace's objection that forward-looking accounts are not directed exclusively at the wrongdoer but at anyone susceptible to influence, i.e. sanction falling on a non-performer or being detached from the performer. — Wallace 1994

is not directed exclusively toward the individual agent who has done something morally wrong, but takes account of anyone else who is susceptible to being influenced by our responses

ADJACENT (1/5) — Group/collective responsibility and individual contributions: Kutz and others on how to assess individual accountability when agency is distributed. This bears on grain-of-bearer questions (individual vs. collective), though not on sanctions for assertion. — Kutz 2000; Lawson 2013; Petersson 2013

for the purposes of assessing individual moral accountability, we should refer not to individual causal contributions but rather to the pilots' overlapping intentions and attitudes

ADJACENT (2/5) — Standing to blame and forgiveness, and conversational accounts of responsibility: the target must be a candidate for moral exchange. A persisting agent with act-indexed costs resembles the conversational requirement that the addressee can take up and answer for the act. — McKenna 2012; Wallace 1996; Watson [1987]2004

It will be reasonable to hold accountable only someone who is at least a candidate for this kind of exchange of criticism and justification

Nearest positions. Forward-looking/instrumentalist accounts, where sanction is applied to shape motives and may fall beyond the wrongdoer — Schlick, Smart, criticized by Wallace; Fairness-of-sanction capacity conditions on blame — Wallace 1994, Watson, Rosen 2015; Conversational accounts requiring a persisting addressee who can answer for the act — McKenna 2012, Watson, Wallace 1996
